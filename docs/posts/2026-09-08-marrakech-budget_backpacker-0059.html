中文

飛機在清晨五點四十分降落馬拉喀什曼納拉機場,艙門一打開,乾燥的熱風就撲進來,帶著一種塵土與遠方燒柴的氣味,和我出發前想像的濕熱北非完全不同。這是我第三次在非洲大陸落地,但前兩次都有人接機,這一次,我只有一張手繪地圖和訂房確認信上「舊城區巴布杜卡拉附近」這幾個字。

清晨降落:從機場到麥地那的第一堂課

機場外的計程車招呼站永遠是第一個關卡。官方告示牌寫著到麥地那(舊城)大約七十迪拉姆(約七美金),但司機們第一句開價幾乎都是一百五起跳。我學乖了,直接說出告示牌上的數字,對方愣了兩秒,笑著說「你來過摩洛哥」,然後就上車了——其實我只是提前做了功課。車程約二十分鐘,穿過寬闊、法式規劃的新城區,忽然一個轉彎,道路變窄,牆面從灰白轉成一種曬過太陽的赭紅色,那是麥地那的顏色,官方稱作「馬拉喀什紅」,整座舊城的建築法規都規定外牆必須維持這個色調。

司機把我放在庫圖比亞清真寺附近的路口,因為麥地那內部巷弄狹窄,車子進不去。接下來的十五分鐘,我拖著行李箱,輪子卡在石板縫裡發出喀啦喀啦的聲響,穿過還沒睡醒的市集——攤販正在拉開鐵捲門,空氣裡開始有薄荷、孜然和剛出爐麵包的味道混在一起。我靠著Google Maps和牆上偶爾出現的手繪路牌,花了比預期多一倍的時間才找到那間riad(傳統宅院旅館),一晚含早餐約十二美金,含一個小小的天井和滿牆的馬賽克磁磚。老闆Hassan一邊遞給我一杯薄荷茶一邊說:「在麥地那,GPS只能帶你到附近,最後一段路要靠問人。」這句話後來成了我這趟旅行的座右銘。

在麥地那,GPS只能帶你到附近,最後一段路要靠問人。

迷宮修行:徒步前往巴伊亞宮的路線實錄

放下行李、睡了兩小時補眠之後,已經是上午十一點。我決定用雙腳走完當天唯一的「正式景點」——巴伊亞宮(Bahia Palace),距離我住的riad直線距離不到一公里,但在麥地那,直線距離毫無意義。

我從Bab Doukkala城門出發,先沿著Rue Bab Doukkala走到Semarine市集主幹道,這條路寬得可以讓兩輛推車錯身,兩側掛滿染色皮革、金屬燈具和成堆的橄欖桶,光線從頭頂搭建的蘆葦棚縫隙灑下來,形成一格一格的光斑,踩在腳邊晃動。走了大約十分鐘,我在一個賣香料的攤位前迷路——五條巷子看起來一模一樣,攤販老闆看出我的困惑,主動用法語加英語混雜地說「Bahia,你要往左,不要跟那個要當導遊的男生走,他會收你二十迪拉姆」。這句話後來證實非常準確:果然有個年輕人湊上來說要免費帶路,我婉拒了三次他才放棄。

整段路我大概花了四十五分鐘,中間問了四次路,繞了至少兩次冤枉路,實際直線距離走出來像是三公里。抵達巴伊亞宮時是正午,售票口寫著七十迪拉姆(約七美金),排隊大概十分鐘。宮殿內部最讓我屏息的是「大理石庭院」,地上鋪滿手工雕花的石膏拱門陰影,正午陽光直射庭院中央的噴泉,水聲和外頭市集的喧囂完全隔絕開來——那一刻我才明白,這些十九世紀的宮殿設計,本身就是一種對抗酷熱與噪音的建築工程。建議上午十點前或下午四點後前往,避開正午的觀光團人潮與最烈的陽光,而且務必穿好走的鞋,園區內全是不平整的磁磚地。

深夜食堂:吉馬艾芙納廣場的塔吉亞與蝸牛湯

傍晚六點半,我沿著窄巷往吉馬艾芙納廣場(Jemaa el-Fnaa)走去,還沒到廣場,先聞到炭火燒烤的煙味,接著是耍蛇人手鼓「咚咚咚」的節奏聲從遠處傳來。廣場白天只是個普通空地,入夜後卻像變魔術一樣,兩百多個編號攤位一夜之間搭起帳篷,蒸氣、油煙、叫賣聲混成一片。

我按照旅館老闆Hassan的推薦,找到編號第一號攤位「Chez Aicha」,坐上長條板凳,點了一份塔吉亞(tanjia)——這是馬拉喀什獨有的燉肉料理,和大家熟知的塔吉鍋(tagine)不同,tanjia是把牛肉、牛骨髓、鹽膚木香料和一整顆蒜頭封進陶罐裡,送到公共澡堂(hammam)的餘燼裡悶煮六到八個小時。端上桌時陶罐還冒著蒸氣,肉一夾就整片脫落,油脂滲進湯汁裡,鹹香帶一點煙燻味,配上剛烤好的圓麵包沾湯吃,一份大約三十五迪拉姆(約三點五美金)。隔壁攤位大嬸看我吃得投入,又端來一小碗蝸牛湯(babbouche)——用茴香、丁香、甘草根熬煮數小時的湯底,蝸牛本身沒什麼味道,精華全在那碗琥珀色的湯,喝下去先是藥草的苦,接著回甘,據說是本地人治感冒的偏方,一碗只要五迪拉姆。

坐在攤位間的塑膠矮凳上,四周是本地家庭、計程車司機、收工的市集老闆,沒有人特別看我這個外國人一眼,那種融入感,比任何一張景點門票都值得。

一份三十五迪拉姆的塔吉亞,配上鄰座陌生人遞來的蝸牛湯,是我這趟旅行吃過最誠實的一餐。

蒸氣裡的信任:哈曼與齋月晚禱

第二天下午,我照著riad老闆的建議,走進巴布杜卡拉區一間本地人日常使用的公共哈曼(hammam),而非觀光客常去的高級spa版本。門口沒有招牌,只有一塊寫著「男」的木牌,入場費十迪拉姆,租一條刷子和一塊黑肥皂(savon noir)再加十迪拉姆。裡面沒有精油蠟燭,只有三間逐漸升溫的蒸氣石室,赤裸上身的本地男人們互相幫忙刷背,水桶敲擊石地的聲音在挑高的圓頂下迴盪。一位叫Youssef的中年大叔看我搓澡搓得笨拙,直接拿過我的刷子,用摩洛哥人特有的力道幫我刷背,邊刷邊笑說「觀光客都太溫柔」,刷完之後皮膚泛紅發亮,像脫了一層皮。那種毫無保留讓陌生人碰觸身體的信任感,是我在任何一間五星飯店spa裡都感受不到的。

離開哈曼時正好是傍晚,清真寺的喚拜聲(adhan)一聲接一聲從四面八方的尖塔傳來，我這才意識到當時正值齋戒月(Ramadan)。街道在日落前十分鐘忽然安靜下來,攤販停止叫賣,家家戶戶的餐桌上已經擺好椰棗和哈里拉湯(harira),等待廣播裡宣布可以開齋(iftar)的那一刻。Youssef邀我到他家附近的小巷,和幾個街坊鄰居一起,坐在門口的塑膠椅上分食椰棗與甜點shebakia,沒有人在意我是不是穆斯林,那種在齋戒月傍晚共享食物的氣氛,比任何導覽書描述的「摩洛哥好客文化」都來得具體。

帳本攤開:花費、住宿與避雷筆記

回頭整理十天的花費,住宿是最大的變數:麥地那內的riad青旅通床位一晚約八到十二美金,含早餐和露台;如果想體驗有游泳池的riad雙人房,一晚也不過三十到四十五美金,遠比想像中便宜,但務必在Booking上看清楚「是否在城牆內」,城外雖然便宜卻要天天叫計程車。餐飲方面,街邊攤一餐約三到五美金,若進到觀光客導向的屋頂餐廳,價格可以翻上三到五倍,而味道往往不成正比。

幾個真實踩過的雷,寫下來給後來的旅人:第一,廣場上的耍蛇人、猴子表演,只要你舉起手機對準拍照,無論有沒有真的拍到,對方都會走過來要錢,行情約二十到五十迪拉姆,不想付錢就別靠近三公尺內;第二,巷弄裡常有人主動說「這條路封了,我帶你走另一條」,九成是為了把你帶去他親戚開的地毯店,禮貌但堅定地說「不用」然後繼續走原路,通常那條路根本沒封;第三,計程車一定要先講好價錢,咪表基本上是裝飾品;第四,免稅刷卡在部分小店會被加收手續費,盡量準備現金,ATM在Jemaa el-Fnaa廣場周邊很密集;第五,染henna(指甲花彩繪)攤位常先用低價吸引你,畫到一半才說材質升級要加價,建議畫之前先把總價談死並拍照存證。

離開前的黃昏:庫圖比亞清真寺尖塔下的最後一杯薄荷茶

離開的前一晚,我特地繞回庫圖比亞清真寺(Koutoubia Mosque)外的花園,坐在長椅上等日落。橘紅色的光線把七十公尺高的宣禮塔染成蜜糖色,燕子成群繞著塔頂盤旋,廣場方向隱約傳來鼓聲,像是為明天要離開的我提前敲響的告別曲。我在附近一間毫不起眼的茶攤點了最後一杯薄荷茶(atay),老闆倒茶時故意把壺舉得老高,茶水拉出一條細長的弧線落進玻璃杯,泡沫堆得像啤酒一樣,價格只要五迪拉姆。喝下去先是茶葉的澀,接著滿滿的薄荷清香衝上鼻腔,甜度高到有點誇張,但配著眼前橘色的天空,竟意外地合適。

那一刻我明白,馬拉喀什這座城市從來不是靠某個單一景點說服你愛上它,而是靠這些散落在巷弄、市集、蒸氣室與茶攤裡,一次次讓你放下防備的瞬間,慢慢把你留下來。

ENGLISH

My plane touched down at Marrakech Menara Airport at 5:40am, and the moment the cabin door opened, dry heat rushed in carrying the smell of dust and distant woodsmoke — nothing like the humid North Africa I had imagined before the trip. This was my third landing on the African continent, but the first two times someone met me at arrivals. This time I had a hand-drawn map and a booking confirmation that simply said 'near Bab Doukkala, the old medina.'

Landing at Dawn: My First Lesson from Airport to Medina

The taxi rank outside the airport is always the first test. An official sign quotes roughly 70 dirhams (about $7) to the medina, but drivers open with 150 or more. I'd learned my lesson and simply quoted the sign's price back. The driver paused, laughed, said 'you've been to Morocco before,' and waved me in — really, I'd just done my homework. The twenty-minute ride cut through the wide, French-planned new town before suddenly narrowing into tight streets, the walls shifting from grey-white to a sun-baked ochre red — the color codified by law throughout the old medina, locally called 'Marrakech red.'

The driver dropped me near the Koutoubia Mosque, since the alleys inside the medina are too narrow for cars. What followed was fifteen minutes of dragging my suitcase over cobblestones, wheels clattering against uneven stone, through a market still waking up — shutters rolling open, the smell of mint, cumin, and fresh bread mixing in the air. Relying on Google Maps and the occasional hand-painted street sign, it took me twice as long as expected to find my riad, a small guesthouse with a courtyard and walls covered in mosaic tile, about $12 a night including breakfast. The owner, Hassan, handed me a glass of mint tea and said, 'In the medina, GPS only gets you close. The last stretch, you have to ask people.' That line became the motto of my whole trip.

In the medina, GPS only gets you close. The last stretch, you have to ask people.

A Walking Pilgrimage: My Real Route to Bahia Palace

After a two-hour nap, it was already 11am, and I set out on foot for the day's only 'official' sight — Bahia Palace, less than a kilometer away as the crow flies. In the medina, straight-line distance means nothing.

I left through the Bab Doukkala gate and followed Rue Bab Doukkala into the main Semarine market artery, wide enough for two handcarts to pass, lined with dyed leather goods, metal lanterns, and barrels of olives. Light filtered down through gaps in the reed-woven awnings overhead, scattering into moving patches on the ground. About ten minutes in, I got lost at a spice stall — five identical-looking alleys branched out. The vendor noticed my confusion and said, in mixed French and English, 'Bahia, go left. Don't follow that boy who wants to guide you, he'll charge you 20 dirhams.' Sure enough, a young man approached offering a 'free' guide, and it took three polite refusals before he gave up.

The whole route took about 45 minutes, including four wrong turns and four times asking for directions — what should have been a straight kilometer became closer to three. I arrived at Bahia Palace right at noon; the entrance fee was 70 dirhams (about $7), with a ten-minute queue. What stopped me in my tracks was the marble courtyard, its floor covered in the shifting shadows of hand-carved plaster archways, with midday sun striking the central fountain dead center — the water's sound completely swallowing the market noise outside. It was then I understood that these 19th-century palaces were, at their core, an architectural response to heat and noise. Go before 10am or after 4pm to avoid tour groups and the harshest sun, and wear proper walking shoes — every surface inside is uneven tile.

Late-Night Kitchen: Tanjia and Snail Soup at Jemaa el-Fnaa

By 6:30pm I was winding through narrow alleys toward Jemaa el-Fnaa square. Before I even reached it, I smelled charcoal smoke and heard the steady 'thump-thump-thump' of a snake charmer's hand drum in the distance. By day the square is just an open lot; by night it transforms — over two hundred numbered food stalls erect their tents within an hour, steam and smoke and shouted orders blending into one continuous roar.

Following Hassan's recommendation, I found stall number one, 'Chez Aicha,' and sat on a long wooden bench to order tanjia — a dish unique to Marrakech, distinct from the tagine most people know. Tanjia seals beef, bone marrow, sumac, and a whole head of garlic inside a clay urn, which is then buried in the embers of a public hammam furnace to slow-cook for six to eight hours. It arrived still steaming, the meat falling apart at the first touch of a fork, fat melting into the broth — salty, smoky, deeply savory — eaten with fresh round bread torn and dipped straight into the sauce. One portion cost about 35 dirhams (roughly $3.50). The woman at the next stall, seeing how much I was enjoying myself, brought over a small bowl of babbouche, snail soup simmered for hours with fennel, cloves, and licorice root. The snails themselves are nearly flavorless — the magic is entirely in that amber broth, bitter with herbs at first sip, sweet on the finish. Locals swear by it as a cold remedy. One bowl: 5 dirhams.

Sitting on a plastic stool among local families, taxi drivers finishing their shifts, and market vendors closing up shop, nobody gave this foreigner a second glance — and that sense of simply belonging was worth more than any entrance ticket.

A 35-dirham bowl of tanjia, followed by a stranger's snail soup passed across the bench — the most honest meal I ate on this entire trip.

Trust in the Steam: A Hammam and a Ramadan Evening

The next afternoon, on Hassan's advice, I walked into a neighborhood public hammam in Bab Doukkala — not the upscale spa version tourists usually book, but the kind locals use daily. There was no sign, just a wooden plaque reading 'Men.' Entry cost 10 dirhams, with another 10 for a scrub brush and a block of black soap (savon noir). Inside, no essential oils or candles — just three stone chambers of rising heat, bare-chested local men scrubbing each other's backs, the clang of buckets against stone echoing under the vaulted ceiling. A middle-aged man named Youssef, watching me fumble with my brush, simply took it from my hands and scrubbed my back with real Moroccan force, laughing, 'Tourists are always too gentle.' Afterward my skin was pink and raw, like a layer had been peeled away. That kind of unguarded trust, letting a total stranger handle your body, is something no five-star spa has ever given me.

I stepped out just as evening fell, and the call to prayer (adhan) rippled out from minaret to minaret across the city. Only then did I realize it was Ramadan. Ten minutes before sunset, the street fell suddenly silent — vendors stopped calling out, and every household's table already held dates and harira soup, waiting for the announcement that iftar could begin. Youssef invited me to sit with him and his neighbors on plastic chairs outside his alley, sharing dates and the honeyed pastry shebakia. Nobody cared whether I was Muslim. That shared meal at dusk during Ramadan said more about Moroccan hospitality than any guidebook paragraph ever could.

Settling the Accounts: Costs, Lodging, and Scams to Avoid

Looking back over ten days, lodging was the biggest variable: a dorm bed in a medina riad hostel runs $8–12 a night including breakfast and rooftop access; a private double room in a riad with a pool costs $30–45, far cheaper than expected — just make sure on Booking.com that it's actually within the city walls, since cheaper options outside the medina mean daily taxi fares. Street food runs $3–5 a meal, while rooftop restaurants aimed at tourists can charge three to five times more, without the food being any better.

A few real mistakes worth passing on. First, the snake charmers and monkey handlers in the square — the moment you raise a phone to take a photo, whether or not you actually captured anything, someone will come demanding 20–50 dirhams; stay three meters back if you don't want to pay. Second, people in the alleys often say 'this road is closed, let me show you another way' — nine times out of ten they're steering you toward a relative's carpet shop; a firm, polite 'no thanks' and continuing on your original path usually reveals the road was never closed at all. Third, always negotiate the taxi fare before getting in — the meter is largely decorative. Fourth, some small shops add a surcharge for card payments, so carry cash; ATMs are plentiful around Jemaa el-Fnaa. Fifth, henna artists often quote a low starting price, then claim halfway through that you've 'upgraded' to a pricier paste — agree on the total price and even photograph the agreement before they start.

One Last Evening Beneath the Koutoubia Minaret

On my final evening, I made a point of returning to the garden outside the Koutoubia Mosque to watch the sunset. The orange light turned the seventy-meter minaret the color of honey, swallows circling its top in loose spirals, drumbeats drifting faintly from the square like an early farewell song for my departure the next day. At an unremarkable tea stall nearby, I ordered one last glass of mint tea (atay). The vendor poured from an absurd height, the stream arcing thin and long into the glass, foam piling up like beer, all for 5 dirhams. The first sip was bitter with tea leaves, then mint rushed up through my nose, sweetness almost excessive — but against that orange sky, it felt exactly right.

It was in that moment I understood that Marrakech never wins you over through any single monument. It wins you over through these scattered instances — in alleyways, in markets, in steam rooms and tea stalls — each one quietly lowering your guard, until you realize the city has already kept you.
