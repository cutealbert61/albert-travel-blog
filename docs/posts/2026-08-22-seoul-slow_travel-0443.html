中文

飛機降落仁川的那個晚上,我沒有直奔明洞或弘大,而是拖著行李箱搭上機場鐵路,轉了兩次地鐵,在景福宮站三號出口爬上樓梯時,已經快晚上十一點。巷子裡的燈籠是暖黃色的,賣魚糕串的攤子正在收攤,老闆娘用韓語跟我說了句我聽不懂的話,我只回她一個笑,她也笑了。那一刻我就決定,這趟首爾,我不列景點清單,我只想像個住在這裡的人一樣,把日子過慢一點。

清晨六點半,西村巷弄裡醒來的城市

我住在西村(서촌)一間老式韓屋民宿,主人把它取名叫「行村」,只有六個房間,地板是傳統溫突暖炕,冬夏都燒得剛剛好。清晨六點半,我是被隔壁院子的掃地聲吵醒的——那種竹掃把刷過石板路的沙沙聲,規律得像鐘擺。推開木格子窗,空氣裡飄著鄰居家煮大醬湯的味道,混著一點潮濕泥土氣,是首爾秋天特有的清冷。我沿著弼雲大路慢慢走,看見社稷壇公園裡幾個歐吉桑正在做晨操,動作緩慢卻認真,一位阿嬤牽著小狗經過,朝我點了點頭。走到二十分鐘外的大吾書店(대오서점)——首爾現存最老的書店之一,1951年開業,如今前半是書店,後半改成庭院咖啡座——我點了一杯手沖咖啡,約四千五百韓元,坐在柿子樹下的木頭長椅上。老闆娘的貓懶洋洋躺在書堆旁曬太陽,完全不理會來拍照的觀光客。那一刻,我才真正感覺自己「到了」首爾,而不是路過首爾。

慢旅行不是把行程排得更鬆,而是願意把時間讓給一頓早餐、一段沉默,和一個陌生人不經意的微笑。

通仁市場的銅板便當:一場慢下來才吃得到的午餐

從大吾書店往下走十分鐘,穿過紫霞門路15街,就是通仁市場(통인시장)。這裡最有名的不是哪一攤小吃,而是「엽전 도시락」——銅板便當。市場入口的旅遊服務台用五千韓元換十枚仿古銅板,再加五百韓元押金租一個復古鐵便當盒,拿著這些銅板走進市場的小吃攤位巷弄,每枚銅板可以換一份小菜:辣炒年糕、煎蛋捲、炸物、涼拌菜、幾片午餐肉,最後到中央的白飯攤位盛飯,把所有小菜一格一格排進鐵盒裡,像小時候玩扮家家酒。我大約十一點四十分到,算是抓得剛好——後來聽賣煎蛋捲的阿珠媽說,中午十二點半一過,人潮會排到巷口,有些搶手的小菜像煎蛋捲常常一點多就賣完。我第一次不知道飯要去哪裡盛,愣在原地,是隔壁攤的阿珠媽直接拉著我的袖子帶過去,還笑我「慢郎中」。整個便當加起來大概花了五千五百韓元,便當盒吃完歸還可以退回五百韓元押金。市場二樓有一個開放式的共用座位區,我端著鐵便當盒坐在那裡,從窗口望出去是一整片舊式鐵皮屋頂,吃完飯後陽光正好斜斜照進來,竟有種回到韓國八零年代電影場景的錯覺。

廣藏市場的煙火氣:綠豆煎餅、馬格利,和一整個下午的胃口

下午一點多,我從獨立門搭一號線轉到鐘路三街,再走幾分鐘到廣藏市場(광장시장)8號出口。這裡跟安靜的通仁市場完全是兩個世界——油煙味、蒸氣、此起彼落的叫賣聲、頭頂上一整排泛黃的招牌燈箱,擠得幾乎要肩並肩才能走動。我直奔開業超過一甲子的順熙家(순희네빈대떡),點了一份綜合綠豆煎餅(모듬빈대떡),八千韓元,加一瓶池平馬格利(지평막걸리),四千韓元。阿珠媽站在一口比臉盆還大的鐵鍋前,兩把鍋鏟同時翻動十幾片煎餅,油花濺得劈啪作響。煎餅端上桌時邊緣是焦脆的金黃色,咬下去中間卻是濕潤綿密的綠豆泥,混著豆芽菜、蔥花跟一點點五花肉丁,沾一點醬油醋汁,酸香把油膩感整個化開。馬格利倒進不鏽鋼小碗裡,微微氣泡,帶著一點甜甜的米酒發酵味,冰涼順口,跟熱騰騰的煎餅是絕配。我坐在窄小的塑膠板凳上,跟旁邊一對來買菜順便午餐的老夫婦擠著同一張桌子,他們熱情地把自己那盤炸雞胗推過來一半,說「一起吃才香」。臨走前又忍不住買了一份三千韓元的迷你紫菜飯捲(마약김밥),沾芥末醬油,一口一個,是那種讓人邊走邊吃、根本停不下來的小點心。

午後,走進阿珠媽的目慾湯:首爾人的洗澡哲學

吃得有點撐,下午三點左右我決定去體驗首爾人消解油膩的方式——目慾湯(목욕탕),也就是社區澡堂。我走回西村邊緣的杏村洞,一間招牌已經斑駁的老澡堂,入場費八千韓元。櫃檯阿珠媽收了錢,比手畫腳提醒我幾件事:進浴池前一定要先徹底沖洗全身,浴池裡不能穿泳衣,也不能大聲說話。裡頭其實很安靜,只有水聲跟蒸氣機的低鳴,幾位年長的婆婆坐在小板凳上互相搓背,牆角有位阿珠媽提供「때밀이」全身搓澡服務,加價一萬五千韓元,力道大得我一度懷疑自己少了一層皮,搓下來的角質捲成灰白色的細條,她自己看了都笑出聲。泡在溫熱的湯池裡,我跟旁邊一位常客阿姨用破碎的英韓夾雜聊起天,她說再過幾天就是秋夕(추석),她已經開始準備做松糕(송편),要跟出嫁的女兒一起包,傍晚還要去市場買祭祖用的水果跟乾明太魚。她提到年輕時澡堂裡如果看到有人身上有刺青,大家會刻意避開,因為那過去跟幫派形象連結很深,現在雖然觀念鬆動了些,但很多老字號澡堂還是會禮貌謝絕。她一邊說一邊從包包裡拿出一塊剛蒸好、還溫熱的松糕分給我,外皮是淡淡松針香氣,裡頭包著芝麻豆沙,吃起來一點都不甜膩。那個下午我沒有拍到任何一張「打卡照」,卻覺得比逛完十個景點還要更貼近首爾。

在首爾,真正的風景常常藏在市場阿珠媽多塞給你的一塊松糕裡,而不是任何一張明信片上。

望遠漢江公園的黃昏:治麥、夜色,和捨不得說再見的一天

洗完澡整個人輕盈許多,傍晚五點四十五分,我搭地鐵轉公車到望遠漢江公園。首爾人的漢江儀式很簡單:一張野餐墊、一手啤酒、外送炸雞。我學在地人打開炸雞外送App,點了一份原味炸雞加起司球套餐,約一萬八千韓元,送餐機車手竟然直接騎進公園步道,對照著我傳的定位找到野餐墊,整個過程不到二十五分鐘。便利商店買了兩罐生啤酒,加起來不到五千韓元,和炸雞就這樣鋪在墊子上。夕陽把整條漢江染成橘紅色,對岸밤섬(栗島)的輪廓漸漸模糊,騎單車的年輕人成群從步道呼嘯而過,遠處還有人放風箏,晚風帶著河水濕潤的氣味,混著炸雞的油香。天黑之後我沿著望遠市場走回住處,路邊一攤糖餅(호떡)還冒著熱氣,一千五百韓元一個,咬開瞬間黑糖漿燙嘴卻又忍不住一口接一口。回到西村的韓屋民宿已是晚上九點多,巷子裡幾乎沒有遊客,只有幾戶人家窗口透出暖黃燈光。坐在庭院寫今天的筆記時,我突然明白,這趟首爾之旅我沒去景福宮拍韓服照,也沒去明洞掃貨,但我卻覺得,自己好像真的在首爾生活過一天。

離開首爾那天早上,我又繞回通仁市場買了一份銅板便當當早餐,阿珠媽一眼就認出我,笑著多給了我一塊煎蛋捲。原來慢旅行留下的,不是相機裡的照片,而是這種被記得的瞬間。

ENGLISH

The night I landed in Incheon, I didn't head straight for Myeongdong or Hongdae. I dragged my suitcase onto the airport railway, switched trains twice, and climbed the stairs out of Exit 3 at Gyeongbokgung Station close to eleven at night. The lanterns lining the alley glowed a warm amber, and a fish-cake vendor was closing up her cart. She said something to me in Korean I couldn't understand; I just smiled, and she smiled back. Right then I decided this trip wouldn't have a checklist. I just wanted to live in Seoul for a few days the way a local would—slowly.

6:30 AM: Seochon Wakes Up One Alley at a Time

I was staying in a small hanok guesthouse called Haengchon in Seochon, six rooms total, with traditional ondol heated floors that stayed just warm enough all night. I woke at 6:30 to the rhythmic swish of a bamboo broom sweeping the stone courtyard next door—steady as a metronome. When I pushed open the wooden lattice window, the air carried the smell of a neighbor's doenjang soup mixed with damp autumn earth. I wandered down Pilun-daero and found a few ajusshis doing slow, deliberate stretches in Sajikdan Park, while an elderly woman walked her dog past me and gave a small nod. Twenty minutes later I reached Daeo Bookstore, one of the oldest bookshops still standing in Seoul, opened in 1951, its front half still lined with dusty paperbacks and the back turned into a small courtyard café. I ordered a pour-over coffee for about 4,500 won and sat on a wooden bench under a persimmon tree. The owner's cat sprawled in a patch of sun on top of a book pile, completely unbothered by the tourists snapping photos. That was the moment I finally felt like I had arrived in Seoul, rather than just passing through it.

Slow travel isn't about loosening your itinerary—it's about being willing to give your time to a single breakfast, a silence, a stranger's unplanned smile.

Tongin Market's Coin Lunchbox: A Meal You Can Only Eat If You Slow Down

Ten minutes downhill from Daeo Bookstore, past Jahamun-ro 15-gil, sits Tongin Market. Its signature experience isn't any single stall but the yeopjeon dosirak—the brass coin lunchbox. At the market's tourist center, 5,000 won gets you ten replica brass coins, plus a 500-won refundable deposit for a retro tin lunchbox. You then wander the market's side-dish alley, spending one coin per item: spicy stir-fried rice cakes, rolled omelette, fried croquettes, seasoned vegetables, a few slices of luncheon meat, finishing at the central rice stall to fill the last compartment with rice. I arrived at 11:40, which turned out to be good timing—an ajumma frying rolled omelettes later warned me that after 12:30 the alley gets so packed that popular items like her omelette often sell out by early afternoon. The first time through, I stood frozen not knowing where to get rice; an ajumma at a neighboring stall simply grabbed my sleeve and pulled me over, teasing me for being too slow. The whole lunch came to about 5,500 won, with the 500-won deposit refunded when I returned the empty tin. Upstairs there's an open communal seating area overlooking a sea of old tin rooftops; I sat there eating my little tin box of food as the afternoon light slanted in, feeling like I'd stepped into a scene from an eighties Korean film.

Gwangjang Market's Fire and Smoke: Mung Bean Pancakes, Makgeolli, and an Appetite That Never Quits

Just after one, I took Line 1 from Dongnimmun, transferred at Jongno 3-ga, and walked a few minutes to Exit 8 of Gwangjang Market. It's a completely different world from the quiet of Tongin Market—thick with frying oil, steam, overlapping shouts of vendors, and rows of yellowed signboards hanging so low you have to duck. I made a beeline for Sunhee's Bindaetteok, a stall running for more than sixty years, and ordered a mixed mung bean pancake for 8,000 won along with a bottle of Jipyeong makgeolli for 4,000 won. The ajumma stood in front of a wok wider than my torso, flipping a dozen pancakes at once with two spatulas, oil crackling and popping with every turn. When mine arrived, the edges were crisp and deeply golden while the center stayed soft and creamy, studded with bean sprouts, scallions, and small cubes of pork belly. Dipped in the vinegar-soy sauce, the acidity cut right through the richness. The makgeolli, poured into a small stainless bowl, was faintly fizzy with a mild sweet fermented tang, and impossibly refreshing next to the hot pancake. I squeezed onto a plastic stool beside an elderly couple who had just finished their grocery shopping; they slid half their plate of fried chicken gizzards toward me, insisting food always tastes better shared. On the way out I couldn't resist a 3,000-won portion of mini seaweed rice rolls dipped in mustard-soy sauce—the kind of snack you eat one bite at a time without ever meaning to stop.

Afternoon in a Neighborhood Bathhouse: Seoul's Own Philosophy of Bathing

Feeling pleasantly overstuffed, around three o'clock I decided to try what locals actually do to cut through all that grease—a neighborhood bathhouse, mokyoktang. I walked back to the edge of Seochon into Haengchon-dong, where a faded old sign marked a bathhouse charging 8,000 won at the door. The ajumma at the counter gestured through the rules: rinse thoroughly before entering the tub, no swimsuits allowed, and keep your voice down inside. It was quiet in there, just water sounds and the low hum of a steam machine, with a few elderly women scrubbing each other's backs on low stools. In one corner, an ajumma offered a full-body scrub service, ttaemiri, for an extra 15,000 won—the pressure was strong enough that I briefly wondered if I'd lost a layer of skin, and the rolls of dead skin she scrubbed off made even her laugh. Soaking in the hot pool, I struck up a broken conversation in half-English, half-Korean with a regular sitting nearby. She mentioned Chuseok was just days away, and that she'd already started planning to make songpyeon rice cakes with her married daughter, and would head to the market that evening for fruit and dried pollock for the ancestral rites. She also told me that, back when she was young, people with tattoos were quietly avoided in bathhouses because of their old association with organized crime—attitudes have loosened somewhat, but plenty of traditional bathhouses still politely turn tattooed visitors away. Then, without much ceremony, she pulled a still-warm songpyeon from her bag and handed me half—its skin faintly scented with pine needles, filled with sesame and sweet red bean, barely sweet at all. I didn't take a single photo that afternoon worth posting, and yet it felt closer to the real Seoul than any ten landmarks combined.

In Seoul, the real scenery is often hiding in the half a rice cake an ajumma slips you at the market—not on any postcard.

Sunset at Mangwon Hangang Park: Fried Chicken, Nightfall, and a Day I Wasn't Ready to Let Go Of

Freshly scrubbed and lighter on my feet, I took the subway and a short bus ride to Mangwon Hangang Park by 5:45. The Seoulite ritual by the river is simple: a picnic mat, a can of beer, and delivery chicken. I copied the locals and ordered a fried chicken set with cheese balls through a delivery app for about 18,000 won; the rider actually drove his scooter straight down the riverside path, matching my pinned location, and had it to me in under twenty-five minutes. A convenience store run got me two draft beers for under 5,000 won total, and we laid it all out on the mat. The sunset turned the entire river a deep orange, the silhouette of Bamseom Island slowly dissolving into the dusk, cyclists streaming past in loose clusters, someone flying a kite in the distance. The evening breeze carried the damp mineral smell of the river mixed with the oily warmth of the chicken. After dark, I wandered back through Mangwon Market, where a hotteok stall still had its griddle smoking—1,500 won for a syrup-filled pancake so hot inside that it burned my tongue on the first bite, and yet I kept going back for more. By the time I got back to the hanok guesthouse in Seochon, it was past nine, the alley empty of tourists, just a few windows glowing warm yellow. Sitting in the courtyard writing notes that night, I realized I hadn't taken a single hanbok photo at Gyeongbokgung or shopped a single store in Myeongdong—yet somehow it felt like I had actually lived a day in Seoul.

On my last morning, I circled back to Tongin Market for one more coin lunchbox breakfast. The ajumma recognized me instantly and slipped an extra piece of rolled omelette into my box with a grin. That's when it hit me—what slow travel really leaves behind isn't the photos on your camera roll, but the small, unrepeatable moments of being remembered.
