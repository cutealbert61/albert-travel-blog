中文

清晨五點十七分,鬧鐘還沒響,窗外一乘寺的鳥叫聲已經把我吵醒了。三年前第一次來京都,我跟著旅行團擠在清水坂上動彈不得,人潮把整條坡道堵成一鍋粥,那次之後我發誓要再回來一次,用完全不同的方式旅行。這回我在洛北一乘寺租了一間老町家住上一週,行程裡沒有二年坂,沒有伏見稻荷的千本鳥居,我把時間留給那些連京都朋友都要想一下才會推薦給我的地方。這是一篇寫給想要「慢下來」的旅人的日誌——不是要你避開名所,而是想告訴你,京都真正迷人的地方,往往藏在時刻表之外。

Day 1・清晨五點半:洛北無人的圓光寺

町家的木拉門在身後發出「咔啦」一聲輕響,我踩著還帶露水的石板路往北走。一乘寺這一帶清晨幾乎沒有店家開門,只有自動販賣機的燈亮著,冷冷地照在無人的巷口。我沿著詩仙堂旁的小徑往上走,六點左右抵達圓光寺門前,山門緊閉,但門縫裡飄出來的,是濕潤泥土混著杉木的氣味,還有遠處竹林被風吹動的沙沙聲。一位穿著藏青色作務衣的老先生正拿著竹掃把清掃參道,他抬頭看我一眼,用京都腔跟我說「早いですね」(你來得真早呢),然後繼續低頭掃他的落葉,動作慢得像是在跟時間商量。我在門口的石階上坐了將近三個小時,看著天光一點一點從灰藍轉成鵝黃,鳥叫聲從稀疏變得密集,直到九點山門終於打開,我買了五百日圓的票,成為當天第一位踏進庭園的訪客。

圓光寺最有名的「十牛之庭」是一片鋪滿青苔的枯山水庭園,楓樹枝葉在頭頂交織成傘,陽光穿過葉隙灑在苔蘚上,像撒了一層碎金。庭園深處藏著一座水琴窟,我學著解說牌上的方法,用竹筒舀水澆進石缽,俯身湊近才聽見地底傳來清亮如古箏的滴水聲,一聲、兩聲,在寂靜裡格外分明。整個庭園裡只有我和一位在角落寫生的老太太,她的畫本上是還沒上色的鉛筆線稿,我們相視一笑,誰也沒有說話,那種安靜比任何導覽詞都更能說明京都的美。

京都最奢侈的不是金閣寺的金箔,而是能獨自擁有一整座庭園十分鐘的安靜。

Day 1・上午:一乘寺書店街與一碗晨間拉麵

從圓光寺下山,大約十點,我走進「惠文社一乘寺店」。這間獨立書店在文青圈頗有名氣,但多數觀光客根本不知道要繞進這條住宅區小路才找得到。木質書架高及天花板,擺書的邏輯不按出版社或作者分類,而是按策展人的心情——攝影集旁邊放著陶藝雜貨,詩集旁邊擺著手工香氛。我在角落待了快一個鐘頭,翻完一本1970年代京都老照片集,捨不得放下,最後花了兩千兩百日圓買回台灣。

一乘寺同時也是京都在地人才知道的「拉麵激戰區」,整條白川通兩側短短幾百公尺內擠了七、八間名店,天下一品本店就是其中之一。十一點半開店前,店門口已經排了七、八個穿西裝的上班族,不是觀光客,是趕著吃完午餐回辦公室的京都人。我點了招牌的「こってりラーメン」,九百五十日圓,濃稠得像芝麻醬一樣的雞骨湯頭端上桌時還冒著油亮的光,喝第一口湯有種介於濃湯與火鍋高湯之間的厚重感,鹹香裡帶一點回甘,老闆娘看我吃得滿頭大汗,笑著遞來一杯冰麥茶,說「京都人夏天就是靠這個活下去的」。

Day 2・整日:鞍馬到貴船,沒有地圖能教會你的山徑

第二天我起得更早,七點十五分從一乘寺車站搭上叡山電車,單程四百二十日圓,車廂裡只有零星幾個扛著登山杖的當地老人。列車行經二之瀨到市原之間會鑽進一段「紅葉隧道」,車掌特地放慢車速並關掉車廂燈,讓楓紅完整灑進窗內,即使我去的是初夏,滿眼翠綠也同樣震撼。約七點五十分抵達鞍馬站,一出站就撞見那尊巨大的紅色天狗面具,鼻子比我整個人還高,是這一帶最無害卻最有存在感的地標。

從仁王門買票入山,「愛山費」三百日圓,我選擇放棄普明線纜車,走傳統的九十九折參道徒步上山,四十分鐘的碎石階梯走得我腿軟,途中經過由岐神社,十月的「鞍馬火祭」就是從這裡出發,神社的木造建築被杉樹遮得幾乎不見天日,空氣裡full是松脂與潮濕土壤混合的氣味。過了本殿金堂繼續往奧之院走,進入真正考驗腳力的「木之根道」——樹根盤根錯節地裸露在步道表面,像大地的血管,必須手腳並用才能不滑倒,這段路手機完全沒有訊號,森林安靜到只聽得見自己的喘氣聲跟偶爾竹葉落下的聲音。走到魔王殿時大約中午十二點半,再往下坡走約四十分鐘,雙腿已經開始打顫,直到聽見潺潺水聲,才知道貴船到了。

整趟健行大約花了兩個半小時,建議穿真正的登山鞋而非帆布鞋,夏天記得帶足飲水,山裡沒有便利商店。貴船神社的本宮前擺著「水占みくじ」,籤紙要浮在神社湧泉上才會顯現文字,我花兩百日圓求了一張,泡進冰涼的泉水裡等了將近一分鐘,字才慢慢浮現,寫著「待人:近くにいる」(所盼之人,就在近處),當下竟有點鼻酸。夏季的貴船川沿岸會架起「川床」納涼床,一整套會席料理動輒八千日圓起跳,我囊中羞澀,只在河畔的茶寮點了一份抹茶與御手洗糰子,共八百日圓,坐在木造平台上,腳邊就是嘩嘩流過的溪水,涼意順著木板往上爬,比任何冷氣都更貼近皮膚。

Day 3・午後:今宮神社前的千年烤麻糬

第三天下午兩點,我搭公車來到北區紫野的今宮神社,這裡以「玉之輿神社」聞名,是求良緣與翻身運的信仰中心,但真正讓我特地跑一趟的,是東門參道前那兩間對望了四百年的烤麻糬老店——一文字屋和輔,當地人簡稱「一和」,與對街的「かざりや」。一和相傳創業於平安時代長保二年,也就是西元一千年,店家自己都笑說「連我們也搞不清楚到底開了幾代」。

我在茅草屋簷下的長凳坐下,點了一份六百日圓的あぶり餅,十三支手指粗的小麻糬串,裹著黃豆粉,先在炭火上烤到微焦,焦香混著炭煙味撲面而來,再淋上一層白味噌甜醬。咬下去外層帶著焦脆的顆粒感,內裡卻是軟糯拉絲的口感,黃豆粉的香氣先衝上來,接著白味噌的甘甜與微鹹在舌尖化開,一點都不膩。負責烤麻糬的是位年約七十的老太太,穿著藏青色圍裙,手法俐落地把竹籤在炭火上翻面,她告訴我這門手藝是跟婆婆學的,婆婆又是跟婆婆的婆婆學的,「一代傳一代,連醬汁的比例都沒變過」。配上一碗現泡煎茶,坐在吱嘎作響的木頭長凳上,看著參道上香客與麻糬店的炭煙裊裊升起,那一刻我才真正理解,什麼叫做「活著的傳統」。

有些味道不需要米其林星星認證,一千年來只靠一支炭火與一代代不曾改變的手勢,就足夠說服所有人。

Day 3・傍晚到夜晚:巷弄地藏盆與船岡溫泉的湯氣

從一和走回去的路上,傍晚五點半,我在紫野的小巷裡撞見一場意外的熱鬧——路口的地藏菩薩石像前搭起了紅白布幔,地上鋪著榻榻米,十幾個孩子圍坐在一起玩賓果,幾位主婦忙著分裝零食袋。一位頭髮花白、繫著碎花圍裙的婆婆看我探頭探腦,招手要我坐下,用不太標準的英文夾雜日文跟我解釋,這是「地蔵盆」,每年農曆七月尾聲(陽曆多在八月二十三、二十四日前後)町內都會為守護孩子的地藏菩薩辦一場小廟會,和尚會來誦經,孩子們則整天玩遊戲、領糖果,是京都巷弄裡最不觀光、卻最有人情味的夏日儀式。婆婆塞給我一小袋煎餅跟一顆彈珠汽水糖,說「這是給有緣人的」,我捧著那袋糖果站在巷口,看著提燈一盞盞被點亮,忽然覺得比任何祭典煙火都更觸動人心。

晚上七點半,我走進附近的船岡溫泉,這是一間建於大正十二年(1923年)的老錢湯,木造玄關雕著複雜的欄間木刻,描繪的竟是日俄戰爭的場景,天花板與磁磚拼貼全是當年流傳下來的裝飾藝術。入湯費四百九十日圓,租條毛巾另加兩百日圓。浴池分成好幾種:檜木浴槽飄著木頭香、電気風呂通著微弱電流讓皮膚微微發麻、還有一池是氡溫泉,水色略帶淡黃。池邊坐著幾位京都阿伯,彼此用京都腔閒聊著今天的天氣與棒球比賽,完全沒有人多看我這個外國臉孔一眼——這種被當成日常一部分的感覺,遠比任何一次被店員親切招呼的觀光體驗更讓我安心。泡到渾身發燙走出浴場,夜裡的紫野巷弄涼風徐徐,遠處還傳來孩子們地藏盆的笑鬧聲,那一刻我知道,這趟旅程真正的紀念品,不是任何一張照片,而是這身被溫泉泡透、被人情包圍的疲憊與滿足。

回程的路上,我沒有再去想清水寺的排隊人潮,也沒有再羨慕別人手機裡的伏見稻荷千本鳥居打卡照。京都教會我的事情很簡單:真正的風景,往往需要你願意早起、願意走遠一點的山路、願意在陌生的巷口坐下來,才會慢慢對你顯現。

ENGLISH

It was 5:17 in the morning and my alarm hadn't even gone off yet when the birds of Ichijoji woke me first. Three years earlier, on my first trip to Kyoto, I'd been stuck shoulder to shoulder with a tour group on the Kiyomizu-zaka slope, the crowd so thick the whole hill had turned into a slow-moving stew of people. I swore that day I would come back and do it differently. This time I rented a small machiya townhouse in Ichijoji, in the quiet northern reaches of the city known as Rakuhoku, for a full week. There was no Ninenzaka on my itinerary, no thousand torii gates of Fushimi Inari. I gave my time instead to the places even my Kyoto friends had to pause and think before recommending. This is a journal for travelers who want to slow down—not to convince you to skip the famous sites, but to show you that Kyoto's real magic tends to live just outside the timetable.

Day One, 5:30 AM: The Empty Temple of Enkoji

The wooden door of the machiya slid shut behind me with a soft clack, and I walked north along stone paving still wet with dew. At this hour almost nothing in Ichijoji is open—only the cold glow of a vending machine lit the empty alley. I followed the narrow path past Shisen-do and reached the gate of Enkoji temple around six. The main gate was still closed, but through the gap seeped the smell of damp earth and cedar, along with the rustle of bamboo groves stirring in the wind. An elderly man in a navy work jacket was sweeping the approach with a bamboo broom. He glanced up and said, in a soft Kyoto accent, “You're up early,” then went back to his slow, unhurried sweeping, as if negotiating with time itself. I sat on the stone steps for nearly three hours, watching the sky shift from gray-blue to pale gold, the birdsong thickening from scattered notes into a full chorus, until at nine the gate finally opened. I paid five hundred yen and became the first visitor of the day.

Enkoji's famous “Garden of the Ten Oxen” is a dry landscape garden carpeted in moss, its maple branches weaving a canopy overhead so that sunlight filters through in scattered gold flecks across the green. Tucked in a corner is a suikinkutsu—a buried ceramic pot that turns dripping water into music. Following the instructions on a small sign, I scooped water from a bamboo ladle into a stone basin and leaned close to the ground, and there it was: a clear, koto-like note rising from beneath the earth, drop after drop, startlingly distinct in the silence. I shared the garden that morning with only one other person, an elderly woman sketching in a corner, her pencil lines still unfilled with color. We exchanged a smile and said nothing at all—a silence that explained Kyoto's beauty better than any guidebook ever could.

The real luxury of Kyoto isn't the gold leaf of Kinkaku-ji—it's having an entire garden to yourself for ten quiet minutes.

Day One, Late Morning: A Bookshop Alley and a Bowl of Ramen

Coming down from the temple around ten, I wandered into Keibunsha Ichijoji, an independent bookstore beloved by Kyoto's creative crowd but tucked so deep into a residential side street that most tourists never stumble upon it. Floor-to-ceiling wooden shelves hold books arranged not by publisher or author but by the curator's mood—a photography collection sits beside handmade ceramics, poetry beside artisanal incense. I lost nearly an hour in one corner, flipping through a 1970s photo book of old Kyoto streets, and ended up carrying it home for 2,200 yen, unable to put it back.

Ichijoji is also, unknown to most visitors, one of Kyoto's fiercest ramen battlegrounds—seven or eight legendary shops packed within a few hundred meters along Shirakawa Street. Tenkaippin's flagship store is among them. By 11:30, before it even opened, a line of seven or eight salarymen in suits had already formed—not tourists, but locals rushing to eat lunch before heading back to the office. I ordered the signature kotteri ramen for 950 yen. The chicken-bone broth arrived thick as sesame paste, glistening with oil, somewhere between a soup and a hot pot base—salty, rich, with a faint sweetness lingering after each sip. Watching me sweat through the bowl, the owner's wife laughed and brought over a glass of cold barley tea. “This,” she said, “is how Kyoto people survive the summer.”

Day Two, All Day: Kurama to Kibune, a Trail No Map Can Teach You

On the second day I woke earlier still, boarding the Eizan Electric Railway from Ichijoji Station at 7:15 for 420 yen one way. The car held only a scattering of local elders carrying hiking poles. Between Ninose and Ichihara stations the train slows and dims its interior lights as it passes through a stretch nicknamed the “maple tunnel”—even in early summer, with the leaves still deep green rather than red, the effect of light pouring through the canopy was breathtaking. I arrived at Kurama Station around 7:50, stepping straight into the shadow of a giant red tengu mask, its nose taller than I am, the harmless yet unmistakable guardian of the town.

At the Nio-mon gate I paid the 300-yen mountain protection fee and, skipping the cable car, chose to climb the traditional zigzagging stone stairway on foot—forty grueling minutes that left my legs trembling. Along the way I passed Yuki Shrine, starting point of the famous Kurama Fire Festival held every October, its wooden buildings so deeply shaded by cedar that daylight barely reached the ground, the air thick with the scent of resin and wet soil. Past the main hall, the trail toward Okunoin became a true test of footing—the Root Path, where tree roots break through the surface like exposed veins of the earth, forcing me to scramble with both hands and feet to avoid slipping. My phone had no signal at all here; the forest was so quiet I could hear nothing but my own breathing and the occasional rustle of falling bamboo leaves. I reached Maou-den, the temple of the demon king, around 12:30, then descended another forty minutes with legs shaking, until the sound of rushing water told me Kibune was near.

The whole hike took roughly two and a half hours. Proper hiking shoes are essential—canvas sneakers will not do—and in summer you need to carry your own water, since there are no convenience stores anywhere on the mountain. In front of Kibune Shrine's main hall, visitors can draw a mizu-uranai fortune slip that only reveals its text once floated on the shrine's sacred spring water. I paid 200 yen and dipped mine into the icy water for nearly a minute before the characters slowly surfaced: “The one you await is near.” I felt an unexpected lump in my throat. In summer, riverside dining platforms called kawadoko line the Kibune River, offering elaborate kaiseki courses that start around 8,000 yen—well beyond my budget that day—so instead I sat at a small riverside teahouse and ordered matcha with mitarashi dango for 800 yen combined, my feet dangling near the rushing stream below the wooden platform, the coolness climbing up through the boards far more effectively than any air conditioner.

Day Three, Afternoon: Thousand-Year-Old Grilled Mochi at Imamiya Shrine

On the afternoon of my third day, I took a bus to Imamiya Shrine in Murasakino, in Kyoto's Kita ward. The shrine is known as the “Palanquin of Jewels Shrine,” a place people visit to pray for good marriages and reversals of fortune—but what had actually drawn me there was the pair of rival mochi shops that have faced each other across the eastern approach for roughly four hundred years: Ichiwa, short for Ichimonjiya Wasuke, and its neighbor Kazariya across the street. Ichiwa reportedly traces its founding back to the year 1000, during the Heian period, and even the staff joke that they've lost count of exactly how many generations have run it.

I sat on a wooden bench beneath the thatched eaves and ordered a plate of aburi mochi for 600 yen—thirteen small skewered mochi, each finger-thick, coated in roasted soybean flour, grilled over charcoal until lightly charred, then finished with a sweet white miso glaze. The smoky char hit me first, followed by a texture that was crisp on the outside and impossibly soft and stretchy within, the toasted soybean aroma rising before the miso's gentle sweetness and salt unfolded on the tongue—rich, but never cloying. The woman grilling the mochi, in her seventies and wearing a navy apron, flipped the skewers over the coals with practiced ease. She told me the technique had been passed down from her mother-in-law, who'd learned it from hers before that. “Even the ratio of the miso sauce hasn't changed,” she said. Sipping a cup of freshly brewed sencha on a creaking wooden bench, watching pilgrims pass and charcoal smoke curl upward from the grill, I finally understood what it means for a tradition to still be alive rather than merely preserved.

Some flavors need no Michelin star—a single charcoal fire and a gesture unchanged for a thousand years is proof enough.

Day Three, Evening into Night: A Neighborhood Jizo Festival and the Steam of Funaoka Onsen

Walking back from Ichiwa around 5:30 in the evening, I stumbled into an unexpected gathering in a narrow Murasakino side street—red-and-white curtains strung around a small stone statue of Jizo at the corner, tatami mats laid out on the pavement, a dozen children crowded around playing bingo, while a few mothers busily packed bags of sweets. A silver-haired woman in a floral apron noticed me lingering and waved me over, explaining in a mix of broken English and Japanese that this was Jizo-bon—a summer festival held by nearly every neighborhood association in Kyoto around the 23rd and 24th of August, honoring Jizo, the guardian deity of children. A priest comes to chant sutras while the children spend the whole day playing games and collecting candy. It's one of the least touristy, most human rituals of the Kyoto summer, unfolding quietly in alleys that no guidebook ever mentions. The woman pressed a small bag of rice crackers and a marble soda candy into my hands, saying, “This is for someone we're glad to have met.” I stood at the mouth of the alley holding that little bag of sweets, watching paper lanterns being lit one by one, and felt something more moving than any fireworks display.

At 7:30 that night I made my way to Funaoka Onsen, a public bathhouse built in 1923 whose wooden entrance is carved with intricate transom reliefs—astonishingly, depicting scenes from the Russo-Japanese War—its ceiling and tilework preserved intact from that era's decorative style. Admission was 490 yen, with towel rental an additional 200. The baths came in several varieties: a hinoki cypress tub fragrant with cedar, a denki-buro electric bath that sent a faint tingling current across the skin, and a radium bath tinted a pale amber. A few elderly Kyoto men soaked at the edge, chatting in thick local dialect about the day's weather and a baseball game, paying no attention whatsoever to the foreign face among them—and that feeling of simply being folded into the everyday, rather than politely welcomed as a guest, was far more comforting than any customer-service smile I'd received all trip. I stepped out into the cool night air of Murasakino thoroughly overheated and relaxed, the distant laughter of children still drifting from the Jizo-bon gathering down the street. In that moment I understood that the real souvenir of this trip wasn't any photograph, but this exact feeling—body soaked through with hot water, spirit wrapped in other people's ordinary kindness.

On the way back, I found I wasn't thinking about the crowds at Kiyomizu-dera anymore, or envying anyone's photo in front of the thousand torii gates at Fushimi Inari. What Kyoto taught me was simple: its truest scenery only reveals itself to those willing to wake up early, walk a little farther up the mountain, and sit down, uninvited, at the mouth of an unfamiliar alley.
