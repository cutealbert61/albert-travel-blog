京都的線條與土壤:一場關於設計美學的城市漫遊Lines and Clay: A Design Pilgrimage Through Kyoto

中文

清晨六點,我拖著行李箱走出京都車站的驗票口,還沒找到出口,先被眼前的巨大挑高震住。原廣司設計的車站大樓像一座倒扣的玻璃山谷,晨光斜斜切進鋼骨格柵,在地面上投下一格一格的光影,像有人把整座城市的秩序感提前攤開在我面前。手扶梯的機械聲、行李輪子輾過磁磚的喀啦聲,在這個挑高十五層樓的空間裡被放大又消散。我站在那座著名的「大階段」下往上看,一百七十一階的台階本身就是一件裝置藝術,傍晚會有燈光秀,但清晨的它只是安靜地反射天光。我心想,如果京都要用一句話介紹自己的設計哲學,大概就是這座車站——古都與未來感毫不違和地疊在一起。

清晨六點,車站裡的光之教堂與東福寺的方格庭園

我沒有搭計程車,而是直接在車站裡轉搭JR奈良線。從京都站到東福寺站只要兩分鐘,IC卡逼一聲扣款一百五十日圓。出站往右轉,沿著一條夾在民宅與石垣之間的窄巷走十分鐘,經過賣土產的小店還沒開門,只有貓從屋簷跳下來看我一眼。走過通天橋前,買票入內參拜是五百日圓,方丈庭園九點才開放,我特意壓線抵達,為的就是避開後面湧進來的旅行團。

方丈庭園的「市松の庭」是重森三玲一九三九年的作品,用青苔與方形石塊拼出棋盤格,乍看像蒙德里安的畫被搬進了枯山水裡。我蹲在緣廊邊看了快二十分鐘,聽見小沙彌用竹掃把耙地面碎石的沙沙聲,一格苔蘚一格白砂,線條乾淨到近乎冷酷,卻又讓人捨不得移開視線。比起金閣寺那種鍍金的華麗,這裡的美學安靜得多,是一種需要蹲下來、放慢呼吸才能讀懂的設計語言。

京都的美從不喧嘩,它藏在方格與留白之間,等你先安靜下來。

陶土的重量:河井寬次郎紀念館與尾張屋的五重漆盒

離開東福寺,我搭207號公車到五条坂,下車後沿著坡道往上走,兩側全是賣清水燒的陶器店,茶碗坂的名字不是白叫的。河井寬次郎紀念館藏在一條窄巷裡,若不是門口一塊不起眼的木牌,很容易錯過。這裡曾是這位民藝運動大師的住家兼工坊,入館九百日圓,館方沒有多餘的解說牌,就讓你自己在挑高的木造空間裡走動。地爐還留著使用過的痕跡,寬次郎親手設計的椅子、餐桌帶著厚重的手感,木頭表面被幾十年的手摩挲得發亮。穿過屋子後方,是一座八個窯室相連的登窯,爬上斜坡的磚造結構像一條沉睡的龍,空氣裡還飄著陳年柴燒和灰燼混合的氣味。我站在窯口往裡看,忽然理解為什麼他堅持「用即是美」——這些鍋碗瓢盆從來不是為了展示,而是為了被使用到磨損。

中午我搭巴士到二条附近,鑽進本家尾張屋。這間蕎麥麵店有五百五十年歷史,店面本身就是一棟保存良好的京町家,格子門、坪庭、榻榻米座位一應俱全。我點了招牌的「宝来そば」,三千三百日圓,端上來是一個五層朱漆重箱,一層麵配一層不同的配料——鴨肉、海苔、蔥花、山藥泥、溫泉蛋,一層層堆疊像在拆禮物。蕎麥麵條帶著粗糙的顆粒感,咬下去有穀物的香氣,湯汁鹹鮮回甘。捧著這只漆器,我忽然覺得,連一碗麵都被裝進了五層的工藝品裡,京都人對「盛裝」這件事的講究,近乎信仰。

在京都,連一碗蕎麥麵都被裝進五層的漆器裡,彷彿在提醒你,設計從來不只是外表。

西陣的經緯:一根絲線裡的城市記憶

下午三點,我搭地鐵烏丸線到今出川,再步行到西陣。這一帶巷弄安靜得幾乎聽不見觀光客的聲音,只有偶爾傳出的機杼聲提醒你,這裡曾是支撐整個日本高級和服產業的心臟地帶。我走進織成館,一棟改建自舊織屋的町家,裡頭陳列著早期的手織機和圖案設計圖。一位年約七十歲的山口婆婆正坐在木造織機前示範綴織技法,手裡的梭子來回穿梭,發出規律的喀噠聲,像某種古老的節拍器。她告訴我,西陣織最複雜的圖案需要靠打孔卡片控制經緯線的升降,這套系統其實正是十九世紀提花織機的原理,後來啟發了早期電腦的打孔卡邏輯——聽到這裡我起了雞皮疙瘩,原來我腳下這座城市,早在工業革命時代就已經和「程式設計」牽上了線。她泡了一杯焙茶給我,叮嚀我不要用手直接碰觸未完成的織品,因為皮膚的油脂會讓絲線變色,這是她奶奶那一輩傳下來的規矩。聊到後繼無人的產業困境,她笑得有點無奈,說現在願意學這門手藝的年輕人,一年可能不到十個。

如果建築是凝固的音樂,那麼西陣的織機聲,就是京都還在呼吸的證據。

屏風祭與祇園祭之夜:老町家開門迎客的一晚

我刻意把行程安排在七月中旬,正好碰上祇園祭的宵山期間。傍晚七點左右,我從烏丸通轉進新町通,眼前的景象讓我停下腳步——一整排原本緊閉的町家,此刻全部拉開了木格子門,屋內點著紙燈籠,展示著代代相傳的屏風,金箔在燈光下泛著溫潤的光澤,這就是所謂的「屏風祭」。街道上擠滿了穿浴衣的男女,空氣裡混著烤玉米醬油的焦香和粽子形狀的護身符「粽」的青草味,遠處鉾車傳來祇園囃子的鉦鼓與橫笛聲,一陣一陣飄過來。

我在一間老布莊門口停下,和坐在店門口的老闆聊了起來。他指著屋裡那幅琳派風格的屏風說,這是他曾祖父那一代傳下來的第五代收藏,每年祇園祭才拿出來曬曬空氣、給街坊鄰居看看,但絕對禁止開閃光燈拍照,因為顏料裡的金屬粉末經年累月很怕強光。他順手遞給我一串掛在門口的粽子護身符,說這是祈求整年無病無災,典故要追溯到西元八六六年那場瘟疫,祇園祭最初其實是一場驅疫的儀式,不是單純的觀光慶典。這段對話讓我對眼前這條擠滿人潮的街道有了完全不同的理解——這不只是節慶,是一整座城市對抗災難的集體記憶,用織品與繪畫的形式活了一千多年。

晚上九點,我沿著鴨川走進先斗町的窄巷,巷子窄到兩人擦身而過都要側身,河岸邊的納涼床亮著暖黃燈光,倒映在水面上晃啊晃。我在一間小料亭的吧檯坐下,點了一份會席料理,重頭戲是這個季節限定的鱧魚——祇園祭期間京都人特別愛吃鱧魚,甚至被暱稱為「鱧祭」。廚師當著我的面片開薄如蟬翼的魚皮,燙成牡丹花的形狀,沾點梅肉醬汁入口,細刺早已被片刀處理得幾乎無感,只剩下清爽的鮮甜。一份套餐大約八千日圓,坐在吧檯看著師傅專注擺盤的側臉,窗外傳來遠處祭典的鼓聲,我忽然覺得,京都的設計美學從來不只在美術館的白牆裡,它就活在一碗麵、一片魚、一根絲線,和一整條願意為了祭典而敞開家門的老街上。

隔天清晨我又回到京都車站,準備搭新幹線離開。回頭看了一眼那片玻璃天頂,忽然明白這座城市最厲害的設計,從來不是某一棟建築或某一件工藝品,而是它讓一千兩百年的時間感,毫不違和地摺進了同一條巷子裡。

At six in the morning I dragged my suitcase out of the ticket gates at Kyoto Station, and before I even found the exit, the sheer scale of the atrium stopped me cold. Architect Hiroshi Hara's terminal building looks like an inverted glass canyon, morning light slicing through the steel lattice roof and scattering grid-shaped shadows across the concourse floor, as if the whole city's sense of order had been laid bare in front of me. The mechanical hum of escalators mixed with the clatter of suitcase wheels on tile, both amplified and swallowed by the fifteen-story void above. I stood at the base of the famous Grand Stairway — one hundred seventy-one steps that double as a public art installation, lit up with projections at night but silent and reflective in the early light. If Kyoto had to summarize its design philosophy in one building, I thought, it might be this station: ancient capital and future-facing form, layered without contradiction.

Six A.M.: A Cathedral of Light, and the Checkerboard Garden of Tofuku-ji

I skipped the taxi line and hopped straight onto the JR Nara Line inside the station. Tofukuji Station is only two minutes away, one hundred fifty yen tapped on my IC card. Out the gate, I turned right and followed a narrow lane squeezed between old stone walls and residential houses for about ten minutes, passing souvenir shops still shuttered, a cat leaping down from a tiled roof to eye me suspiciously. Before crossing the Tsutenkyo Bridge, I paid five hundred yen to enter the Hojo garden, timed to arrive right when it opened at nine — early enough to beat the tour buses that would flood in an hour later.

The Hojo's 'Ichimatsu no Niwa,' designed by Shigemori Mirei in 1939, arranges moss and square stone blocks into a checkerboard pattern that, at first glance, looks like a Mondrian painting transplanted into a dry landscape garden. I crouched by the veranda for nearly twenty minutes, listening to a young monk rake gravel with a bamboo broom, the soft scraping sound punctuating each square of moss and white sand. The lines are cut with an almost ruthless precision, yet I couldn't look away. Compared to the gilded extravagance of Kinkaku-ji, this felt like a quieter design language — one that only reveals itself if you slow down and crouch low enough to read it.

Kyoto's beauty never shouts. It hides between the grid lines and the empty spaces, waiting for you to go still first.

The Weight of Clay: Kawai Kanjiro's House and Owariya's Five-Tiered Lacquer Box

From Tofuku-ji I caught bus 207 to Gojozaka, then walked up the slope lined with Kiyomizu-ware pottery shops — the street is literally nicknamed 'Chawan-zaka,' Teacup Slope. Kawai Kanjiro's House sits tucked in a narrow alley, marked only by an unassuming wooden sign easy to miss. Entry is nine hundred yen. This was once the home and workshop of the great mingei movement potter, and there are almost no explanatory plaques — you're simply left to wander the tall wooden interior on your own. The sunken hearth still bears traces of use; the chairs and tables he designed by hand carry a heavy, worn patina, decades of palms having polished the wood to a soft shine. Behind the house, a climbing kiln with eight connected chambers snakes up a gentle slope like a sleeping dragon, the air still faintly smelling of aged firewood and ash. Standing at the kiln's mouth, I finally understood his insistence that 'use is beauty' — none of these vessels were made to be admired behind glass, but to be worn down through daily use.

At noon I bused over to the Nijo area and ducked into Honke Owariya, a soba restaurant with five hundred fifty years of history, housed in a beautifully preserved machiya with lattice doors and an interior garden. I ordered the signature Horai Soba, three thousand three hundred yen, served in a five-tiered vermillion lacquer box — each layer holding buckwheat noodles topped with something different: duck, nori, scallions, grated yam, a soft-poached egg. Unstacking it felt like unwrapping a gift. The noodles had a coarse, grainy bite and a faint nutty aroma, the dipping broth salty-sweet with depth. Cradling that lacquered box, I realized even a bowl of noodles here gets dressed in five layers of craftsmanship — Kyoto's devotion to presentation borders on religious.

Here, even a bowl of soba noodles arrives nested in five layers of lacquerware, as if to remind you design was never only about the surface.

The Warp and Weft of Nishijin: A City Remembered in Thread

By three in the afternoon I'd taken the Karasuma subway line to Imadegawa and walked into Nishijin. The alleys here are almost eerily quiet, tourist chatter replaced by the occasional clack of a loom drifting from behind a wooden facade — a reminder that this district once formed the beating heart of Japan's high-end kimono industry. I stepped into Orinasukan, a machiya converted from an old weaving workshop, its interior lined with antique hand looms and pattern-design sketches. A woman in her seventies, whom everyone called Yamaguchi-obasan, sat at a loom demonstrating tsuzure-ori tapestry weaving, her shuttle darting back and forth with a rhythmic clack like some ancient metronome. She explained that the most complex Nishijin patterns are controlled by punch cards that raise and lower the warp threads — a system borrowed from the nineteenth-century Jacquard loom, whose punch-card logic later inspired the earliest computers. A chill ran down my spine hearing this: this city had been quietly wired into the logic of 'programming' long before the industrial revolution ended. She poured me a cup of hojicha and warned me never to touch unfinished textile with bare fingers, since skin oils could discolor the threads over time — a rule passed down from her grandmother's generation. When I asked about the industry's shrinking future, she laughed a little ruefully, saying fewer than ten young people take up the craft seriously each year now.

If architecture is frozen music, then the clack of Nishijin's looms is proof this city is still breathing.

A Night of Folding Screens and Festival Drums

I'd deliberately timed my trip for mid-July, right in the middle of Gion Matsuri's Yoiyama nights. Around seven in the evening, I turned from Karasuma-dori onto Shinmachi-dori and stopped in my tracks: an entire row of normally shuttered machiya had thrown open their wooden lattice doors, paper lanterns glowing inside to illuminate family heirloom folding screens, gold leaf shimmering warmly in the light. This is Byobu Matsuri, the Folding Screen Festival. The street was packed with people in summer yukata, the air a mix of charred soy sauce from grilled corn stalls and the grassy scent of chimaki charms shaped like rice dumplings, while gion-bayashi music — gongs, drums, and flutes — drifted over from the floats in waves.

I stopped outside an old textile shop and struck up a conversation with the owner sitting at his doorstep. He pointed to a Rinpa-style screen inside, telling me it was his great-grandfather's, now in its fifth generation of family ownership, brought out into the open air only once a year during the festival for neighbors to admire. Flash photography, he warned firmly, was strictly forbidden — the metallic pigments couldn't withstand repeated bursts of bright light over the years. He handed me a chimaki charm hanging by his door, explaining it was meant to ward off illness for the coming year, its origin tracing back to a plague in the year 866. Gion Matsuri, he reminded me, wasn't born as a tourist spectacle but as a citywide ritual against disaster. That conversation completely reframed the crowded street for me — this wasn't mere festivity, but a collective memory of survival, kept alive for over a millennium through textile and paint.

By nine that night I wandered into the narrow alley of Pontocho, so tight two people had to turn sideways to pass, the riverside dining platforms above the Kamo River glowing amber and rippling in the water below. I took a seat at the counter of a small kappo restaurant and ordered a seasonal kaiseki course built around hamo, conger pike eel — a Gion Matsuri delicacy so beloved locally the festival is sometimes nicknamed 'Hamo Matsuri.' The chef sliced the skin paper-thin right in front of me, curling it into the shape of a peony blossom, blanched briefly, dipped in plum sauce. The countless tiny bones had been cut so meticulously I barely noticed them, leaving only a clean, delicate sweetness. The course ran about eight thousand yen. Watching the chef's focused hands as festival drums echoed faintly through the window, I realized Kyoto's design sensibility never lived only behind museum glass — it lives in a bowl of noodles, a slice of fish, a strand of silk thread, and an entire street willing to throw open its doors once a year.

The next morning I was back at Kyoto Station, waiting for my shinkansen home. I looked up once more at that glass ceiling and finally understood: this city's greatest design achievement was never any single building or craft object, but its ability to fold twelve hundred years of time seamlessly into the same narrow alley.
