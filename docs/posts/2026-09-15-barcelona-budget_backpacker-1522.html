中文

飛機降落在El Prat機場時是清晨六點半,機艙窗外還是一片濛濛的藍紫色,我拖著登機箱走出航廈,冷氣瞬間被地中海式的溫潤空氣取代——那是一種混著海水鹹味與汽油味的氣息,我後來才知道,這就是巴塞隆納機場特有的味道。這趟旅程我給自己訂下一個規矩:不訂五星飯店,不搭計程車,能走路就走路,能搭大眾運輸就搭大眾運輸,像個真正的背包客一樣,把每一分錢都花在刀口上。

拂曉抵達:從機場到哥德區的第一堂課

機場入境大廳的自動門一打開,就有三、四個穿著螢光背心、假裝拿著官方識別證的人湊上來說要幫我叫『官方計程車』,我學乖了,頭也不回地往機場巴士站牌走。Aerobus的站牌就在T1航廈出口正對面,藍白相間的招牌很好認,單程票5.9歐元,來回票10.2歐元,我買了來回,因為知道自己五天後還要從同一個機場飛走。巴士大概每十分鐘一班,車廂乾淨、有冷氣、也有免費Wifi,車程約35分鐘,終點站是加泰隆尼亞廣場(Plaça Catalunya),整個巴塞隆納舊城區的心臟地帶。

下車時大概早上七點四十分,廣場上噴水池的水聲、鴿子撲翅的聲音、還有清潔車刷洗石板路的水聲交織在一起,晨光斜斜地打在蘭布拉大道(La Rambla)的梧桐樹梢,樹葉的影子在地上晃啊晃。我背著背包沿著蘭布拉大道往南走,準備前往我訂好的青年旅館『Sant Jordi Hostel Rock Palace』,步行大概十五分鐘。路上第一個學到的教訓是:蘭布拉大道白天看起來人畜無害,但這裡是全巴塞隆納扒手密度最高的地方之一,我親眼看到一個穿著背心短褲的觀光客,手機從牛仔褲後口袋被抽走,他完全沒發現,直到要拍照才驚覺不對。我把背包整個轉到胸前抱著,護照跟現金分開放,這是我後來整趟旅程都維持的習慣。

青年旅館櫃檯的西班牙小哥聽說我從台灣來,笑著說『Very far!』,幫我辦好入住手續,一晚含早餐的六人房大概28歐元,床位上有插座跟置物櫃,鎖要自備或現場租(1歐元)。放下行李,沖了個澡,窗外已經是完全清醒的城市,樓下的咖啡機在磨豆子,空氣裡飄著烤麵包跟咖啡的香氣,我知道,真正的巴塞隆納一天,才剛要開始。

在蘭布拉大道上,防備心跟好奇心要同時打開,這是巴塞隆納給每個背包客的第一堂課。

聖家堂實戰攻略:訂票、爬塔與排隊眉角

放下行李後我沒有睡回籠覺,直接殺去聖家堂(Sagrada Família),因為我早就在出發前兩週在官網訂好了上午十點半、含『誕生立面塔樓』(Nativity Tower)的門票,票價33歐元。這裡是我這趟旅程唯一捨得多花錢的景點,原因很簡單:現場排隊買票的人潮通常要排一到兩小時,而且熱門的塔樓時段常常提前一週就被訂光。

從加泰隆尼亞廣場搭地鐵L2線(紫線)或L5線(藍線)到Sagrada Família站,車程約十分鐘,單程用T-Casual十次卡,一趟大概1.14歐元。一出地鐵站電扶梯,還沒轉身,那八座尚未完工的高塔就已經刺穿了天空,我整個人愣在原地將近一分鐘——照片再怎麼看都比不上親眼看到那種粗糲又細膩並存的石雕質感,近看誕生立面上密密麻麻的葡萄、烏龜、天使雕刻,每一吋都像正在生長的有機體。

進場前我特別注意到官網跟現場都會提醒:如果買了塔樓票,必須在指定時段前十五分鐘到達安檢口,遲到超過時段就會被要求改到下一梯次甚至作廢,我因為在附近的麵包店買可頌耽誤了五分鐘,差點被擋在門外,後來跟安檢人員解釋才勉強放行,這是給後來者的提醒:塔樓行程真的要抓緊時間。爬塔樓的樓梯非常窄,螺旋而下時腿會有點抖,建議背包客帶少量隨身物品就好,大件行李寄物櫃在入口處,一次1歐元。整個參觀含塔樓大概花了我兩個半小時,中殿內部的七彩玻璃在正午陽光直射時,把整片石柱森林染成流動的藍紫色與金黃色,那一刻我理解了高第所說『大自然才是最偉大的建築師』是什麼意思。

波蓋利亞市場的清晨滋味

隔天我刻意起了個大早,七點半就從旅館出發,走進蘭布拉大道中段的波蓋利亞市場(Mercat de la Boqueria)。這個時間點是在地人採買的時段,遊覽車還沒到,攤位間充滿了魚鱗與柑橘混合的氣味,魚販正把整箱冰塊倒進海鮮攤上,冰塊碰撞的聲音清脆又響亮。

我直奔市場深處一個沒有招牌卻永遠排隊的吧台——El Quim de la Boqueria,老闆Quim本人常常就站在鐵板前親自料理。我點了招牌的『炒蛋佐鵝肝與馬鈴薯泥』(Huevos estrellados con foie),12.5歐元,配一杯現榨柳橙汁2.5歐元。蛋液在滾燙的鐵板上被快速攪散,半熟的蛋黃裹著炸得金黃酥脆的馬鈴薯條,鵝肝一放上去瞬間微微融化,鹹香跟油脂香在嘴裡炸開,搭配旁邊坐著的巴塞隆納在地上班族,他們喝著一小杯cortado咖啡,跟老闆用加泰隆尼亞語聊著昨晚的足球賽,那種市場才有的喧鬧跟煙火氣,是任何米其林餐廳都給不了的。

吃完蛋我又忍不住走到旁邊的Pinotxo Bar,這是波蓋利亞市場裡歷史最悠久的吧台之一,老闆Juanito已經在這裡站了超過五十年。我加點了一份鷹嘴豆燉血腸,6歐元,燉得軟爛入味的血腸帶著淡淡肉桂與丁香香氣,配著吧台旁的老先生用西班牙文跟我閒聊,他說他每天早上都來這裡吃早餐,『這裡比我家廚房更像我家』,一句話讓我對這座市場的感情瞬間濃厚起來。整個市場早餐吃下來大概花了21歐元,但那種被煙燻火燎、人聲鼎沸包圍的用餐體驗,是我整趟旅程裡最鮮明的記憶之一。

波蓋利亞市場教我的事:巴塞隆納最好吃的早餐,永遠沒有招牌,只有排隊的當地人。

深夜Tapas吧與海灘上的生活節奏

下午兩點到五點,整座城市像被按下暫停鍵,El Born區的服飾店紛紛拉下鐵門,連咖啡館都只留自動門,這是傳承已久的Siesta文化。我起初很不習慣,一心想著要繼續逛街卻撲了個空,後來索性學在地人,回旅館小睡一下,才明白這座城市的節奏是『白天休息,晚上才是正餐』。

晚上九點,我才慢悠悠走進El Born巷弄裡的El Xampanyet,這是一間開業超過八十年的老字號卡瓦吧,店內磁磚牆面斑駁,天花板掛著醃漬桶,一走進去就是滿滿的醋漬鯷魚跟橡木桶的氣味。我點了一杯招牌的自家氣泡酒配一份鯷魚,酒加小菜大概6歐元,鯷魚酸香帶點微微的大蒜味,配上冰涼氣泡酒,在悶熱的九月晚上格外爽口。吧台擠滿了下班後的當地人,大家都是站著吃,肩並肩碰杯,聊天聲跟玻璃杯碰撞聲混在一起,完全感受不到觀光客的拘謹。

吃飽喝足後,我搭地鐵L4線到Barceloneta站,走十分鐘到海灘,晚上十一點的巴塞隆納塔海灘依然熱鬧,沙灘上有人彈吉他、有人光著腳踩浪,空氣中混著防曬乳、烤玉米攤跟海水的鹹味。要提醒的是,沙灘上常有人兜售自製Mojito或啤酒,一杯大約3歐元,雖然便宜但屬於違法街賣,警察偶爾會巡邏取締,買的時候動作都很匆忙,衛生條件也難以保證,我後來選擇在海灘旁的合法酒吧買了一瓶Estrella啤酒,2.5歐元,安心地坐在防波堤上,看著遠處奧林匹克港的燈塔一閃一閃,才真正感覺自己融入了這座城市深夜的呼吸。

美賽節的火焰與人塔:節慶尾聲與背包客的帳本

我這趟剛好遇上九月下旬的美賽節(La Mercè),整座城市為了守護聖母美賽而陷入為期一週的慶典。傍晚六點,我跟著人潮擠進聖哈梅廣場(Plaça Sant Jaume),廣場中央圍出一圈空地,一群穿著白衣、腰間綁著寬版黑腰帶的男女老少,正一層一層疊起人塔(Castells),最底層是壯漢們手勾著手扛住整個結構,最頂端爬上去的是一個看起來不超過八歲的小女孩,全場觀眾屏氣凝神,直到她高舉手臂比出勝利手勢,現場才爆出如雷掌聲。我旁邊一位帶著孩子來看表演的巴塞隆納媽媽Marta跟我解釋,人塔文化源自加泰隆尼亞鄉間,『爬到最頂端的孩子,是整個社區最信任的孩子』,那一刻我才明白這不只是表演,而是一整個社區世代傳承的信任儀式。

晚上十點,廣場周邊開始施放Correfoc(火焰奔跑),打扮成惡魔模樣的表演者手持噴火煙火棒,在人群中奔跑穿梭,火花四濺,現場瀰漫濃濃的硫磺與煙硝味,主辦單位會提醒圍觀民眾穿長袖、戴帽子,我因為只穿了短袖T恤,被熱烈的火星燙到手臂,雖然不嚴重但也讓我學到:參加Correfoc一定要做好防護,這不是隔著安全距離看的煙火秀,而是真正會被波及的近距離火焰儀式。

回顧這五天四夜的巴塞隆納背包旅程,住宿花了112歐元(四晚青年旅館),交通含機場巴士與市區地鐵十次卡約28歐元,餐飲平均一天30歐元左右(市場早餐加一次tapas晚餐),景點門票(聖家堂、奎爾公園)加起來約60歐元,總花費控制在330歐元左右,不含機票。最後想提醒後來的背包客幾件事:奎爾公園(Park Güell)一定要提前上官網訂票,現場常常額滿;地鐵三號線跟蘭布拉大道是扒手熱區,包包一定要抱在前面;許多標榜『Menu del Día』的餐廳其實是觀光陷阱,價格比在地人常去的小店貴上一倍,選店訣竅是往巷弄裡走,看到裡面坐滿本地老人與上班族的店,通常錯不了。巴塞隆納教會我最重要的一課是,慢下來,跟著這座城市的呼吸走,才能真正吃到、看到、感受到屬於它的靈魂。

ENGLISH

The plane touched down at El Prat at half past six in the morning, the sky outside still a hazy blue-violet. I dragged my carry-on out of the terminal and the air-conditioning gave way instantly to warm Mediterranean air—a smell of salt water mixed faintly with jet fuel, which I'd later learn is simply the particular scent of this airport. I'd made myself one rule for this trip: no five-star hotels, no taxis, walk whenever possible, take public transport for everything else. I wanted to travel like an actual backpacker, spending every euro deliberately.

Dawn Arrival: My First Lesson from Airport to Gothic Quarter

The moment the arrivals hall doors slid open, three or four men in fluorescent vests waving fake-looking badges swarmed toward me offering an 'official taxi.' I'd read enough warnings to know better, so I walked straight past them toward the airport bus stop. The Aerobus stop sits directly across from the Terminal 1 exit, easy to spot with its blue-and-white signage. A one-way ticket costs €5.90, round trip €10.20—I bought the return since I knew I'd be flying out of the same airport five days later. Buses run roughly every ten minutes, the cabin is clean, air-conditioned, with free WiFi, and the 35-minute ride ends at Plaça Catalunya, the beating heart of Barcelona's old town.

I stepped off around 7:40am into a square filled with the sound of fountains, pigeons flapping, and street-cleaning trucks scrubbing the cobblestones. Morning light slanted through the plane trees lining La Rambla, shadows swaying on the pavement. I shouldered my backpack and walked south down La Rambla toward my hostel, Sant Jordi Hostel Rock Palace, about fifteen minutes away. My first real lesson of the trip came quickly: La Rambla looks harmless in daylight, but it's one of the densest pickpocket zones in the entire city. I watched a tourist in a tank top and shorts get his phone lifted clean out of his back pocket—he had no idea until he reached for it to take a photo. From then on I carried my backpack against my chest, and split my cash and passport into separate pockets, a habit I kept for the rest of the trip.

The Spanish guy at hostel reception heard I'd flown in from Taiwan and laughed, 'Very far!' before checking me in. A bed in a six-person dorm with breakfast included ran about €28 a night, with an outlet and locker built into each bunk—you either bring your own padlock or rent one for €1. I dropped my bags, showered, and by the time I looked out the window the city was fully awake: the coffee machine downstairs grinding beans, the smell of toasted bread drifting up the stairwell. My real first day in Barcelona was only just beginning.

On La Rambla, curiosity and caution have to switch on at the same time—that's the first lesson Barcelona teaches every backpacker.

Sagrada Família in Practice: Booking, Climbing, and Queue Etiquette

I didn't nap after dropping my bags. Instead I headed straight for Sagrada Família, since I'd already booked a 10:30am slot online two weeks before departure, including access to the Nativity Tower, for €33. This was the one attraction I let myself splurge on, for a simple reason: walk-up lines routinely run one to two hours, and popular tower time slots often sell out a full week ahead.

From Plaça Catalunya, I took metro line L2 (purple) or L5 (blue) to Sagrada Família station, about a ten-minute ride, roughly €1.14 per trip on a T-Casual ten-journey card. The moment I rode the escalator up and out of the station, before I'd even turned around, the eight unfinished towers were already piercing the sky above me. I stood frozen for almost a full minute—photographs never do justice to that combination of raw, unfinished stone and impossibly delicate carving. Up close, the Nativity Façade is packed with grapes, turtles, and angels, every inch looking like something still growing.

Before entering, both the website and staff on-site repeatedly warn that tower-ticket holders must arrive at the security checkpoint fifteen minutes before their slot, or risk being pushed to a later group or denied entirely. I lost five minutes buying a croissant at a nearby bakery and nearly got turned away—only after explaining myself to security was I let through. Consider this a warning: if you've booked the tower, guard your schedule fiercely. The spiral staircase down from the tower is extremely narrow, and your legs will genuinely shake by the bottom, so bring only minimal belongings; larger bags go into lockers at the entrance for €1. The whole visit, towers included, took about two and a half hours. When midday sun hit the stained glass inside the nave, the entire forest of stone columns turned into a flowing wash of blue-violet and gold, and in that moment I finally understood what Gaudí meant when he said nature is the greatest architect.

Breakfast at La Boqueria: What Locals Actually Eat

The next morning I deliberately got up early, leaving the hostel at 7:30am to walk into Mercat de la Boqueria, halfway down La Rambla. This early, it's still locals doing their shopping—the tour buses haven't arrived yet—and the air between stalls smells of fish scales mixed with citrus, fishmongers dumping crates of ice onto seafood displays with a sharp, bright clatter.

I made straight for a sign-less counter deep inside the market that always has a line anyway: El Quim de la Boqueria, where owner Quim himself is often standing right at the flat-top grill. I ordered the signature 'huevos estrellados con foie'—scrambled eggs with foie gras and crispy potatoes—for €12.50, with a fresh-squeezed orange juice for €2.50. The eggs are scrambled fast on a scorching hot plate, the yolk still runny, wrapped around golden fried potato sticks; the moment the foie gras hits the heat it softens just slightly, and the combination of salt and fat explodes in your mouth. Beside me sat local office workers sipping small cortados, chatting with the owner in Catalan about last night's football match—that kind of noisy, unpolished vitality is something no Michelin restaurant can replicate.

Still hungry, I wandered over to Pinotxo Bar next door, one of the oldest counters in the entire market, where owner Juanito has been standing behind the bar for over fifty years. I ordered a chickpea-and-blood-sausage stew for €6, the sausage stewed until soft with faint notes of cinnamon and clove. An old man beside me struck up a conversation in Spanish, telling me he comes here for breakfast every single morning—'this place feels more like home than my own kitchen,' he said, and that one line made me fall for this market instantly. My whole breakfast came to about €21, but that experience—smoke-cured, crowded, and loud—remains one of the sharpest memories of the entire trip.

What La Boqueria taught me: Barcelona's best breakfasts never have a sign—just a line of locals waiting.

Late-Night Tapas Bars and the Rhythm of the Beach

Between two and five in the afternoon, the whole city seems to hit pause. Shops in El Born pull down their metal shutters, even cafés leave only a single automatic door open—the legacy of siesta culture. I fought it at first, annoyed at finding shops closed when I wanted to browse, but eventually gave in and napped back at the hostel like everyone else. That's when I understood the city's real rhythm: rest through the day, and the real meal happens at night.

At nine that evening, I finally strolled into El Xampanyet, tucked in a narrow street in El Born, a cava bar that's been open for more than eighty years. The tiled walls are worn and mismatched, pickling barrels hang from the ceiling, and the whole place smells of vinegar-cured anchovies and old oak. I ordered a glass of their house cava with a plate of boquerones en vinagre, drink and tapa together running about €6. The anchovies were tangy with a faint hit of garlic, perfectly matched with the cold, crisp bubbles on a muggy September night. The bar was packed with locals just off work, everyone standing, clinking glasses shoulder to shoulder, conversation and glassware clattering together—nothing about it felt staged for tourists.

Full and pleasantly buzzed, I took the L4 metro to Barceloneta station and walked ten minutes to the beach. Even at eleven at night, Platja de la Barceloneta was alive—someone strumming a guitar, others walking barefoot through the surf, the air a mix of sunscreen, grilled corn from a nearby cart, and salt water. Worth noting: vendors on the beach often sell homemade mojitos or beer for around €3 each—cheap, but technically illegal street sales, and police do occasionally sweep through. The transactions happen fast and hygiene is questionable at best. I ended up buying a bottled Estrella beer for €2.50 at a legitimate beach bar instead, sitting on the breakwater watching the Olympic Port lighthouse blink in the distance. That was the moment I finally felt in sync with this city's nighttime pulse.

Fire and Human Towers: La Mercè and the Backpacker's Final Ledger

My trip happened to coincide with La Mercè in late September, when the entire city spends a week celebrating its patron saint. At six in the evening, I pushed through the crowd into Plaça Sant Jaume, where a ring of open space had formed in the center. A group in white shirts with wide black sashes around their waists—men, women, children, all ages—were building a human tower, or castell, layer by layer. The base was a mass of men gripping each other's arms to anchor the whole structure, while the person climbing to the very top was a girl who couldn't have been older than eight. The entire crowd held its breath until she raised her arm in a victory salute at the summit, and the plaza erupted in applause. A local mother named Marta, standing next to me with her own child, explained that castell culture originated in rural Catalonia—'the child who climbs to the top is the one the whole community trusts most,' she told me. That's when I understood this wasn't performance for spectacle; it was a ritual of trust passed down through generations of a community.

By ten that night, the streets around the plaza filled with Correfoc, the 'fire run,' where performers dressed as devils sprint through the crowd carrying fireworks that spark and spray in every direction. The air turned thick with sulfur and smoke, and organizers repeatedly warned onlookers to wear long sleeves and hats. I'd only worn a short-sleeve T-shirt and ended up with a small burn on my arm from a stray spark—nothing serious, but it taught me that Correfoc isn't a fireworks show you watch from a safe distance; it's a close-contact fire ritual, and you will get hit if you're not covered up.

Looking back on five days and four nights in Barcelona, accommodation cost me €112 for four hostel nights, transportation—including the airport bus and a metro ten-journey card—came to about €28, food averaged around €30 a day (a market breakfast plus one tapas dinner), and attraction tickets (Sagrada Família and Park Güell combined) added up to roughly €60. My total spend landed around €330, not including flights. A few final notes for anyone following in my footsteps: book Park Güell online well in advance, as walk-up slots fill fast; metro line 3 and La Rambla remain pickpocket hotspots, so keep your bag in front of you at all times; and restaurants advertising a 'Menu del Día' near tourist landmarks are often overpriced traps charging double what locals pay just a few streets over—walk into the side alleys instead, and if you see a place full of local retirees and office workers, you're almost certainly in the right spot. The biggest lesson Barcelona taught me was to slow down and move at the city's own rhythm—that's the only way to actually taste, see, and feel what belongs to it.
