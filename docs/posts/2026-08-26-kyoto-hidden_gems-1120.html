中文

凌晨四點五十分,我的鬧鐘在京都車站附近的膠囊旅館裡響起。窗外一片墨藍,連便利商店的招牌都還沒完全甦醒。這是我第三次來京都,前兩次都在清水寺的坂道上跟著滿滿的觀光團龜速移動,這一次,我決定把行程完全交給時間差——所有人潮還沒醒來之前的京都,才是京都人真正生活的城市。

破曉前的伏見稻荷:當狐狸還醒著,遊客還沒到

五點十五分,我從JR稻荷駅走出來,月台上只有我跟一位提著垃圾袋準備打掃的伯伯。伏見稻荷大社的千本鳥居白天永遠塞滿人,拍一張沒有陌生人入鏡的照片幾乎是不可能的任務,但清晨五點半的這裡,只剩下我的腳步聲跟山間的鳥叫。從本殿出發,穿過奧社奉拜所,一路往上爬,大約二十分鐘後會抵達「四つ辻」——這裡是整段登山路線的第一個觀景台,可以俯瞰京都盆地,晨霧還沒散,遠方的京都塔像一根細針插在灰白色的雲層裡。大多數觀光客走到四つ辻就折返了,但我繼續往上,沿著較窄的石階往「一ノ峰」的方向走,路變得濕滑,兩旁的鳥居顏色也斑駁許多,顯然維護的頻率不如山下。整趟到山頂來回大約花了兩個半小時,中途在「御膳谷奉拝所」的自動販賣機買了一瓶熱綠茶暖手,五十日圓的香油錢投進去,聽見銅板落下的聲音在空山裡特別清脆。要提醒的是,山路沒有路燈,手機手電筒是必備品,而且七月盛夏這段山路蚊蟲極多,長袖比短袖實用得多。整趟行程完全免費,只在幾間小茶屋門口的木箱投香油錢,五十到一百日圓不等。七點半下山時,第一批遊覽車正好抵達山腳,我在人群逆流而上時,忽然有種偷跑了一場的竊喜。

京都最誠實的樣子,往往藏在觀光客還沒起床的那兩個小時裡。

上賀茂社家町的晨光與明神川

八點十分,我搭市巴士北上到上賀茂神社。這裡是京都最古老的神社之一,卻因為位置偏北,幾乎沒有旅行團會排進行程。神社本身固然莊嚴,但真正讓我駐足的是神社外圍那條「明神川」——沿岸是被稱為「社家町」的白牆聚落,自古以來就是為上賀茂神社服務的神職人員住所,家家戶戶門前都有小橋跨過清澈的水渠。我在其中一戶人家門口,遇見一位七十多歲的婆婆正蹲在水邊清洗一籃深紫色的茄子。她告訴我那是「賀茂茄子」,是京都的傳統京野菜之一,個頭比一般茄子圓胖許多,只有在上賀茂一帶的黏質壤土才種得出這種口感。她一邊沖洗一邊跟我閒聊,說她從小就在這條川邊長大,小時候夏天會跟兄弟姊妹在水裡抓螢火蟲的幼蟲,現在水質雖然不如從前,但每年六月初,傍晚時分還是能看見零星的螢光在渠道兩旁閃爍。她說得雲淡風輕,我卻聽得入神——這種未經修飾的生活片段,是任何旅遊書都寫不出來的京都。臨走前她塞了兩根還帶著水珠的小黃瓜給我,說是自家菜園種的,要我路上配著吃。

一乘寺深處的靜謐:詩仙堂與金福寺

九點半,我轉搭叡山電鐵到一乘寺,這一帶因為有幾間老字號拉麵店而被年輕人稱作「拉麵激戰區」,但很少人知道,拉麵店背後的坡道往上走十分鐘,藏著兩座極安靜的庭園寺院——詩仙堂與金福寺。詩仙堂原是江戶初期文人石川丈山的隱居之所,門口不起眼,售票處甚至沒有電子看板,只有一位穿著藏青色作務衣的老先生坐在木框窗後收兩百五十日圓的門票。走進去之後,整個空間豁然安靜下來,枯山水的白砂被耙成同心圓,苔蘭沿著石階邊緣蔓延,偶爾傳來竹製「添水」敲擊石頭的「咚」一聲,用來嚇走鹿群,卻也成了整座庭園唯一的節奏。我在緣廊坐了將近四十分鐘,身邊只有兩對日本老夫妻低聲交談,沒有一句英文,也沒有任何人舉著自拍棒。走出詩仙堂,沿著竹林小徑再往上五分鐘,便是金福寺,這裡與俳句詩人松尾芭蕉、與謝蕪村都有淵源,寺內的芭蕉庵小巧得像個放大的鳥籠,站在庵前望向遠方的比叡山稜線,忽然明白為什麼古人會選在這裡寫詩——安靜到連自己的呼吸聲都聽得見。

今宮神社門前的炙餅與西陣午後

中午十二點半,肚子開始抗議,我搭公車轉往西陣的今宮神社。神社本身供奉的是鎮疫之神,每年四月的「やすらい祭」被列為京都三大奇祭之一,但吸引我的其實是神社東門參道兩側那兩間對望超過三百年的老鋪——「一文字屋和輔」(かざりや)與另一間「一和」。我選了かざりや,推開暖簾坐進榻榻米座位,點了一份炙餅,五百日圓十三支。竹籤上串著拇指大小的麻糬,先裹上黃豆粉再用備長炭火直接炙烤,表面帶著焦香的斑點,最後淋上以白味噌調製的甜味醬汁。咬下去外層微脆帶炭香,內裡卻是溫熱綿密的麻糬芯,白味噌的鹹甜滋味在舌尖化開,配著店家奉上的熱焙茶,那種樸實的滿足感遠勝過任何精緻甜點。店裡負責烤麻糬的是一位穿著藍染圍裙的阿姨,手法俐落得像是重複了幾十年的肌肉記憶——後來我才知道,這間店真的從平安時代開到現在,是全世界公認最古老的餐飲店之一,而它至今仍然只是一間鄰里熟悉的路邊茶屋,沒有排隊人龍,沒有整理過度的網美布置。吃完炙餅,我又沿著西陣的巷弄晃了一圈,這裡曾是京都織品業的心臟地帶,午後陽光斜斜灑進狹窄的巷道,不時能聽見織布機規律的喀噠聲從老屋子裡傳出來,那是還在運作的家庭式西陣織工坊。

真正的京都美食,往往不在排隊名單上,而在一位阿姨重複了半輩子的手勢裡。

上七軒花街的黃昏:一見さんお断りの世界

下午四點半,我走進上七軒——京都最古老的花街,比祇園還要早了將近兩百年,卻因為規模小、遊客少,幾乎沒有出現在多數旅遊清單上。石板路兩側是低矮的木造茶屋,家家戶戶門口掛著白底紅字的燈籠,寫著茶屋的名號。我在一間老茶屋「老松」買了幾個和菓子,順口問了店主人「一見さんお断り」到底是怎麼一回事。老闆娘笑著解釋,茶屋文化講究的是信任與熟客介紹制,第一次上門的客人如果沒有熟客帶路,茶屋不會讓你進去,不是傲慢,而是因為茶屋要為客人量身安排藝伎、料理與話題,沒有背景資訊根本無從準備。她說每年二月三日節分祭那天,是少數外人也能近距離接觸上七軒藝伎與舞伎的機會——當天她們會盛裝前往附近的北野天滿宮參拜、撒豆驅邪,沿途民眾可以自由圍觀,完全不需要預約或介紹人。我後來特地查了時間,把隔年二月的行程記進手機備忘錄裡,決定下次一定要專程再來一趟。傍晚五點四十分,我沿著石板路走到盡頭,轉進附近的船岡山,這是京都人私下推薦的「大文字送り火」觀賞地點,不像鴨川沿岸那樣人擠人,山丘上只有零星幾組在地家庭鋪著野餐墊。夕陽把整座京都盆地染成柿子色,寺院的瓦頂一片接一片地暗下來,我坐在草地上,聽著遠處傳來的寺廟晚鐘聲,忽然覺得這趟避開人潮的旅程,反而讓我更靠近了這座城市真正的心跳。

晚上七點,我沒有回市區找排隊名店,而是走進町家巷弄裡一間只有六個吧檯座位的小料理屋,老闆娘一個人張羅著所有客人,菜單只有手寫的三行字。我點了一份季節野菜天婦羅拼盤和一杯玉之光純米酒,一千八百日圓,坐在吧檯邊看她俐落地把賀茂茄子下鍋油炸——沒錯,就是早上在明神川邊看見婆婆清洗的那種茄子。那一刻我忽然覺得,這一整天的路線,像是被某種看不見的線悄悄串在一起:清晨的稻荷山、上賀茂的川水、詩仙堂的寂靜、西陣的炙餅香、上七軒的燈籠,最後在這碗熱騰騰的天婦羅裡收尾。京都從不缺少壯觀的景點,缺的只是願意在天還沒亮就出門的旅人。

ENGLISH

My alarm went off at 4:50am in a capsule hotel near Kyoto Station, the sky outside still ink blue, even the convenience store signs seemed half asleep. This was my third trip to Kyoto, and the first two had been spent shuffling behind tour groups on the sloped lanes of Kiyomizu-dera. This time I decided to build the entire itinerary around one simple idea: the Kyoto that exists before the crowds wake up is the only Kyoto that still belongs to the people who live there.

Before Dawn at Fushimi Inari: When the Foxes Are Still Awake and the Tourists Aren't

At 5:15am I stepped off the train at Inari Station, the platform empty except for an old man sweeping with a trash bag in hand. By daylight, the thousand torii gates of Fushimi Inari Taisha are permanently jammed with visitors, and getting a photo without a stranger in frame is close to impossible. But at half past five in the morning, all that existed was the sound of my own footsteps and birds calling somewhere in the hills. From the main hall I passed the Okusha worship site and kept climbing; about twenty minutes up, the path opens onto Yotsutsuji, the first viewpoint on the mountain, where you can look out over the Kyoto basin. The morning mist hadn't yet cleared, and Kyoto Tower stood in the distance like a thin needle pinned into a pale grey sky. Most tourists turn back at Yotsutsuji, but I kept going, following the narrower stone steps toward Ichinomine at the summit. The path grew slick, the vermillion paint on the gates more weathered, clearly maintained less often than the gates below. The full round trip to the summit and back took about two and a half hours. Halfway up, at the Gozenbani worship site, I bought a hot green tea from a vending machine and dropped a fifty-yen coin into a wooden offering box outside a small teahouse, the coin's clink echoing strangely loud in the empty mountain air. Worth noting: there are no streetlights on this trail, so a phone flashlight is essential, and in midsummer the mosquitoes on the upper paths are relentless — long sleeves beat shorts, no question. The entire hike costs nothing beyond small optional donations of fifty to a hundred yen at each teahouse. By 7:30am, as I descended, the first tour buses were just pulling into the base of the mountain, and walking against that incoming tide of visitors gave me a small, guilty thrill, like I'd gotten away with something.

Kyoto's most honest self usually hides in the two hours before the tourists get out of bed.

At 8:10am I took a city bus north to Kamigamo Shrine, one of Kyoto's oldest shrines, yet rarely included in tour itineraries simply because of its distance from downtown. The shrine itself is stately, but what really stopped me in my tracks was the Myojingawa canal running along its edge, lined by the whitewashed houses of Shake-machi, a neighborhood that has housed Shinto priests serving the shrine for centuries. Each house has a small bridge crossing the clear canal at its doorstep. In front of one house I found an elderly woman crouched by the water, washing a basket of deep purple eggplants. She told me they were Kamo eggplants, a traditional Kyoto vegetable, plumper and rounder than ordinary eggplants, and that only the clay-rich soil of this particular district produces their distinct texture. As she rinsed them she chatted easily, telling me she grew up along this canal, that as a child she and her siblings used to catch firefly larvae in the water during summer, and that even now, in early June, faint flickers of firefly light still appear along the canal at dusk, though not as many as before. She said it all so plainly, but I was riveted — this kind of unpolished, lived-in detail is something no guidebook can manufacture. Before I left, she pressed two dew-covered small cucumbers into my hands, telling me they were from her own garden and I should eat them on the road.

Silence in the Depths of Ichijoji: Shisen-do and Konpuku-ji

At 9:30am I switched to the Eizan Railway toward Ichijoji, a neighborhood young locals half-jokingly call ramen battleground for its cluster of famous shops. Few visitors realize that a ten-minute walk uphill past those ramen counters leads to two remarkably quiet temple gardens: Shisen-do and Konpuku-ji. Shisen-do was once the retirement retreat of the Edo-period scholar Ishikawa Jozan; its entrance is unassuming, and the ticket counter isn't even electronic — just an elderly man in an indigo work coat sitting behind a wooden window frame, collecting a 250-yen entry fee. Inside, the space falls suddenly still. Raked white sand forms concentric circles in the dry garden, moss creeps along the stone steps, and every so often a bamboo shishi-odori knocks against a rock with a single hollow clack, traditionally used to scare off deer, though here it simply became the garden's only rhythm. I sat on the veranda for nearly forty minutes, the only other visitors two elderly Japanese couples speaking in hushed tones — not a word of English, not a single selfie stick in sight. From Shisen-do, a five-minute walk along a bamboo path leads to Konpuku-ji, associated with the haiku poets Matsuo Basho and Yosa Buson. The tiny Basho-an hermitage inside looks like an oversized birdcage, and standing before it, looking toward the ridgeline of Mount Hiei in the distance, I finally understood why poets chose to write here — it's quiet enough to hear your own breathing.

By 12:30pm my stomach was protesting, so I took a bus toward Nishijin and Imamiya Shrine. The shrine itself is dedicated to a deity of plague prevention, and its Yasurai Festival every April is considered one of Kyoto's three strangest festivals, but what drew me was the pair of teahouses facing each other across the shrine's eastern approach, both over three hundred years old — Ichimonjiya Wasuke, known as Kazariya, and its rival across the path, Ichiwa. I chose Kazariya, slid open the noren curtain, and sat on the tatami to order a plate of aburi mochi — thirteen skewers for 500 yen. Each skewer holds thumb-sized pieces of mochi first coated in roasted soybean flour, then grilled directly over binchotan charcoal until lightly charred, and finally drizzled with a sweet white miso sauce. The outside was crisp and faintly smoky, the inside warm and impossibly soft, the sweet-salty miso melting on my tongue with each bite, paired with a cup of hot roasted tea the shop brought without being asked. That plain, unfussy satisfaction beat any elaborately plated dessert I'd had all trip. The woman grilling the mochi wore a blue-dyed apron and worked with the practiced economy of decades of muscle memory — I later learned this shop has been operating continuously since the Heian period, making it one of the oldest food establishments on earth, and yet it remains, to this day, just a neighborhood teahouse with no queue, no over-styled photo corners. After eating, I wandered further into Nishijin's back alleys, once the beating heart of Kyoto's textile industry. In the slanting afternoon light I could still hear the rhythmic clack of looms coming from inside old houses — small family-run Nishijin-ori weaving workshops still at work.

The real food of Kyoto is rarely on a waiting list — it's in the hands of a woman who has repeated the same motion for half a lifetime.

At 4:30pm I walked into Kamishichiken, Kyoto's oldest hanamachi geisha district, established nearly two centuries before Gion, yet largely absent from most travel itineraries thanks to its small size and modest visitor numbers. Stone-paved lanes are lined with low wooden teahouses, each doorway hung with a white lantern printed with red characters bearing the teahouse's name. At an old confectionery shop called Oimatsu, I bought a few wagashi sweets and asked the owner to explain the custom of ichigen-san okotowari — the tradition of refusing first-time customers. She laughed and explained that teahouse culture runs on trust and introduction: a first-time guest without a regular patron vouching for them won't be admitted, not out of arrogance, but because a teahouse needs background information to properly arrange geisha, cuisine, and conversation suited to that guest. She mentioned that every year on February 3rd, Setsubun, ordinary visitors get one of their rare chances to see Kamishichiken's geiko and maiko up close, as they visit nearby Kitano Tenmangu Shrine in full formal dress to throw beans and drive away evil spirits — no reservation or introduction required, just show up. I made a note in my phone right there to build next year's trip around that date. At 5:40pm I walked to the end of the stone lane and up nearby Funaoka-yama, a hill locals quietly recommend for watching the Daimonji Gozan Okuribi bonfire festival, far less crowded than the riverbanks along the Kamo River. Only a scattering of local families sat on picnic mats on the grass. The setting sun turned the entire Kyoto basin the color of a ripe persimmon, temple roofs going dark one after another, and as I sat listening to a distant evening bell, I realized this whole day of avoiding the crowds had brought me closer to the actual heartbeat of the city.

At 7pm, instead of heading back downtown to queue for a famous restaurant, I ducked into a tiny six-seat counter restaurant tucked in a machiya alley, run single-handedly by one woman, the menu just three lines of handwriting. I ordered a seasonal vegetable tempura set and a glass of Tamanohikari junmai sake, about 1,800 yen total, and watched from the counter as she dropped Kamo eggplant into the fryer — yes, the very same variety I'd watched an old woman wash by the canal that morning. In that moment the whole day suddenly felt stitched together by an invisible thread: the dawn climb up Inari mountain, the canal water of Kamigamo, the silence of Shisen-do, the smoky sweetness of grilled mochi, the lanterns of Kamishichiken, all converging in this bowl of hot tempura. Kyoto has never lacked spectacular sights. What it lacks is travelers willing to leave the hotel before the sun comes up.
