中文

飛機在傍晚六點半降落登巴薩伍拉萊國際機場(DPS)的那一刻,機艙外飄進來的空氣裡混著椰子防曬油、汽油與濕熱土地的味道,那是我對峇里島最初的印象。我背著一個用了五年、肩帶已經磨到起毛球的65公升登山包,護照夾層裡還塞著剛落地簽證留下的皺巴巴收據。接下來十天,我打算用最貼近在地人的方式,把烏布這座被外國人稱為「心靈小鎮」的地方,走到骨子裡去。

落地峇里:從機場到烏布的交通實戰

出關之後我才知道,峇里島機場對Grab、Gojek這類叫車軟體設下了重重限制——司機不能直接開進航廈接客,得走到機場外圍指定的接送點才能上車,而那段路,機場排班計程車的招攬員會用各種話術攔住你:「先生,叫車軟體在這裡是違法的喔」「外面很遠很危險」。我沒理會,拖著行李箱在35度的濕熱空氣裡走了將近十分鐘,穿過停車場外圍的圍籬缺口,才終於在Google Maps定位的角落等到一台Grab機車。但機車後座塞不下我的大背包,司機建議我改叫四人座的Grab Car,結果又因為系統偵測到我在機場範圍內而被自動取消了三次。

最後我放棄治療,走回機場官方計程車櫃檯,用印尼盾現金付了固定價Rp450,000(約新台幣900元)搭上藍鳥計程車(Blue Bird)——這是峇里島少數口碑良好、跳表誠實的計程車行,車身是深藍色,司機證件會掛在儀表板上。車程原本應該只要五十分鐘,但傍晚七點多的Jalan By Pass Ngurah Rai塞成一條發光的長龍,機車引擎聲、廟宇傳出的甘美朗(Gamelan)樂音、路邊小攤販炭烤沙嗲的煙霧,全部混在一起從搖下的車窗灌進來。抵達烏布市中心的Jalan Raya Ubud時,已經快晚上九點,我在便利商店買了一張Telkomsel的觀光客SIM卡,15GB上網流量加通話,大概Rp150,000,店員手腳俐落地幫我插卡設定,整個過程不到五分鐘。

在烏布,時間感是被廟鐘、雞鳴和機車喇叭聲重新校準的,而不是手錶。

住進烏布:民宿選擇與真實花費

我在Penestanan區、遠離主街喧囂的一條巷子裡,訂了一間叫Made's Homestay的家庭式民宿,一晚Rp220,000(約新台幣440元),含早餐與wifi。這種傳統峇里式院落住宅其實是最划算的住法——主屋是屋主一家人住的,客房則環繞著中庭的小神龕(Sanggah)而建,清晨六點多我就會被院子裡窸窸窣窣的腳步聲吵醒,那是民宿主人的媽媽Wayan阿姨,端著椰葉編成的小盤子,在每個房門口、車輪下、神龕前放上供品「canang sari」,順手點燃一支線香。早餐通常是香蕉煎餅淋椰糖漿,或黑糯米粥「bubur injin」配椰奶,吃起來甜而不膩,還帶一點薑的辛香尾韻。

整趟十天的花費,我後來攤開手帳本仔細算過:住宿共Rp2,200,000、三餐加咖啡大約Rp950,000(平均一天不到Rp100,000,因為我幾乎都吃當地warung)、租機車十天Rp700,000(一天Rp70,000,不含油錢)、景點門票與活動體驗約Rp650,000、SIM卡與雜項約Rp250,000,總計不到Rp4,750,000,換算新台幣大約9,500元,不含來回機票。想省更多的背包客,其實可以住到一晚Rp150,000以下的青年旅館多人房,但我覺得多花那幾十塊台幣換來一個安靜的院子和會煮早餐的阿姨,非常值得。

租機車這件事有幾個避雷重點:烏布城區周邊的Jalan Raya Andong與往Tegalalang方向的路口,警察會不定期設置臨檢(razia),專門攔查沒有國際駕照的觀光客,開口就是Rp100,000到Rp300,000的「和解金」。我隨身帶著台灣監理站辦的國際駕照證明加中文駕照正本,被攔過一次,員警看完文件後只是提醒我頭燈要開著,就揮手放行。沒帶駕照的背包客朋友就沒這麼幸運,被要求當場繳錢了事。

清晨六點的坎普罕山脊:一次完整的步道體驗

我把Campuhan Ridge Walk排在抵達烏布的第二天清晨,鬧鐘設在五點四十分。走出Made's Homestay時天還沒全亮,巷子裡只有野狗和早起要去市場採買的阿嬤,空氣濕涼帶著露水味。我沿著Jalan Raya Campuhan一路往西走,經過還沒開門的Ibu Oka烤乳豬本店,再走過橫跨Campuhan河的小橋——橋下就是供奉濕婆與雪山女神的Pura Gunung Lebah寺廟,晨霧從河谷裡緩緩升起,廟鐘聲混著蟲鳴,那個畫面我到現在閉眼還能看見。

步道入口就在Karsa Spa招牌旁邊的小坡道上,完全免費,沒有售票亭也沒有柵欄。單程大約2公里,走起來平緩好走,是一條沿著山脊修剪整齊的水泥步道,兩側是及腰的象草與少數幾棵香蕉樹,視野完全開闊。清晨六點半左右太陽剛翻過山頭,金黃色的光線斜斜打在草坡上,遠遠可以看到阿貢火山(Gunung Agung)的三角形剪影浮在雲層上方——那是峇里島人心中的聖山,傳說眾神就住在山頂。走到步道中段時遇到一位牽著兩頭牛去吃草的當地大叔,他用簡單的英文跟我說「早安,慢慢走,不趕時間」,那句話莫名讓我把腳步放慢了下來。

單程走完大約45分鐘,我在步道盡頭的Karsa Kafe坐下來,點了一顆現削冰鎮椰子Rp25,000,老闆用開山刀俐落地削開椰殼插上吸管遞給我,椰子水清甜微鹹,一口氣灌下半顆才捨得停。回程原路折返,加上拍照發呆的時間,來回總共花了將近兩個半小時。想提醒的是:整條步道完全沒有遮蔭,建議一定要趕在早上八點半前完成,晚一點太陽會毒辣到讓人中暑;若前一晚下過雨,步道兩側的泥土小徑會濕滑,建議穿抓地力好的運動鞋而非人字拖。

Ibu Oka的烤乳豬飯與巷口那攤Nasi Campur:味覺記憶

中午十一點半,我特地避開觀光團的用餐尖峰,走進烏布皇宮附近的Ibu Oka 3(創始店隔壁的分店,座位比較多也比較不用排隊)。一份烤乳豬飯Babi Guling大約Rp60,000,店員直接在攤位前現切,豬皮烤得焦脆帶蜂窩狀氣孔,咬下去先是清脆的「喀」一聲,接著是溫熱多汁的豬肉纖維,混著薑黃飯、切碎的豬皮脆片、血腸(lawar)和一大匙用了十幾種辛香料磨成的香料醬,辣度不高但香氣層層堆疊,吃到後半段額頭已經開始冒汗。用餐環境很家常,塑膠椅、鐵皮屋頂,隔壁桌是三個當地建築工人,吃完飯直接用手抓最後幾粒飯,配著一小杯冰茶(es teh)豪邁地喝完。

晚餐我常去Jalan Karna巷口一間沒有招牌、只掛著手寫「Nasi Campur」木牌的路邊攤,老闆娘是一位皮膚曬得黝黑、笑起來眼睛瞇成一條線的阿姨。一份Rp22,000,白飯上鋪滿炸天貝(tempe)、涼拌青木瓜、水煮空心菜、一小塊咖哩雞,最後淋上招牌生辣椒醬「sambal matah」——用檸檬葉、香茅、紅蔥頭和小米辣現拌而成,辣得直接又帶著檸檬葉的清香,吃第一口整個人精神都來了。空氣裡飄著隔壁攤丁香菸(kretek)燃燒時特有的甜香,混著廟裡飄來的線香味,是我對烏布夜晚最深刻的氣味記憶。

如果吃膩了重口味,烏布也有給數位遊牧民族與瑜伽客的另一面——像是隱身在稻田邊的Warung Sopa,一碗火龍果果昔碗(smoothie bowl)加穀物脆片和新鮮芒果,大概Rp45,000,坐在戶外座位能直接看到農夫在田裡插秧,兩種截然不同的用餐體驗,卻同樣是烏布日常的一部分。

在烏布吃飯,價格從一杯咖啡到一頓皇室等級的烤乳豬,落差可以是十倍,但誠意從來沒有打折。

供品、鐘聲與月圓祭典:烏布人的日常信仰

住在Made's Homestay的第三個晚上,我跟屋主的兒子Made(對,峇里島很多男生真的都叫Made,因為這是排行老二的傳統名字)坐在院子裡喝薑黃咖啡聊天,他隨手拿起一片剛編好的椰葉小盤跟我解釋:canang sari裡的每一種顏色都對應一個方位與神祇,白色代表濕婆,紅色代表梵天,黃色代表毗濕奴,中間會放米粒、花瓣、餅乾碎片,甚至一小片菸草,「這是給神明的,也是給遊蕩的靈的,讓祂們吃飽了就別來打擾我們」,他笑著說。峇里島人每天早晚都要準備供品,一天可能要做二十幾份,分別放在店門口、車輪下、神龕、廚房灶台旁,那種近乎日常呼吸般的儀式感,是我在其他東南亞地方沒見過的。

我剛好遇上農曆滿月「Purnama」那晚,傍晚六點多,街上突然湧出大批身穿白色紗籠、頭上盤著白布巾的居民,手裡端著疊得像小山一樣高的水果供品,成群結隊走向Pura Taman Saraswati——那座建在蓮花池邊、以文學女神命名的寺廟。我跟著人群走到廟口,一位廟方志工遞給我一條紗龍布巾要我圍上腰間才准入內,並提醒我不可以踩過供品、不能把腳底對著神龕方向坐。廟埕裡甘美朗樂團敲擊出層層疊疊的金屬音色,一群穿著金色紗籠的女子跳起雷貢舞(Legong),手指纖細地翻轉,眼神隨著節奏左右瞟動,火把的光影打在她們臉上,那個畫面比我看過任何一場舞台表演都震撼。

離開前,幾個實際踩過雷的提醒也想留給後來的背包客:烏布皇宮附近的聖猴森林(Monkey Forest)裡的獼猴真的會搶走遊客的墨鏡、手機甚至拖鞋,我親眼看到一個韓國女生的墨鏡被扯走,最後只能付錢請馴猴人幫忙拿香蕉「贖」回來;租車務必確認是否有基本第三責任險,烏布郊區路面坑洞多、機車自摔的背包客我在青旅就遇到兩個;峇里島現在對外國旅客收取一次性的「觀光稅」Rp150,000(可在抵達前線上申報或機場現場繳納,務必留存電子收據,部分景點會抽查);提款機盡量選擇銀行本身設立在分行內的ATM,路邊獨立機台盜刷案例時有所聞。最後一晚,我坐在Campuhan河邊一間小咖啡館的露台,看著晚霞把整片稻田染成橘紅色,遠處傳來誦經的錄音混著蟬鳴,那一刻突然覺得,烏布教會我的不是什麼「找到自己」的心靈雞湯,而是怎麼把生活的節奏,重新調慢到跟呼吸一樣自然。

ENGLISH

The plane touched down at Ngurah Rai International Airport (DPS) at half past six in the evening, and the first thing that hit me stepping off was a smell I hadn't expected: coconut sunscreen, exhaust fumes, and wet earth all at once. That's still my first memory of Bali. I had a battered 65-liter backpack on my shoulders, five years of travel worn into the straps, and a crumpled visa-on-arrival receipt tucked in my passport. For the next ten days, I wanted to live in Ubud the way locals actually lived, not the way the brochures sold it.

Landing in Bali: Getting From the Airport to Ubud, the Hard Way

I learned fast that Bali's airport has serious restrictions on Grab and Gojek — drivers aren't allowed to pick up passengers directly from the terminal, and you have to walk out to a designated pickup zone. Along that walk, taxi touts kept intercepting me with lines like 'sir, ride-hailing apps are illegal here' and 'it's very far and dangerous outside.' I ignored them and dragged my bag through 35-degree heat for nearly ten minutes, squeezing through a gap in the perimeter fence, before finally meeting my Grab motorbike at the pin I'd dropped on the map. Except the bike couldn't fit my backpack, so the driver told me to switch to a Grab Car — which got auto-cancelled three times because the app kept detecting I was still inside airport grounds.

Eventually I gave up and walked back to the official taxi counter, paying a fixed rate of Rp450,000 (roughly US$29) in cash for a Blue Bird taxi — one of the few taxi companies in Bali with an actual reputation for honest metering and no funny business. The ride should have taken fifty minutes; instead, Jalan By Pass Ngurah Rai around 7pm was a glowing river of brake lights. Motorbike engines, gamelan music drifting out of a roadside temple, and smoke from satay grills all poured in through the cracked window. By the time we reached Jalan Raya Ubud, it was nearly 9pm. I grabbed a Telkomsel tourist SIM at a convenience store — 15GB data plus calls for about Rp150,000 — and the clerk had it installed and working in under five minutes.

In Ubud, time isn't measured by a watch — it's recalibrated by temple bells, roosters, and the honk of passing scooters.

Settling Into Ubud: Where I Slept and What It Actually Cost

I booked a small family-run homestay called Made's Homestay, tucked down a quiet lane in Penestanan, well away from the main strip. It ran Rp220,000 a night (about US$14) including breakfast and wifi. This kind of traditional Balinese compound house is genuinely the best value option — the family lives in the main building, and guest rooms wrap around a central courtyard built around a small family shrine (sanggah). Every morning around six, I'd wake to soft shuffling footsteps outside: Ibu Wayan, the host's mother, carrying a tray of woven palm-leaf offerings, placing canang sari at every doorway, under car wheels, and in front of the shrine, lighting a stick of incense as she went. Breakfast was usually banana pancakes drizzled with palm sugar syrup, or black rice pudding (bubur injin) with coconut milk — sweet but not cloying, with a faint ginger warmth at the finish.

When I finally sat down and did the math after ten days, it looked like this: accommodation totaled Rp2,200,000; food and coffee came to about Rp950,000 (under Rp100,000 a day on average, since I ate almost exclusively at local warungs); a scooter rental for ten days ran Rp700,000 (Rp70,000/day, fuel excluded); entrance fees and activities added up to around Rp650,000; and SIM card plus miscellaneous expenses were roughly Rp250,000. Grand total: under Rp4,750,000, or around US$300, not counting flights. Backpackers wanting to cut costs further could easily find dorm beds under Rp150,000 a night, but I'll say this — the extra few dollars for a quiet courtyard and a woman who makes you breakfast every morning was worth every rupiah.

A word of caution on scooters: around Jalan Raya Andong and the junctions heading toward Tegalalang, police run occasional spot checks (razia) specifically targeting tourists without an International Driving Permit, and the opening demand is usually Rp100,000 to Rp300,000 in 'settlement fees.' I carried my IDP alongside my original license and got stopped once — the officer glanced at the paperwork, reminded me to keep my headlight on, and waved me through. A few backpackers I met at the hostel weren't carrying one and had to pay on the spot.

Six A.M. on Campuhan Ridge: A Full Walkthrough

I scheduled the Campuhan Ridge Walk for my second morning in Ubud, alarm set for 5:40am. The lane outside Made's Homestay was still dark, empty except for stray dogs and an elderly woman heading to market. The air was cool and damp, thick with the smell of dew on grass. I walked west along Jalan Raya Campuhan, past the still-shuttered original Ibu Oka stall, and crossed the small bridge over the Campuhan River — beneath it sits Pura Gunung Lebah, a temple dedicated to Shiva and his consort. Morning mist was rising slowly off the riverbed, temple bells mixing with insect noise. I can still see it clearly with my eyes closed.

The trailhead sits on a small slope right beside the Karsa Spa sign — completely free, no ticket booth, no gate. The path runs about 2km one way along a neatly paved ridge, flanked by waist-high elephant grass and the occasional banana tree, with completely open views on both sides. Around 6:30am the sun cleared the hills and threw gold light sideways across the grass; far in the distance I could make out the triangular silhouette of Gunung Agung floating above the clouds — the volcano Balinese Hindus consider the seat of the gods. Halfway along, I passed a farmer leading two cows to graze, who said in simple English, 'good morning, walk slow, no hurry' — a sentence that somehow made me actually slow down.

The one-way walk took about 45 minutes. At the far end, I sat down at Karsa Kafe and ordered a chilled young coconut for Rp25,000; the owner hacked it open with a machete and handed it over with a straw already in place — the water was sweet with a faint salinity, and I drank half of it in one go before I could stop myself. The return trip, plus time spent standing around taking photos, brought the round trip to almost two and a half hours. Worth noting: there's zero shade along the entire ridge, so finish before 8:30am or the heat becomes brutal; and if it rained the night before, the dirt shoulders of the path get slick, so proper trainers beat flip-flops here.

Ibu Oka's Suckling Pig and the Nameless Nasi Campur Stall

At 11:30am, deliberately timed to dodge the tour-bus rush, I walked into Ibu Oka 3 near Ubud Palace — a sister branch of the original, with more seating and shorter lines. A plate of babi guling (roast suckling pig) ran about Rp60,000, carved to order right at the counter. The skin was blistered and honeycombed, cracking with an audible snap on the first bite before giving way to warm, juicy meat, all piled over turmeric rice with crispy shredded skin, a spoonful of lawar (mixed meat and vegetable salad), and a thick spice paste built from more than a dozen ground aromatics. It wasn't overtly spicy, but the flavor built in layers until I was sweating by the second half of the plate. The setting was plastic chairs and a tin roof; the table next to me held three local construction workers eating with their bare hands, washing it all down with iced tea (es teh) in enormous gulps.

For dinner, I kept returning to an unnamed roadside stall on Jalan Karna, marked only by a hand-painted wooden sign reading 'Nasi Campur.' The woman running it had deeply tanned skin and a smile that closed her eyes into two lines. A plate cost Rp22,000: rice topped with fried tempeh, shredded green papaya salad, boiled water spinach, a small piece of curried chicken, all finished with a spoonful of sambal matah — a raw relish of lime leaf, lemongrass, shallots, and bird's eye chili, tossed together fresh. It hit hard and bright, with the citrus perfume of lime leaf cutting through the heat, and the first bite genuinely woke me up. The air around the stall carried the sweet smoke of clove cigarettes (kretek) from the next table, mingling with incense drifting over from a nearby temple — that combination is, for me, the exact smell of Ubud at night.

For anyone tired of heavier flavors, Ubud also caters to its digital-nomad and yoga crowd. Warung Sopa, tucked beside rice paddies, does a dragon fruit smoothie bowl with granola and fresh mango for about Rp45,000; sitting outside, you can watch farmers transplanting rice seedlings while you eat. Two completely different meals, both equally part of Ubud's daily rhythm.

A meal in Ubud can swing tenfold in price, from a single coffee to a royal suckling-pig feast — but the care behind it never gets cheaper.

Offerings, Bells, and the Full Moon: How Ubud Practices Its Faith

On my third night at Made's Homestay, I sat in the courtyard drinking turmeric coffee with the owner's son, also named Made — a reminder that a huge number of Balinese men share this name, since it traditionally denotes the second-born child. He picked up a freshly woven canang sari and walked me through it: each color inside corresponds to a direction and a deity — white for Shiva, red for Brahma, yellow for Vishnu — with rice grains, flower petals, biscuit crumbs, even a shred of tobacco placed at the center. 'This is for the gods, and for the wandering spirits too,' he said, laughing. 'Feed them, and they leave us alone.' Balinese households prepare these offerings morning and evening, sometimes more than twenty a day, set outside shop doors, under car tires, at shrines, beside kitchen stoves — a ritual so woven into daily breathing that I hadn't seen anything quite like it anywhere else in Southeast Asia.

I happened to be there for Purnama, the full moon. Around 6pm, the streets suddenly filled with residents dressed in white sarongs and white headcloths, carrying towering fruit offerings stacked almost a meter high, walking in loose processions toward Pura Taman Saraswati — the temple built beside a lotus pond and named for the goddess of literature. I followed the crowd to the entrance, where a temple volunteer handed me a sarong sash and tied it around my waist before letting me in, reminding me not to step over any offerings and not to sit with my feet pointing toward the shrine. Inside, a gamelan ensemble hammered out layered metallic tones while a group of women in gold sarongs performed the Legong dance, fingers curling with impossible precision, eyes flicking side to side on the beat, torchlight flickering across their faces. It was more striking than any staged performance I've ever paid to see.

Before I left, a few hard-earned warnings worth passing on: the macaques in the Sacred Monkey Forest near Ubud Palace really will snatch sunglasses, phones, even sandals — I watched a Korean traveler have her sunglasses ripped off her face, only to pay a monkey handler with bananas to negotiate them back. Make sure any scooter rental includes basic third-party insurance; the backroads around Ubud are full of potholes, and I met two separate backpackers at my hostel who'd already crashed. Bali now charges foreign visitors a one-time Rp150,000 tourism levy, payable online before arrival or on the spot at the airport — keep the digital receipt, since some attractions spot-check it. And stick to ATMs physically attached to a bank branch; standalone street machines have a documented history of card skimming here. On my last night, I sat on the terrace of a small café by the Campuhan River, watching the sunset burn the rice paddies orange, a recorded chant drifting somewhere in the distance under the cicadas. What Ubud taught me wasn't some tidy 'find yourself' cliché — it was simply how to slow my own rhythm back down to something as unhurried as breathing.
