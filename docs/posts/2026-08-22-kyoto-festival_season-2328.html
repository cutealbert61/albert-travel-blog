中文

七月十四日,傍晚六點,我拖著行李箱走出京都車站中央口,一股濕熱的空氣像布幕般直接罩下來。手機裡存著半年前訂的旅館地址——四条烏丸附近一間町家改建的民宿,老闆娘在電話裡只提醒我一句話:「這幾天路上會很多人,行李箱記得收好。」我後來才明白,她說的不是普通的觀光人潮,而是祇園祭。

會選在盛夏造訪京都,說實話違背了所有旅遊書的建議。八月平均濕度動輒八成,午後溫度輕鬆衝破三十四度,連京都在地人都會苦笑著說「這種天氣觀光客才會出門」。但我早就聽在京都念書的朋友說過一句話——「沒看過鉾町亮起提燈的京都人,等於沒真正住過京都。」這句話在我心裡放了三年,這次終於決定,不管熱不熱,都要來一趟。

七月的京都,一場與神轎的約定

放下行李已是晚上七點,我沿著烏丸通往北走,還沒轉進小巷,遠遠就聞到烤鰻魚混著醬油焦香的氣味,從車站地下街一路飄到地面。蟬鳴在這個時間點特別密集,一種叫做「日暮蟬」的蟬種,叫聲比白天的油蟬更清亮、更悠長,像是替這座城市的黃昏打拍子。我站在四条通口,看著路面電車般緩慢的車潮,才第一次真切感受到,這座城市在七月中旬,呼吸的節奏跟平常完全不同。

宵山:鉾町提燈亮起的那個傍晚

七月十五日,也就是祇園祭「宵山」的第二天,我特意提早在下午四點出門,想避開晚間最擁擠的人潮,先繞去看看白天的鉾町。三十幾座山鉾——木造的巨大祭典花車,高的甚至超過二十五公尺——已經在四条烏丸周邊的巷弄裡搭建完成,長刀鉾、函谷鉾、月鉾一座座沿街矗立,木頭與麻繩交錯捆綁的結構,完全沒有使用一根釘子,這是我站在長刀鉾正下方抬頭看才注意到的細節。

到了晚上七點半,天色終於暗下來,家家戶戶掛出的紅色提燈同時亮起,整條新町通瞬間變成一條會呼吸的光廊。鉾上樂師開始演奏「祇園囃子」,銅鑼、太鼓與能管交織出一種既緩慢又催促的旋律,老京都人稱它「コンコンチキチン」,是模擬樂器聲響的擬聲詞,我聽了兩三次就跟著哼了起來。攤販賣著冰涼的甘酒,一杯四百日圓,微微發酵的米香在舌尖化開,配著悶熱的晚風,意外地順口。

提燈亮起的那一刻,整條巷子忽然安靜了半拍,彷彿連蟬鳴都在等待樂聲響起。

我在函谷鉾前買了一支「粽」——後來才知道這不是拿來吃的,而是掛在自家門口驅邪保平安用的護符,一支要價一千日圓,比想像中貴,但看著攤位前排隊的在地婆婆媽媽,一買就是好幾支要分送給鄰居,我才明白這筆錢買的不是紀念品,而是一整年的心安。

清晨五點半,山鉾巡行的第一排

七月十七日是整個祇園祭的高潮——山鉾巡行。我前一晚特地查過,付費觀覽席早在五月就已經在票務網站售罄,一席大約四千一百日圓,附椅子與遮陽傘,但對像我這種臨時起意的旅人來說,唯一的選擇就是清晨去搶免費站位。

鬧鐘設在四點五十分,天還沒亮透,我從民宿走到四条河原町路口,大約十五分鐘腳程。凌晨五點的京都安靜得不可思議,前一晚被人潮擠得水洩不通的巷子,此刻只剩灑水車經過的水聲。抵達路口時已經有零星的人在拉起的封鎖線後方鋪野餐墊,我在河原町通靠近高島屋百貨的路段找到一處視野不錯的位置,距離封鎖線第一排,靠著護欄站定,那時是五點四十分。

接下來的三個多小時,說實話並不輕鬆。太陽七點左右就毫不留情地曬進街道,我全程只能靠一把小扇子跟從超商買來的一瓶一百二十日圓麥茶撐著。九點整,遊行正式開始,長刀鉾率先出場,鉾上坐著一名真人扮演的「稚兒」,是唯一被允許登上鉾車的孩童,象徵神明的使者。最讓全場屏息的,是每座鉾行經四条河原町路口時必須完成的「辻迴し」——巨大的木輪無法轉向,壯漢們得先在地面鋪上削薄的竹片,潑水降低摩擦力,再用繩索合力拉動,喊著號子把整座重達十幾噸的鉾硬生生轉九十度。那一刻圍觀群眾的歡呼聲跟太鼓聲疊在一起,我才真正理解為什麼有人願意頂著大太陽站三個多小時。

巡行全程約兩小時,我大概在十一點半左右,雙腿已經痠得發抖時才慢慢退場。提醒一下,沿線流動廁所數量有限,排隊動輒二十分鐘以上,最好提前在便利商店解決。

錦市場尾端的一碗鱧魚涼麵

遊行結束後飢腸轆轆,我沿著三条通走了約十分鐘,鑽進一間叫做「権太呂」的老蕎麥麵店。店內是暗色木頭裝潢,老闆娘穿著藏青色浴衣,櫃檯上擺著一整排手打蕎麥麵的工具。排隊等了大概二十分鐘,終於在正午時分坐進店裡,點了一份鱧魚涼麵套餐,一千九百八十日圓。

京都人稱鱧魚為「祭りの魚」,因為牠的產季恰好與祇園祭重疊。鱧魚全身佈滿細小魚骨,職人得用一種叫做「骨切り」的刀工,在魚肉上每一公分切三十六刀卻不切斷魚皮,吃起來才不會刺喉。端上桌的涼麵是冰鎮過的手打蕎麥麵,鱧魚以梅子醬汁涼拌,魚肉燙熟後外層微微捲曲,咬下去先是清爽的酸梅香氣,接著是魚肉本身近乎透明的細膩口感,搭配旁邊一小碟切得極薄的酢橘,擠一點汁進沾麵醬裡,整碗麵的味道瞬間更立體。坐在我旁邊的老先生一邊喝著冰麥茶一邊跟老闆娘聊天,說今年鱧魚特別肥美,是「祭りの贈り物」——祭典送的禮物。

八坂神社前,老闆娘教我的禁忌

下午三點多,我走到八坂神社,在西樓門旁的小攤位前停下腳步。攤位是山鉾町其中一個「保存會」設的臨時攤,賣的正是前一晚我在函谷鉾買的那種粽狀護符。顧攤的婆婆姓田中,說自己家裡三代都在做這門生意,每年只做這十天。

她跟我說了一個傳說:牛頭天王旅行時投宿在貧窮的蘇民將來家,對方雖窮卻誠心款待,牛頭天王便賜給他茅草編的護符,叮囑後代子孫只要在門口掛上這個標記,就能躲過瘟疫。這也是為什麼粽子絕對不能拆開吃,而是要完整掛在玄關,直到隔年祇園祭期間才拿回神社或保存會焚燒,換上新的一支。

田中婆婆笑著說:「這不是紀念品,是我們京都人一整年的護身符。」

她接著壓低聲音跟我分享一個更少被寫進旅遊書的禁忌——整個七月,傳統的京都人家會刻意不吃小黃瓜。原因是小黃瓜切面的紋路,跟八坂神社的家紋「木瓜紋」十分相似,為了表達對神明的敬意,不少老一輩京都人整個月都避開這道再平常不過的夏季蔬菜。田中婆婆說她自己也遵守這個習慣,直到祭典結束那天,才會在家裡煮一碗涼拌小黃瓜,算是慶祝夏天真正到來。這種藏在日常飲食裡的細膩心思,是我在任何一本旅遊指南裡都沒讀過的京都。

尾聲:如果你想避開七月的溽暑

晚上八點,我坐在鴨川旁的納涼床邊,看著川床餐廳一盞盞燈亮起,河面吹來的風總算帶了點涼意。老實說,七月中旬的京都又濕又熱,行程排得再輕鬆,回旅館後也總是全身濕透。但這趟旅行讓我明白,有些景色只存在於特定的十天,錯過就要再等一年。

如果你跟我一樣好奇京都的節慶,卻沒辦法忍受盛夏濕熱,其實還有幾個時間點值得列入口袋名單:二月中旬到三月初,北野天滿宮的梅花盛開,遊客比賞櫻季少了七八成,反而更能靜靜賞花;六月上旬,三室戶寺兩萬株繡球花齊放,適合安排一趟避開人潮的雨中散步;十一月中旬則是永觀堂與東福寺的楓紅高峰期,建議清晨開門前就到,才能拍到沒有人潮的紅葉隧道。而如果你跟我一樣,願意為了那十天提燈與囃子聲忍受汗流浹背,七月中旬的祇園祭,絕對值得。

離開京都那天早上,我又經過四条烏丸路口,鉾車早已拆卸得一乾二淨,彷彿什麼都沒發生過。但那陣「コンコンチキチン」的樂聲,還有田中婆婆說話時的語氣,一路跟著我回到機場。

ENGLISH

It was six in the evening on July fourteenth when I dragged my suitcase out of Kyoto Station's central exit, and the humid air fell over me like a wet curtain. I had an address saved on my phone for a machiya guesthouse near Shijo-Karasuma, booked six months earlier. On the phone, the owner had only warned me once: 'The streets will be very crowded these days, keep an eye on your bags.' It took me a full day to understand she wasn't talking about ordinary tourist crowds. She meant Gion Matsuri.

Choosing to visit Kyoto at the height of summer breaks every rule in the guidebooks. Average humidity in July and August routinely hits eighty percent, afternoon temperatures push past thirty-four degrees, and even locals joke that only tourists go outside in weather like this. But a friend who studied in Kyoto once told me something that stuck for three years: 'You haven't really lived in Kyoto until you've seen the lanterns light up in the float district.' This time, heat or no heat, I decided to go.

A Rendezvous with the Floats

By the time I dropped my bags it was already seven. Walking north along Karasuma-dori, before I'd even turned into the side streets, I caught the smell of grilled eel and caramelized soy sauce drifting up from the underground shopping arcade beneath the station. The cicadas were unusually loud at that hour — a species called higurashi, whose call is clearer and more drawn-out than the daytime abura-zemi, as if setting the tempo for the city's dusk. Standing at the Shijo intersection, watching the slow crawl of traffic, I felt for the first time that this city breathes on a completely different rhythm in mid-July.

Yoiyama: The Evening the Lanterns Came On

July fifteenth was the second night of Yoiyama, the festival's pre-parade evening. I deliberately left the guesthouse at four in the afternoon, hoping to see the float district before the crowds thickened. More than thirty yamahoko — towering wooden festival floats, some over twenty-five meters tall — had already been assembled in the lanes around Shijo-Karasuma. Naginata Hoko, Kanko Hoko, Tsuki Hoko stood one after another along the streets. Standing directly beneath Naginata Hoko and looking up, I noticed the entire wooden structure was lashed together with rope, without a single nail — a detail no photograph had prepared me for.

By seven-thirty the sky finally darkened, and every household's red paper lantern lit up at once. The whole of Shinmachi-dori turned instantly into a corridor of breathing light. Musicians on the floats began playing Gion-bayashi — gongs, drums, and the reedy noh-kan flute weaving together into a melody that felt both unhurried and urgent. Old Kyoto residents call it 'kon-kon-chiki-chin,' an onomatopoeia for the instruments themselves, and after hearing it two or three times I caught myself humming along. Vendors sold chilled amazake for four hundred yen a cup, the faintly fermented rice sweetness dissolving on my tongue against the muggy night air, unexpectedly refreshing.

The instant the lanterns lit up, the whole lane seemed to pause for half a beat, as if even the cicadas were waiting for the music to begin.

In front of Kanko Hoko I bought what looked like a rice dumpling wrapped in bamboo leaves — a chimaki. I later learned it isn't meant to be eaten at all; it's a talisman hung by the front door to ward off illness for the coming year, and it cost a surprising thousand yen. Watching the local grandmothers ahead of me in line buy several at a time to give to neighbors, I understood that what they were paying for wasn't a souvenir — it was a year's worth of peace of mind.

Five-Thirty in the Morning: Front Row for the Grand Procession

July seventeenth is the climax of the entire festival — the Yamahoko Junko, the grand procession of floats. I'd checked the night before: paid viewing seats, roughly forty-one hundred yen each with a chair and a sunshade, had sold out online back in May. For a spontaneous traveler like me, the only option left was to stake out a free standing spot at dawn.

My alarm went off at four-fifty, well before sunrise. I walked from the guesthouse to the Shijo-Kawaramachi intersection, about fifteen minutes on foot. Kyoto at five in the morning was eerily quiet — the same alleys that had been shoulder-to-shoulder with people the night before now held nothing but the sound of a street-cleaning truck passing by. A handful of people had already laid out picnic mats behind the rope barriers. I found a decent vantage point on Kawaramachi-dori near the Takashimaya department store, right against the front barrier, and settled in at five-forty.

The next three-plus hours were, frankly, brutal. By seven the sun was already merciless, and I survived on a small folding fan and a hundred-and-twenty-yen bottle of cold barley tea from a convenience store. At nine sharp, the procession began. Naginata Hoko led the way, carrying a real child chosen to play the chigo, the only human allowed to ride atop a float, symbolizing a messenger of the gods. The moment that made the whole crowd hold its breath was the tsuji-mawashi — the ninety-degree turn each float must execute at the Shijo-Kawaramachi crossing. Since the massive wooden wheels can't be steered, teams of men lay thin strips of split bamboo on the road, pour water over them to reduce friction, and haul the multi-ton float around by brute force and rope, chanting in rhythm. The crowd's roar merged with the drumbeats at that exact instant, and only then did I understand why anyone would stand in the blazing sun for three hours straight.

The full procession took about two hours; I finally gave up my spot around eleven-thirty, legs trembling from standing so long. One practical note: portable toilets along the route are limited, with waits easily exceeding twenty minutes — better to take care of that at a convenience store beforehand.

A Bowl of Chilled Hamo Noodles Near Nishiki Market

Starving after the parade, I walked about ten minutes along Sanjo-dori and ducked into an old soba shop called Gontaro. Inside, dark wood paneling lined the walls, the owner wore an indigo yukata, and a row of hand-cut soba tools sat behind the counter. After a twenty-minute wait, I was finally seated around noon and ordered the chilled hamo (pike conger eel) soba set for nineteen hundred and eighty yen.

Locals call hamo the 'festival fish,' because its season lines up exactly with Gion Matsuri. The fish is riddled with fine bones, so chefs use a technique called hone-kiri, making roughly thirty-six cuts per centimeter of flesh without slicing through the skin, so it doesn't catch in your throat. What arrived was ice-cold, hand-cut buckwheat noodles topped with hamo dressed in a plum sauce, the parboiled flesh curling slightly at the edges. The first bite was bright with sour plum, followed by the almost translucent, delicate texture of the fish itself. A small dish of thinly sliced sudachi citrus sat beside it — a squeeze into the dipping sauce made the whole bowl come alive. The elderly man beside me, sipping iced barley tea and chatting with the owner, remarked that the hamo was especially fat this year — a gift from the festival, he called it.

What the Auntie Outside Yasaka Shrine Taught Me

Around three in the afternoon I walked to Yasaka Shrine and stopped at a small stall beside the west gate. It was a temporary booth run by one of the neighborhood preservation societies, selling the same chimaki charms I'd bought the night before near Kanko Hoko. The woman running it, whose family name was Tanaka, told me her family had run this stall for three generations, open only these ten days a year.

She told me the legend behind it: the god Gozu Tenno, traveling in disguise, was refused lodging by a wealthy man but welcomed warmly by a poor man named Somin Shorai. In gratitude, the god gave Somin's family a woven straw talisman and promised that as long as their descendants hung it at their door, they would be spared from plague. That's why the chimaki must never be opened or eaten — it stays whole at the entrance until the following year's festival, when it's returned to the shrine or the preservation society to be ritually burned and replaced.

'This isn't a souvenir,' Tanaka-san laughed. 'It's our whole year's protection.'

She then lowered her voice and shared a custom rarely mentioned in guidebooks: throughout July, traditional Kyoto households deliberately avoid eating cucumber. The reason is that a cucumber's cross-section closely resembles the mokko crest of Yasaka Shrine, and out of respect for the deity, many older Kyoto families skip this most ordinary of summer vegetables for the entire month. Tanaka-san said she still keeps the custom herself, only preparing a dish of chilled cucumber salad once the festival ends, as a small celebration that summer has truly arrived. It's this kind of quiet thoughtfulness hidden inside everyday eating habits that no guidebook had ever shown me.

Epilogue: If You'd Rather Skip July's Heat

At eight that evening I sat by the Kamogawa River beside the kawadoko dining platforms, watching lanterns come on one by one along the water as a faint breeze finally brought some relief. Honestly, mid-July in Kyoto is oppressively hot and humid, and no matter how light I kept my itinerary, I returned to the guesthouse soaked through every single day. But this trip taught me something: certain sights exist only within a specific ten-day window each year, and missing them means waiting an entire year more.

If you're drawn to Kyoto's festivals but can't stomach the summer humidity, there are other windows worth keeping in your back pocket: mid-February to early March, when the plum blossoms bloom at Kitano Tenmangu with seventy to eighty percent fewer crowds than cherry blossom season; early June, when twenty thousand hydrangeas bloom at Mimurotoji, perfect for a quiet rainy-day walk; and mid-November, the peak of autumn color at Eikando and Tofukuji, best visited right at opening time before the crowds arrive for that empty tunnel of red maples. But if, like me, you're willing to sweat through ten days of lanterns and festival music, mid-July's Gion Matsuri is absolutely worth it.

On the morning I left Kyoto, I passed by the Shijo-Karasuma intersection again. The floats had already been completely dismantled, as if nothing had ever happened there. But the sound of kon-kon-chiki-chin, and the way Tanaka-san's voice had softened when she spoke, followed me all the way back to the airport.
