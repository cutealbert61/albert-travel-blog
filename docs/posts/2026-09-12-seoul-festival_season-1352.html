中文

飛機降落仁川機場的時候是清晨六點四十分,機艙的遮光板一打開,外頭天空還帶著一點深藍,底下的稻田卻已經染上焦糖色。這是我第三次來首爾,卻是第一次特地挑十一月初這個時間點——朋友告訴我,首爾的秋天短得像一張只寄一次的明信片,銀杏黃、楓葉紅跟清溪川的燈籠會在同一週疊在一起,錯過就要再等一年。

清晨落地:仁川機場到北村的第一道秋光

我搭上AREX機場快線直達首爾站,車程四十三分鐘,票價九千五百韓元。車廂裡很安靜,只有行李輪子偶爾碰到地板的聲音,坐我對面的阿姨捧著一杯即溶咖啡打盹。到首爾站轉三號線到安國站,出三號出口,第一口呼吸到的空氣有點涼,大概攝氏十度上下,混著烤栗子攤還沒開張前殘留的焦糖味。

我訂的韓屋民宿藏在桂洞路巷子裡,叫「北村오래된집」,老闆娘出來開門時穿著厚棉襖,笑著用不太流利的中文說:「你來得剛好,銀杏這兩天才開始黃。」院子裡有一棵老柿子樹,橘紅色的柿子還掛在枝頭沒被摘,她說要留給喜鵲吃,這是首爾老一輩的習慣,叫까치밥,意思是留一點食物給喜鵲,象徵人跟自然分享收成。這句話我後來一路帶著走,總覺得首爾的秋天不是只屬於觀光客的風景,而是這座城市跟自己季節性的約定。

景福宮守門將換防:韓服、銀杏與一場關於時間的儀式

放下行李我沒有睡,直接沿著桂洞길走去景福宮,大概十五分鐘腳程。這條路兩旁都是低矮的韓屋屋簷,九點多的巷子還很安靜,只有幾間韓服店剛捲起鐵門,老闆娘蹲在門口用抹布擦玻璃櫥窗。我在한복사랑租了一套淡藕色韓服,四小時方案兩萬韓元,含頭飾跟一雙布鞋,店員手腳很快,幫我把裙頭綁得緊緊的,說這樣走路才不會被風掀起來。

穿著韓服走近光化門,我才想起這是我第一次因為服裝而免費入宮——景福宮平日票價三千韓元,但穿韓服的人可以直接刷過售票口,守票的大叔看我一眼,點頭讓我進去,連問都沒問。廣場上已經聚了不少人,十點的守門將換防儀式準時開始,鼓聲先響,接著是穿著朝鮮時代服飾的守衛隊伍踏著整齊步伐從光化門兩側走出來,他們手上的長槍跟旗幟在風裡輕輕晃,銀杏葉不時從旁邊的樹上飄下來,落在其中一位守衛的肩甲上,他動都沒動,像是排練好的畫面,卻又那麼自然。

換防儀式每天在光化門前上演三場,分別是上午十點、下午一點跟下午三點,週二公休,整場大概持續二十分鐘,如果想拍到清楚的隊伍正面,建議提早二十分鐘到,站在光化門正對面斑馬線附近卡位,那裡人潮最密集但視野最好。我後來又繞進宮殿內部,勤政殿前的空地鋪滿碎石,踩上去會發出沙沙聲響,據說是古代防止刺客潛入時不發出腳步聲而特意設計的,踩久了腳底微微發麻,卻覺得歷史突然變得很具體。整趟從入口走到景福宮後方的香遠亭,大概花了兩個半小時,中間坐在池邊木椅上休息了一會兒,水面飄著幾片黃葉,遠處的北岳山已經有一半染成金黃色。

首爾的秋天像一封只寄一次的信,你若錯過這十來天,就要等上一整年。

廣藏市場:빈대떡的油香與마약김밥的辣芥末

中午一點多,我搭地鐵一號線到鐘路五街站,出八號出口就是廣藏市場。還沒走進市場,遠遠就聞到一股混雜著芝麻油、辣椒醬跟炭火的氣味,攤販的塑膠棚頂被油煙燻得有點發黃,喊價聲跟長柄鐵鏟敲擊鐵板的聲音疊在一起,像是某種市場專屬的打擊樂。

我在순희네빈대떡前面站定,阿姨動作俐落地把綠豆磨成的糊倒進鐵板,邊緣煎得焦脆,中間鋪滿豆芽菜跟豬肉絲,起鍋前還壓了一下讓油瀝乾。一份五千韓元,端上來邊角還在滋滋作響,咬下去外層像脆餅乾,裡面卻濕潤帶著豆香,沾一點醬油醋汁,酸味把油膩感切開。旁邊坐著一位常客大叔,他說他每週三固定來吃一次,已經吃了二十年,「以前跟我爸來的時候是一千韓元一份」,他笑著補了一句。

吃完빈대떡,我又往市場深處走,找到那家傳說中的마약김밥——迷你紫菜包飯攤,老闆娘手速快到我來不及看清楚她怎麼捲的,一份大概十個小卷,只包了醃蘿蔔跟紅蘿蔔絲,配一碟黃芥末醬油沾醬,三千韓元。米飯偏軟帶著微微芝麻油香,一口一個,沾了芥末後鼻腔突然一陣衝辣,逼得我眼眶泛淚,卻忍不住又拿了下一個。攤位旁擠著五、六張紅色塑膠矮凳,大家肩並肩坐著,一位阿嬤看我拍照拍得起勁,用韓語跟我說了一串話,旁邊會英文的年輕人幫忙翻譯:「她說外國人都喜歡拍照,但要記得趁熱吃,冷掉就不好吃了。」我趕緊把手機收起來,乖乖把剩下的吃完,配著一碗溫熱的馬格利酒,微酸微甜,帶著米麴發酵特有的氣味,喝下去胃裡暖暖的,把剛剛芥末衝出來的眼淚也一起壓下去。

清溪川서울빛초롱축제:燈籠裡的秋夕記憶

晚上六點多,天色完全暗下來,我沿著清溪川散步到清溪廣場,遠遠就看到水面映著一整排燈籠的倒影。首爾빛초롱축제每年約在十月底到十一月中舉行,從清溪廣場一路延伸到水標橋,全長超過一公里,免費入場,晚上六點到十一點點燈。今年的主題燈籠有巨大青龍、身穿傳統喜鵲圖案韓服的仕女、還有一組還原朝鮮世宗大王讀書場景的燈組,燈光透過絲綢燈罩暈染開來,河面被染成一片流動的橘紅與靛藍。

我站在一座燈籠前拍照時,身旁一位帶著孫子的奶奶主動跟我聊起天。她說她小時候的추석(秋夕,韓國中秋節)才是真正屬於孩子的節慶——那時全家會提前兩三天回鄉下老家,女人們聚在一起做송편,一種包著芝麻或豆沙、蒸過帶松針香氣的米糕,小孩負責把松針一根根鋪進蒸籠裡。她說做송편的手法會被上一代長輩檢查,捏得漂亮以後就會生漂亮的孩子,是老一輩流傳下來的說法,現在年輕人多半一笑置之,但她還是每年堅持照做。她也提到秋夕當天清晨要進行차례祭祖儀式,供桌上的食物擺放有嚴格順序,紅色水果放東邊、白色放西邊,已婚的女兒不能在娘家過夜守歲,這是首爾許多家庭至今仍遵守的老規矩,雖然年輕一輩已經開始鬆動這些界線。她笑著說,像清溪川這樣的燈會是首爾市政府後來才辦的活動,原本為了紀念過去清溪川加蓋又重新開放的歷史,但漸漸也變成一種讓城市裡的人在忙碌之餘重新聚在一起看光、聊天、想起小時候提燈籠遊街的秋夕記憶。

當燈籠的光影落在清溪川的水面上,我忽然明白,這座城市把回憶也一併點亮了。

我沿著河岸慢慢走到水標橋附近,河堤兩側坐滿情侶跟家庭,有小孩舉著手比出燈籠的形狀跟父母拍照,空氣裡飄著烤魷魚跟糖炒栗子的味道,混著河水微微的濕氣。走完全程大約花了一個半小時,人潮最擁擠的路段集中在清溪廣場到廣通橋之間,如果想避開人潮,建議晚間九點過後再去,燈還亮著,人已經散去大半。

駱山公園的日出:道別首爾的秋天

離開首爾前的最後一個早晨,我五點半就醒了,摸黑走到梨花洞壁畫村的階梯,一路爬向駱山公園。石階濕濕的,踩上去有點滑,路燈昏黃,只有零星幾個當地人跟我一樣往上爬,大概是晨跑的路線。爬到城郭邊,天色開始從深藍轉成淺灰,再慢慢透出一點橘粉色,我站在城牆邊往下看,整片北村韓屋的黑瓦屋頂上鋪著一層薄薄的金黃銀杏葉,遠處南山塔的燈還亮著,像是還沒睡醒的城市。

風有點冷,我把外套拉鍊拉到最上面,手插在口袋裡,聽著遠處傳來早起攤販搬動推車的金屬聲響。太陽升起的那一刻,光線先落在城牆的石縫上,再一路滑向山腳下的韓屋群,整座城市像是被輕輕鍍上一層暖色。我想起老闆娘說的까치밥,那顆留給喜鵲的柿子,還有清溪川那位奶奶說的송편故事,忽然覺得首爾的秋天從來不是只有銀杏跟楓葉這麼簡單,而是這座城市把一年一度的收成、團聚、還有對過去的記憶,全部濃縮進這短短十幾天裡。

下山後我特地繞去德壽宮石牆路,那條被稱為離別之路的銀杏大道,傳說情侶一起走過這條路會分手,所以韓國年輕人多半結伴而非成雙前來。我一個人走完全程,腳下踩著滿地金黃落葉,發出乾燥的沙沙聲,倒也沒什麼不吉利的感覺,只覺得踏實。如果你也想追上這場只上演十來天的秋光,大概鎖定十月最後一週到十一月第二週之間,提早訂好北村附近的住宿,把景福宮、廣藏市場跟清溪川的行程分散在不同的日子裡走,不要一天塞完,首爾的秋天值得放慢腳步,一口一口地吃、一步一步地走。

ENGLISH

My plane touched down at Incheon at 6:40 in the morning. When the cabin shade lifted, the sky outside was still deep indigo, but the rice paddies below had already turned the color of burnt caramel. This was my third trip to Seoul, but the first time I had deliberately chosen early November, because a friend had warned me that Seoul's autumn is like a postcard that only gets mailed once — the golden ginkgo, the red maples, and the lanterns along Cheonggyecheon all converge in the same narrow week. Miss it, and you wait a full year.

Landing at Dawn: First Autumn Light from Incheon to Bukchon

I took the AREX express train straight to Seoul Station, forty-three minutes for 9,500 won. The carriage was quiet except for the occasional rattle of a suitcase wheel; the woman across from me dozed over a cup of instant coffee. At Seoul Station I switched to Line 3 and got off at Anguk Station, exit 3, and the first breath of air outside was cool, maybe ten degrees Celsius, carrying a lingering caramel smell from a roasted chestnut cart that hadn't opened yet.

The hanok guesthouse I'd booked was tucked into an alley off Gyedong-gil, called Bukchon-Oraedoen-Jip, roughly 'the old house of Bukchon.' The owner opened the door in a thick padded jacket and told me, in halting Mandarin, 'You've come at the right time — the ginkgo only started turning yellow these past two days.' In the courtyard stood an old persimmon tree, its orange fruit still hanging untouched. She said they leave some fruit for the magpies, a custom called kkachi-bap, meaning food left behind for magpies, a small gesture of sharing the harvest with nature. I carried that phrase with me for the rest of the trip; Seoul's autumn, I began to realize, isn't just scenery staged for visitors — it's a seasonal appointment the city keeps with itself.

The Changing of the Guard at Gyeongbokgung: Hanbok, Ginkgo, and a Ceremony About Time

I dropped my bags but didn't sleep, walking straight along Gyedong-gil toward Gyeongbokgung, about a fifteen-minute walk. Low hanok eaves lined both sides of the street; past nine in the morning the alley was still quiet, with only a couple of hanbok rental shops just rolling up their metal shutters, an ajumma crouched at the door wiping down the glass display case. I rented a pale lotus-colored hanbok at a shop called Hanbok-Sarang, a four-hour package for 20,000 won including a headpiece and a pair of cloth shoes. The clerk worked fast, cinching the skirt tie tight and telling me it would keep the wind from lifting the hem as I walked.

Walking toward Gwanghwamun Gate in a hanbok, I realized this was the first time clothing alone had gotten me into a palace for free — regular admission to Gyeongbokgung is 3,000 won on weekdays, but anyone wearing hanbok can walk straight past the ticket booth. The attendant glanced at me, nodded, and waved me through without a word. A crowd had already gathered on the plaza; the ten o'clock changing-of-the-guard ceremony began right on schedule. Drums sounded first, then a procession in Joseon-era military dress marched out from either side of Gwanghwamun in perfect step, spears and banners swaying gently in the wind. Ginkgo leaves kept drifting down from a nearby tree, and one landed on a guard's shoulder armor. He didn't move at all — it looked choreographed, and yet completely natural.

The ceremony runs three times a day in front of Gwanghwamun — 10am, 1pm, and 3pm — closed on Tuesdays, and each performance lasts about twenty minutes. If you want a clear shot of the procession from the front, arrive twenty minutes early and stake out a spot near the crosswalk directly facing the gate; it's the most crowded spot but has the best sightline. Afterward I wandered further into the palace grounds. The open courtyard in front of Geunjeongjeon Hall is covered in loose gravel that crunches underfoot — supposedly a deliberate design so that assassins could never approach silently in the old days. Walking on it long enough left my soles slightly numb, and history suddenly felt very concrete. The whole route from the entrance to Hyangwonjeong Pavilion at the rear of the palace took about two and a half hours, including a rest on a wooden bench by the pond, watching a few yellow leaves drift across the water while Bugaksan Mountain in the distance had already turned half-gold.

Seoul's autumn arrives like a letter that's only mailed once — miss these ten days, and you wait a whole year.

Gwangjang Market: The Oil-Fragrance of Bindae-tteok and the Mustard Kick of Mayak Gimbap

Just past one in the afternoon I took Line 1 to Jongno 5-ga Station; exit 8 opens directly onto Gwangjang Market. Before I even stepped inside I could smell sesame oil, gochujang, and charcoal smoke mixing in the air. The plastic awnings over the stalls had yellowed from years of cooking smoke, and the shouted prices layered over the clang of long spatulas against griddles like some kind of market-specific percussion.

I planted myself in front of Sunhee's Bindae-tteok, watching the ajumma ladle mung bean batter onto the griddle with practiced speed, the edges frying crisp while the center filled with bean sprouts and shredded pork, pressed down once before serving to drain the oil. One order costs 5,000 won, and it arrived still sizzling at the edges — crackly like a savory cookie on the outside, moist and beany within. A splash of soy-vinegar sauce cut right through the richness. The ajusshi seated beside me said he'd been coming every Wednesday for twenty years. 'It used to be a thousand won a plate when my father brought me here,' he added with a laugh.

After the pancake I pushed further into the market to find the legendary mayak gimbap — 'narcotic gimbap,' so nicknamed because people supposedly get addicted to it. The stall owner rolled the tiny seaweed rolls faster than I could follow, each order coming with about ten miniature pieces filled with just pickled radish and julienned carrot, served with a saucer of mustard-soy dip, for 3,000 won. The rice was soft with a faint sesame-oil fragrance; one bite at a time, and each dip in the mustard sent a sharp jolt up through my sinuses, tears welling up, and yet I kept reaching for another piece. Five or six red plastic stools were crammed shoulder to shoulder along the stall. An older woman noticed how enthusiastically I was photographing my food and said something in Korean; a younger customer nearby translated: 'She says foreigners always like taking photos, but you should eat it hot — it's no good once it's cold.' I put my phone away immediately and finished the rest properly, washing it all down with a warm bowl of makgeolli, faintly sour and sweet with the distinct tang of fermented rice, warming my stomach and pushing back the tears the mustard had brought on.

Cheonggyecheon and the Seoul Lantern Festival: Memories Lit Up Like Chuseok

Past six in the evening, once the sky had gone fully dark, I walked along Cheonggyecheon toward Cheonggye Plaza, and from a distance I could already see a whole row of lanterns reflected on the water. The Seoul Lantern Festival runs roughly from late October to mid-November each year, stretching more than a kilometer from Cheonggye Plaza to Supyo Bridge, free to enter, lit nightly from 6pm to 11pm. This year's centerpiece lanterns included a massive blue dragon, court ladies in hanbok patterned with magpie motifs, and a whole tableau recreating King Sejong of Joseon at his studies — the light glowing softly through silk lampshades, staining the water below in shifting streaks of orange and indigo.

While I was photographing one of the lanterns, a grandmother walking with her grandson struck up a conversation with me. She told me that when she was a child, Chuseok — Korea's harvest festival, sometimes called Korean Thanksgiving — was truly a children's holiday. The whole family would return to the countryside two or three days early, the women gathering to make songpyeon, rice cakes filled with sesame or sweet red bean and steamed over pine needles for their fragrance, while the children were tasked with laying the pine needles into the steamer one by one. She said the older generation used to inspect how neatly the songpyeon was shaped — 'fold it prettily, and you'll have a pretty child someday' — an old saying most young people now laugh off, though she still makes them by hand every year regardless. She also described the morning ritual of charye, the ancestral rites performed on Chuseok day, where the food on the offering table follows a strict order — red fruits to the east, white ones to the west — and where a married daughter is traditionally not supposed to stay overnight at her parents' home. Many households in Seoul still observe these rules, even as younger generations quietly push against them. She laughed and added that a festival like this one along Cheonggyecheon was something the city government only started organizing later, originally to mark the stream's history of being paved over and then reopened, but that it had gradually become something else too — a chance for busy city dwellers to gather, watch the lights, talk, and remember the lantern parades of their own childhood Chuseok nights.

When the lantern light fell across the surface of Cheonggyecheon, I understood that this city had lit up its memories too.

I kept walking slowly along the bank toward Supyo Bridge. Couples and families sat shoulder to shoulder on both sides of the stream; children held their hands up in lantern shapes for photos with their parents, and the air carried the smell of grilled squid and roasted chestnuts mixed with the faint dampness rising off the water. The full walk took about ninety minutes; the most crowded stretch runs between Cheonggye Plaza and Gwangtong Bridge. If you want to dodge the crowds, go after nine at night — the lanterns are still lit, but most of the crowd has already dispersed.

Sunrise at Naksan Park: Saying Goodbye to Seoul's Autumn

On my last morning before leaving Seoul I woke at 5:30, feeling my way in the dark up the stairs of Ihwa Mural Village toward Naksan Park. The stone steps were damp and slightly slick underfoot, the streetlights dim, and only a handful of locals climbed alongside me, presumably on their regular morning run. By the time I reached the old fortress wall, the sky had begun shifting from deep blue to pale gray, then slowly bled into orange-pink. Looking down from the wall, the black-tiled roofs of the Bukchon hanok houses were dusted with a thin layer of golden ginkgo leaves, and in the distance the lights of Namsan Tower were still on, as if the city hadn't quite woken up yet.

The wind was sharp; I zipped my jacket all the way up and shoved my hands in my pockets, listening to the metallic clatter of early vendors pushing carts somewhere below. The moment the sun broke the horizon, the light first caught the cracks in the fortress stones, then slid down across the hanok rooftops at the foot of the hill, and the whole city seemed to be gently plated in warm gold. I thought about the kkachi-bap the guesthouse owner had told me about, that persimmon left for the magpies, and about the grandmother's songpyeon story by Cheonggyecheon, and it struck me that Seoul's autumn was never just ginkgo and maple — it was the whole city compressing a year's harvest, its family reunions, and its memory of the past, into this one short stretch of ten or so days.

On my way down I made a detour to the stone wall path around Deoksugung Palace, the ginkgo-lined lane known as the 'Road of Farewell.' Legend has it that couples who walk it together will break up, so young Koreans tend to walk it in groups of friends rather than as pairs. I walked the whole length alone, golden leaves crunching dryly underfoot, and felt none of the supposed bad luck — only a quiet sense of being grounded. If you want to catch this fleeting stretch of Seoul autumn light, aim for the last week of October through the second week of November, book your Bukchon lodging early, and spread the visits to Gyeongbokgung, Gwangjang Market, and Cheonggyecheon across separate days rather than cramming them into one. Seoul's autumn rewards a slower pace — eaten one bite at a time, walked one step at a time.
