中文

飛機降落前,我從舷窗看見的馬拉喀什是一整片赭紅色,像有人把整座城市浸過陶土染缸。落地後第一晚,計程車司機告訴我一句話:「這裡的顏色不是塗上去的,是曬出來的。」我當時沒完全聽懂,直到接下來五天,我用腳走遍舊城巷弄、皇宮廢墟與畫廊庭院之後,才明白他指的不只是顏色,而是這座城市對待建築與美的整套邏輯——一切設計都得經得起沙漠陽光的審判。這篇日誌,我想用「藝術與設計」的眼睛,記錄我實際走過、吃過、談過的馬拉喀什。

清晨六點,巴迪皇宮的廢墟美學

我刻意把第一站排在清晨六點半,趁著旅行團還沒出門。從卡斯巴區(Kasbah)的riad走出來,巷子裡橙花的甜香混著剛出爐的可頌味,鵝卵石路面還帶著夜裡的濕氣,踩上去有點滑。走大約十五分鐘,穿過幾條幾乎看不到人的窄巷,才在一道不起眼的土牆門口找到巴迪皇宮(El Badi Palace)的入口,門票70迪拉姆(約7美元)。

推開門那一刻,我幾乎忘記呼吸——巨大的中庭空無一物,只剩下夯土牆的殘骸和幾座深陷地底的橘園,鸛鳥在斷牆頂端築巢,發出喀喀的敲擊聲,在空曠中迴盪得特別清楚。十六世紀薩阿德王朝的蘇丹曾在這裡鑲滿義大利大理石與黃金,後來繼任者為了報復,把所有能拆的裝飾都運去了梅克內斯,只留下這副骨架。導覽手冊上寫著一句摩洛哥諺語:「今日的廢墟,曾是明日的驕傲。」我站在空蕩的水池邊,看晨光斜斜切過斷牆的鋸齒狀輪廓,忽然覺得這種「未完成感」比任何金碧輝煌都更有說服力——它讓你看見設計最誠實的骨架。

廢墟不是美的失敗,而是時間替建築完成的最後一道工序。

上午九點,本尤素夫神學院裡的幾何秩序

從巴迪皇宮出來,我原本打算直接叫車,但司機建議我用走的穿過麥地那(Medina),說這樣才能「感受城市怎麼呼吸」。於是我沿著Rue Riad Zitoun Jdid往北走,再切進市集深處,結果在一堆賣皮革拖鞋和銅燈的攤位間迷路了將近二十分鐘,最後靠著一位賣香料的大叔用手比劃,才找到通往本尤素夫神學院(Ben Youssef Madrasa)的小廣場。全程走了大約五十分鐘,期間穿越至少三個完全不同氣味的巷弄:皮革鞣製的刺鼻味、剛烤好的msemen薄餅香、還有濕漉漉的薄荷葉味。

神學院門票70迪拉姆,建議一開門(早上九點)就進去,因為到了十點半後,旅行團會把中庭擠滿,拍照幾乎不可能。我在裡面待了將近一個半小時,單純坐在二樓迴廊的陰影裡看光線移動。這座十六世紀伊斯蘭學校的中庭牆面完全被雪松木雕、灰泥浮雕與zellige馬賽克磁磚覆蓋,圖案從地面往上延伸,幾何紋樣越往上越細密,最後在天花板消失於一片幾乎令人暈眩的複雜度裡——這就是伊斯蘭藝術裡著名的「恐懼空白」(horror vacui)美學,每一寸表面都拒絕留白,因為信仰認為完整與無限才值得被讚頌。中央的水池把整片雕花天花板倒映在水面上,微風一吹,圖案就跟著碎開又重組,我坐在那裡看了快二十分鐘捨不得起身。

正午,馬約爾花園與伊夫聖羅蘭美術館的藍

中午我攔了一台小黃色petit taxi去新城區的馬約爾花園(Jardin Majorelle),車資議價後是25迪拉姆,車程約十五分鐘,司機一路用蹩腳法語跟我聊他女兒在卡薩布蘭卡念設計系。花園加美術館的套票是150迪拉姆,建議提前在官網訂,現場排隊我觀察至少要多等四十分鐘。

一踏進花園大門,那抹著名的「馬約爾藍」立刻把我的視線整個吸走——那不是普通的鈷藍,畫家Jacques Majorelle在1930年代自己調配出這個飽和到近乎螢光的藍色,拿來塗滿建築立面,跟仙人掌、竹林與九重葛的橘紅色形成極端對比。錦鯉池裡水流的聲音、竹葉被風吹得沙沙作響,還有隨處可見的柏柏爾陶罐,整座花園像一個被精心設計過的色彩實驗室。隔壁的伊夫聖羅蘭美術館則把這種色彩邏輯延伸到時裝史:展廳裡陳列著聖羅蘭從柏柏爾地毯紋樣、摩洛哥傳統刺繡汲取靈感畫出的手稿,我站在一件1980年代的長袍禮服前,發現領口的幾何滾邊跟我早上在神學院牆上看到的磁磚圖案幾乎一模一樣——原來這座城市的美學語彙,早就悄悄流進了巴黎的伸展台。

傍晚,染坊巷的靛藍雙手與屋頂上的一頓晚餐

下午四點多,我特地繞去舊城東北角的Souk des Teinturiers(染坊巷),這裡遊客明顯變少,巷子兩側掛滿剛染好、還在滴水的羊毛線——藏紅花黃、石榴紅、還有那種深到近乎黑色的靛藍。空氣裡有股濕羊毛混著發酵植物染料的味道,不好聞,但很真實。一位老師傅哈桑(Hassan)看我拿著相機猶豫,主動招手讓我進他的染坊,遞給我一根木棍攪拌染缸,笑著說「這是摩洛哥的洗手液」。我付了他20迪拉姆當作小費,他也沒多要,只叮嚀我「回riad前先用肥皂洗三次,不然要藍三天」——他說對了,我的指甲縫整整藍了四天才褪乾淨。

晚上七點多,我走去香料廣場(Rahba Lakdima)旁邊那間常被旅遊雜誌推薦的Nomad餐廳,爬上三樓露台正好趕上日落。我點了鴿肉巴斯提拉(pastilla au pigeon,約85迪拉姆),外皮是酥脆的warqa薄餅,包著燉得軟爛的鴿肉、杏仁碎與肉桂糖粉,咬下去先是甜、接著鹹香的肉汁湧出來,層次複雜到我特地又點了第二份主菜——燉羊肉配洋李與烤芝麻(約130迪拉姆),肉質燉到用叉子就能撥開,甜鹹交織的醬汁拌著庫斯庫斯吃,幾乎每一口都想閉眼睛。最後上桌的柳橙肉桂沙拉清爽解膩,配一壺倒得老高的薄荷茶,茶水沖進玻璃杯時泡沫翻起來的聲音,加上遠處傳來的宣禮聲,那一刻我確定這頓晚餐會留在我記憶裡很久。

深夜,傑馬夫納廣場的鼓聲與禁忌

晚上九點,我走進傑馬夫納廣場(Jemaa el-Fna),白天賣果汁與猴子表演的攤位早就換成一整片冒著煙的烤肉攤,炭火的煙混著孜然味瀰漫整個廣場,鼓聲從四面八方傳來,分不清哪一團在演奏。我循著一陣特別低沉的鼓聲,找到一群穿著藍色長袍、戴著貝殼裝飾帽子的格納瓦(Gnawa)樂手,他們是摩洛哥撒哈拉以南非裔後裔的傳統音樂人,音樂帶著強烈的儀式感,據說原本用於驅邪治病的入神儀式。我在外圍站著聽,一位年輕樂手示意我坐下,遞給我一杯薄荷茶,用法語混著阿拉伯語跟我解釋:每年夏天在濱海小城索維拉(Essaouira)會有格納瓦音樂節,全世界的爵士樂手都會去朝聖,「但這裡的廣場,才是它真正的家」。

他也提醒我幾件小事:想拍耍蛇人或戴猴子的表演,對方伸手要錢很正常,一般給10到20迪拉姆就好,直接舉相機拍別人臉部特寫容易被視為不禮貌;齋戒月(Ramadan)期間,廣場的節奏會整個改變,日落前的大砲聲一響,所有人瞬間安靜坐下吃棗子跟哈麗拉湯(harira),那種集體屏息又同時鬆一口氣的畫面,他說我如果那個月來一定要親眼看一次。舊城區裡也幾乎看不到公開飲酒的場合,他笑著說「酒精留給新城的飯店吧,這裡的醉,是被鼓聲灌醉的」。

馬拉喀什的美從不安靜,它用顏色喊、用幾何低語,最後用鼓聲把你留到深夜不肯走。

離開前的最後一夜

最後一晚,我又爬回riad的屋頂,看整片舊城在暮色裡從赭紅轉成深棕,宣禮塔的燈一盞盞亮起。五天下來我才明白,馬拉喀什的設計美學從來不是單一風格,而是一層層疊加的結果——薩阿德王朝的廢墟骨架、伊斯蘭幾何的無限秩序、法國殖民時期帶來的裝飾藝術色彩實驗、還有柏柏爾工匠世代相傳的染缸與雕花手藝,全部擠在同一座老城的巷弄裡互相摩擦、互相借用。這座城市教會我最重要的一件事是:真正動人的設計美學,從來不是一塵不染的展示櫃,而是活生生被陽光曬過、被手觸摸過、被信仰與生活反覆使用過的痕跡。

ENGLISH

From the plane window, Marrakech looked like one continuous stretch of ochre, as if someone had dipped the whole city in a pot of terracotta dye. My taxi driver that first night told me, 'The color here isn't painted on — it's baked in by the sun.' I didn't fully understand what he meant until five days of walking through crumbling palaces, tiled courtyards, and dye-stained alleyways taught me that he wasn't just talking about color, but about an entire design philosophy: nothing in this city is built unless it can survive the desert light's judgment. This is my record of Marrakech seen through the lens of art and design — every place I actually walked to, ate at, and talked my way into.

6 a.m.: The Ruined Beauty of El Badi Palace

I deliberately scheduled my first stop for 6:30 in the morning, before the tour buses woke up. Walking out of my riad in the Kasbah district, the alley air smelled of orange blossom and fresh croissants, the cobblestones still slick with overnight dew. After roughly fifteen minutes through nearly empty lanes, I found an unassuming clay-walled entrance to El Badi Palace, admission 70 dirhams (about 7 USD).

The moment I stepped through the gate, I nearly forgot to breathe. The enormous courtyard stood completely empty — nothing but the skeletal remains of rammed-earth walls and several sunken orange groves. Storks nested atop the broken ramparts, their beaks clacking in a rhythm that echoed sharply through the emptiness. The Saadian sultan who built this palace in the sixteenth century once covered it in Italian marble and gold leaf; his successor, out of spite, had every removable ornament shipped off to Meknes, leaving only this bare frame. A local proverb printed in the guidebook reads: 'Today's ruin was yesterday's pride.' Standing at the edge of the empty pool, watching the morning light cut jagged shadows across the broken walls, I realized this unfinished quality was more convincing than any amount of gilding — it lets you see design's most honest skeleton.

A ruin is not beauty's failure — it is time completing architecture's final draft.

9 a.m.: Geometric Order Inside Ben Youssef Madrasa

Leaving the palace, I planned to grab a taxi, but the driver had suggested I walk through the medina instead, insisting it was the only way to 'feel the city breathe.' So I headed north along Rue Riad Zitoun Jdid and cut into the depths of the souk, promptly getting lost for nearly twenty minutes among stalls of leather babouches and hammered copper lamps. A spice vendor finally pointed the way with hand gestures alone. The walk took about fifty minutes total, passing through at least three distinctly different smells: the sharp bite of leather tanning, the buttery scent of fresh msemen flatbread, and the damp green smell of mint leaves.

Entry to the madrasa is 70 dirhams, and I'd recommend arriving right at opening, 9 a.m., because by 10:30 tour groups pack the courtyard so tightly that photography becomes nearly impossible. I stayed almost ninety minutes, mostly just sitting in the shaded upper gallery watching light move across the walls. This sixteenth-century Islamic school's courtyard is entirely covered — cedar wood carvings, carved stucco, and zellige mosaic tile, patterns growing denser the higher they climb until they dissolve into an almost dizzying complexity at the ceiling. This is the famous horror vacui of Islamic art: every surface refuses emptiness, because in this tradition, completeness and infinity are what deserve praise. The central reflecting pool mirrored the entire carved ceiling on its surface; a breeze would ripple through and the pattern would shatter and reassemble, and I sat there for nearly twenty minutes, unwilling to get up.

Noon: The Blue of Jardin Majorelle and the Yves Saint Laurent Museum

At midday I hailed a small yellow petit taxi to the new town's Jardin Majorelle, negotiated down to 25 dirhams, a fifteen-minute ride during which the driver practiced his rough French telling me about his daughter studying design in Casablanca. The combined garden-and-museum ticket runs 150 dirhams — book online in advance, since the walk-up line, by my observation, adds at least forty minutes.

The instant I stepped through the gate, that famous 'Majorelle blue' seized my entire field of vision — not an ordinary cobalt, but a saturated, almost fluorescent blue that painter Jacques Majorelle mixed himself in the 1930s and painted across every architectural surface, creating extreme contrast against cacti, bamboo groves, and orange bougainvillea. The sound of koi stirring the pond, bamboo leaves rustling in the wind, and Berber ceramic pots scattered throughout made the whole garden feel like a meticulously engineered color laboratory. The adjacent Yves Saint Laurent Museum extends this color logic into fashion history: the galleries display Saint Laurent's sketches drawn directly from Berber carpet motifs and traditional Moroccan embroidery. Standing before an evening gown from the 1980s, I noticed its geometric trim was nearly identical to the tile patterns I'd seen that morning on the madrasa walls — this city's visual vocabulary had, it turns out, quietly made its way onto the runways of Paris.

Evening: Indigo Hands in the Dyers' Souk and Dinner on a Rooftop

Around four in the afternoon, I made a point of detouring to Souk des Teinturiers, the dyers' quarter in the medina's northeast corner, noticeably quieter and more tourist-free. Both sides of the alley were hung with freshly dyed wool still dripping — saffron yellow, pomegranate red, and an indigo so deep it verged on black. The air smelled of wet wool mixed with fermenting plant dye, not exactly pleasant, but unmistakably real. An old craftsman named Hassan noticed me hesitating with my camera and waved me into his workshop, handing me a wooden stick to stir the dye vat, joking that this was 'Morocco's hand sanitizer.' I gave him 20 dirhams, which he didn't even ask for, only warning me to 'wash three times with soap before going back to the riad, or you'll be blue for three days' — he was right; the stain under my nails took a full four days to fade.

By seven that evening, I climbed to the third-floor terrace of Nomad, a restaurant tucked beside Rahba Lakdima spice square, timing it perfectly for sunset. I ordered the pigeon pastilla (about 85 dirhams), its crisp warqa pastry wrapped around meltingly tender pigeon meat, crushed almonds, and cinnamon sugar — sweet first, then a rush of savory juices, so layered in flavor I ordered a second course, slow-braised lamb with prunes and toasted sesame (about 130 dirhams), the meat falling apart at the touch of a fork, its sweet-salty sauce soaking into couscous until nearly every bite made me close my eyes. A cinnamon-orange salad cut the richness at the end, alongside a pot of mint tea poured from high above, the foam bubbling up as the call to prayer drifted in from somewhere across the rooftops — a meal I know will stay with me for a very long time.

Late Night: Drums and Taboos in Jemaa el-Fna

By nine at night I walked into Jemaa el-Fna, where the daytime juice stalls and monkey handlers had already been replaced by an entire field of smoking grill stations, cumin-scented charcoal smoke hanging over the whole square, drumbeats arriving from every direction at once, impossible to tell which group was playing what. Following one particularly deep rhythm, I found a group of musicians in blue robes and shell-decorated caps — Gnawa performers, descendants of sub-Saharan African communities brought to Morocco, whose music carries a strong ritual weight, historically used in trance ceremonies for healing and exorcism. Standing at the edge of the circle, a young musician waved me over, handed me a glass of mint tea, and explained in mixed French and Arabic that every summer the coastal town of Essaouira hosts a Gnawa music festival that draws jazz musicians from around the world — 'but this square,' he said, 'is still its real home.'

He also offered a few practical warnings: if I wanted to photograph the snake charmers or the monkey handlers, a tip of 10 to 20 dirhams was expected, and pointing a camera directly at someone's face without asking was considered rude. During Ramadan, he told me, the entire rhythm of the square shifts — the moment the sunset cannon fires, everyone suddenly falls silent and sits to eat dates and harira soup, a collective held breath followed by release that he insisted I witness in person someday. Public drinking is essentially invisible in the old medina, he added with a laugh — 'leave the alcohol to the hotels in the new town; the only thing you get drunk on here is the drums.'

Marrakech's beauty is never quiet — it shouts in color, whispers in geometry, and finally keeps you out past midnight with its drums.

One Last Night

On my final evening, I climbed back up to the riad's rooftop and watched the old city turn from ochre to deep brown in the dusk, the minaret lights switching on one by one. After five days, I finally understood that Marrakech's design aesthetic was never a single style, but a stack of layers pressed together — the skeletal ruins of the Saadian dynasty, the infinite order of Islamic geometry, the Art Deco color experiments brought in during the French colonial period, and the dye vats and carved woodwork passed down through generations of Berber craftsmen — all crowded into the same old alleyways, rubbing against and borrowing from one another. The most important lesson this city taught me is that truly moving design is never found behind a spotless glass case, but in the marks left by sunlight, by hands, and by faith and daily life used over and over again.
