中文

清晨五點五十分,我從里亞德(riad)的木門縫隙擠出去,老城麥地那(Medina)還沒有醒。空氣裡飄著隔夜煤炭與剛出爐薄餅的味道,巷弄兩側斑駁的赭紅色牆面在昏黃路燈下泛著溫度,像是被曬透的陶土。我知道自己趕著一件事:要在遊覽車與旅行團湧入前,獨自站在本尤素夫伊斯蘭學院(Ben Youssef Madrasa)的天井中央,讓那片幾何瓷磚在無人打擾的光線裡,把我徹底淹沒。

晨光未醒,先闖進瓷磚迷宮

本尤素夫伊斯蘭學院九點開門,我八點四十五分就到了門口,前面只排著兩個扛著三腳架的攝影師。買票十二歐元(約台幣四百元),閘門一開,我幾乎是用小跑步穿過陰暗的門廳——然後整個人在天井正中央停住了腳步。四面牆從地板到屋簷,由雪松木雕、灰泥鏤刻、彩色zellige瓷磚三層疊出,沒有一吋是空白的。摩洛哥的伊斯蘭藝術傳統禁止具象人物與動物形象,工匠們於是把全部的創作慾望灌進幾何與書法裡:八角星無限延伸、庫法體古蘭經文沿著牆緣蜿蜒,越靠近細看,越覺得每一片瓷磚都像被單獨計算過角度,才能在轉角處完美接合。我伸手觸摸那些瓷磚接縫,表面冰涼,拼接處竟找不到一絲手工的粗糙感——後來我才知道,這種zellige工藝需要匠人先燒製整塊彩色瓷磚,再用鐵鎚純手工敲鑿成細小碎片,每一種形狀都有專屬的名字與角度公式,一個穹頂的圖案往往要花上數月才能拼完。

九點半左右,第一團遊客的說話聲從門廳傳進來,天井瞬間變得擁擠。我識相地退到二樓迴廊——這裡曾是學生宿舍,窄小的木格窗俯瞰著整個天井,光線從方形天窗斜斜切下來,把地面切成一半金色一半陰影。我在那個轉角站了很久,忽然明白這棟建築真正的設計邏輯:它從不追求對稱的宏偉,而是讓每一個角度、每一次抬頭,都能看到不同的圖案疊加,像是一場永遠看不膩的視覺重複。

藍與黃是一種信仰

離開麥地那,我搭了一輛橄欖綠小計程車前往新城區古利茲(Gueliz),車程約二十分鐘,跳表費用大概三十迪拉姆(約台幣一百元)。馬約雷勒花園(Jardin Majorelle)九點半以後排隊人潮已經開始拉長,我買了含伊夫聖羅蘭美術館的聯票,約三百迪拉姆(近台幣一千元),說貴不貴,但走進去的那一刻,我覺得每一分錢都花得值得。

那種藍不是海也不是天空,是畫家雅克・馬約雷勒為了讓沙漠植物的綠顯得更綠,親手調配出的一種近乎暴烈的顏色信仰。

馬約雷勒藍(Majorelle Blue)濃烈得幾乎不真實,塗滿了花園裡每一座亭閣、每一扇窗框,對比著黃色陶盆與仙人掌的墨綠,構成一種近乎工業感的現代主義配色——這其實正是設計師伊夫聖羅蘭與伴侶皮耶・貝傑在一九八〇年買下花園後刻意保留、甚至強化的美學語彙。隔壁的伊夫聖羅蘭美術館用清水模與陶磚外牆,呼應著傳統柏柏爾織品的經緯紋理,館內收藏的高訂禮服草圖與非洲部落珠寶並列展出,清楚說明了這位設計師如何把摩洛哥的色彩詞彙,一路穿進巴黎伸展台。我在美術館頂樓的咖啡座坐了將近一小時,看著陽光把庭院裡的藍牆曬得發燙,忽然覺得馬拉喀什的美學核心,或許就是這種毫不妥協的高飽和色彩自信。

怎麼玩:一頭闖進染坊與皮革巷

下午一點半,我從傑馬夫納廣場(Jemaa el-Fna)北側的塞馬林市集(Souk Semmarine)入口鑽進麥地那深處,目標是傳說中氣味嗆鼻卻視覺震撼的染坊區與皮革巷。路線其實不複雜:沿著塞馬林主街走約五分鐘,遇到賣香料與地毯的攤位交界處左轉,再跟著地上零星的紅色顏料痕跡走,大約十五分鐘後,鼻子會先告訴你答案——一股混合了鴿糞、石灰與皮革鞣製劑的氣味撲面而來,這就是希奇亞辛皮革與染坊區(quartier des tanneurs)的入口。

一位穿著藍色長袍的老先生主動遞給我一把薄荷葉,說「拿著,湊近鼻子聞,不然你會受不了」,然後帶我爬上一座三層樓民宅的露台——這是當地人常見的「小費導覽」模式,沒有明碼標價,我後來給了他二十迪拉姆(約台幣六十五元)當作謝禮,他也沒有多要。從露台往下看,十幾個石灰岩鑿成的圓形染缸像調色盤一樣嵌在地面,靛藍、赭紅、鵝黃的顏料汩汩冒著氣泡,赤膊的染工站在及膝深的染液裡,徒手把成捆的皮革反覆浸泡、擰乾、攤在牆頭曝曬。這整套鞣製工藝從十一世紀沿用至今,幾乎沒有機械介入,染料仍取自番紅花、罌粟與石榴皮。我在露台上待了大約二十分鐘拍照,下樓後又在皮革巷裡逛了將近一小時,一路討價還價買了一雙手工駱駝皮拖鞋,店家開價四百迪拉姆,我殺到一百八十迪拉姆(約台幣六百元)成交。提醒一下,這一帶巷弄狹窄複雜,建議手機先存好離線地圖,若不想跟隨自願導覽,可以禮貌但堅定地說「la, shukran」(不用,謝謝)。

銅匠街的午后與信仰的節奏

從染坊區走出來,巷子漸漸開闊,叮叮噹噹的敲擊聲從遠處傳來——這裡是銅匠與錫匠聚集的區域,靠近巴伊亞宮(Bahia Palace)後巷。我在一間只有兩坪大的小工坊前停下腳步,店主哈金正用小鐵鎚在一只銅盤上一點一點敲出蔓藤花紋,動作快得幾乎看不清落點。他抬頭看我一眼,笑著用法語混雜阿拉伯語說「這個盤子要敲三天」,又指著牆上掛著的訂婚銅盤說,那是給附近人家嫁女兒用的,傳統上新娘出嫁前,家族會訂製一整套黃銅托盤與茶具,象徵家業與待客之道。

下午約莫四點,清真寺的宣禮聲從庫圖比亞清真寺(Koutoubia Mosque)的宣禮塔傳來,整條巷子的商販幾乎同時放下手邊工作,有人捲起小地毯就地朝麥加方向禮拜,哈金也不例外,他把敲了一半的銅盤輕輕擱在工作台上,示意我稍等五分鐘。我站在原地,看著整條巷子安靜下來,只剩宣禮聲在紅磚牆之間迴盪——那種瞬間的靜止感,比任何博物館裡的展品都更真實地讓我理解,這座城市的美學從來不只是視覺的,而是被日常的信仰節奏切割出來的時間感。禮拜結束後,哈金遞給我一杯薄荷茶,說這是待客的規矩,「你來我的店,就是我的客人」,那杯茶又燙又甜,我喝完才想起自己其實什麼都還沒買。

傑馬夫納廣場的黃昏:味道與故事

傍晚六點半,我回到傑馬夫納廣場,這時候的廣場正在經歷一場魔法般的變身:白天賣果汁與弄蛇人的攤位收起,取而代之的是上百個編號的路邊餐車一字排開,炭火升起,油煙與孜然香氣混著薄荷茶的甜味瀰漫整個廣場。我找到常被在地人推薦的第卅二號攤位(Stall No. 32),點了一碗蝸牛湯(babbouche)——攤主用長長的湯匙撈起浸泡在茴香、丁香與八種以上香料熬煮的湯汁裡的小蝸牛,湯頭帶著微微的甘草回甘,蝸牛本身彈牙但吸滿湯汁,一碗大約十五迪拉姆(約台幣五十元),我一口氣喝完整碗湯,又追加了一份。

接著我走到隔壁攤位點了一份塔吉燉羊肉(tagine),陶鍋一掀開,蒸氣裹著杏桃乾與肉桂的香氣撲面而來,羊肉燉得幾乎脫骨,底層的蜜漬洋蔥吸滿肉汁,配上剛烤好還燙手的圓餅kesra一起吃,大約七十迪拉姆(約台幣兩百三十元)。廣場中央,一群蓋納瓦(Gnaoua)樂手敲著鐵製卡拉卡布響板圍成一圈,節奏又快又催眠,吸引了一整圈觀眾,我後來才知道這種源自西非奴隸與撒哈拉商隊的音樂傳統,原本是驅魔儀式音樂,如今演變成廣場上最日常的即興表演。說書人、講故事的老人、耍猴人與畫指甲花彩繪的婦女穿梭其間,聯合國教科文組織早在二〇〇一年就把這座廣場的「口述與表演傳統」列為非物質文化遺產,而我坐在小板凳上喝著現榨柳橙汁(四迪拉姆,約台幣十三元),忽然覺得這整座廣場本身,就是馬拉喀什最盛大、最不需要門票的藝術館。

在這裡,藝術從不掛在牆上等人參觀,它煮在湯鍋裡、敲在銅盤上、也唱在廣場的夜色裡。

尾聲:紅色城牆下的最後一晚

晚上九點,我爬上廣場邊一間餐廳的頂樓露台,點了一杯薄荷茶,居高臨下看著整座廣場的燈火與煙霧交織成一片橘紅色的霧。庫圖比亞清真寺的宣禮塔在夜色裡打著暖黃燈光,遠處還能聽見隱約的鼓聲。這趟旅程走下來,我發現馬拉喀什的設計美學從來不是被規劃出來的城市景觀,而是層層疊疊的手工傳統——瓷磚匠人、染工、銅匠、廚子、樂手——各自在自己的技藝裡堅持著幾百年沒有改變的節奏。離開的前一晚,我在露台上待到將近午夜,城市的紅慢慢褪成深棕色,而我知道,自己會想念這種顏色很久很久。

ENGLISH

At five-fifty in the morning I slipped out through the wooden door of my riad, and the Medina was still asleep. The air smelled of last night's charcoal and the first flatbreads coming out of a neighborhood oven, and the ochre walls along the alley held a strange warmth under the sodium streetlights, like clay that had been baking in the sun for centuries. I had one goal before the tour buses arrived: to stand alone in the courtyard of the Ben Youssef Madrasa and let its geometric tilework swallow me whole, undisturbed.

Before Dawn Fully Breaks, Into a Maze of Tiles

The madrasa opens at nine, and I was at the gate by eight forty-five, with only two photographers and their tripods ahead of me. The ticket cost twelve euros, and the moment the gate opened I practically jogged through the dim entrance hall — then stopped dead in the center of the courtyard. Every wall, floor to eave, is built up in three layers: carved cedar, sculpted stucco, and colored zellige tile. Not an inch is left blank. Moroccan Islamic art traditionally forbids figurative depictions of people or animals, so craftsmen poured every ounce of creative energy into geometry and calligraphy instead — eight-pointed stars multiplying endlessly, Kufic Quranic script winding along the cornices. The closer I looked, the more it seemed each tile had been individually calculated to meet its neighbor at a perfect angle. I ran my fingers along the seams; they were cool to the touch, and I couldn't find a single rough edge. I later learned that zellige tiles are first fired as full colored squares, then hand-chiseled into tiny fragments with a small hammer, each shape carrying its own name and geometric formula. A single dome pattern can take months to complete.

By around nine-thirty, voices from the first tour group echoed in from the entrance hall and the courtyard suddenly felt crowded. I retreated, sensibly, to the second-floor gallery — once student dormitories — where narrow wooden lattice windows look down over the whole courtyard. Light fell diagonally through the square skylight above, splitting the floor into gold on one side and shadow on the other. I stood in that corner for a long time and finally understood the building's real design logic: it never aims for symmetrical grandeur. Instead, every angle, every upward glance, reveals a different layering of pattern — a visual repetition you never tire of.

Blue and Yellow as an Article of Faith

Leaving the Medina, I caught an olive-green petit taxi to Gueliz, the new town, a twenty-minute ride for about thirty dirhams on the meter. By nine-thirty the line at Jardin Majorelle was already growing, so I bought the combined ticket including the Yves Saint Laurent Museum for roughly three hundred dirhams. It's not cheap, but the moment I stepped through the gate, I felt every dirham was earned.

That blue is neither the sea nor the sky — it is a nearly violent article of color faith, mixed by hand by the painter Jacques Majorelle so that desert green would look even greener.

Majorelle Blue is so saturated it barely looks real, coating every pavilion and window frame in the garden, set against yellow ceramic pots and the deep green of cacti in a color scheme that feels almost industrial in its modernism — an aesthetic deliberately preserved, even intensified, by Yves Saint Laurent and his partner Pierre Bergé after they bought the garden in 1980. Next door, the YSL Museum's raw concrete and terracotta-brick façade echoes the warp and weft of traditional Berber textiles. Inside, haute couture sketches are displayed alongside tribal African jewelry, making it plain how this designer smuggled Morocco's color vocabulary all the way onto the Paris runway. I sat at the rooftop café for nearly an hour, watching sunlight bake the blue courtyard walls, and it struck me that the aesthetic core of Marrakech might simply be this uncompromising confidence in saturated color.

How to Actually Do It: Plunging Into the Dye Pits and Leather Lanes

At one-thirty in the afternoon I entered the Medina from the Souk Semmarine entrance on the north side of Jemaa el-Fna, aiming for the famously pungent, visually stunning tanneries and leather lanes. The route isn't complicated: walk about five minutes down the main souk street, turn left where the spice stalls meet the carpet stalls, then follow the occasional splash of red dye on the ground. After about fifteen minutes, your nose answers the question before your eyes do — a smell of pigeon droppings, lime, and tanning agents hits you all at once. That's the entrance to the tanners' quarter.

An elderly man in a blue djellaba handed me a sprig of mint unprompted and said, 'Hold this close to your nose, or you won't manage,' then led me up to the terrace of a three-story house — the classic informal 'tip-guide' model here, with no fixed price. I gave him twenty dirhams afterward and he didn't ask for more. From the terrace, more than a dozen circular stone vats sit sunk into the ground like a giant paint palette, indigo, ochre, and mustard-yellow dyes bubbling away, while bare-chested workers stand knee-deep in the liquid, dipping and wringing bundles of hide by hand before spreading them across the walls to dry in the sun. This tanning process has been used almost unchanged since the eleventh century, with dyes still drawn from saffron, poppy, and pomegranate skin. I spent about twenty minutes on the terrace taking photos, then wandered the leather lanes for nearly an hour below, haggling over a pair of handmade camel-leather babouches — the seller opened at four hundred dirhams, and we settled at one hundred eighty. A word of caution: this quarter is a genuine maze, so save an offline map on your phone beforehand, and if you don't want an unsolicited guide, a polite but firm 'la, shukran' (no, thank you) works well.

An Afternoon on Coppersmiths' Row, and the Rhythm of Faith

Coming out of the tanneries, the alleys widen and a rhythmic clanging drifts in from somewhere ahead — this is the district of copper- and tinsmiths, tucked behind Bahia Palace. I stopped in front of a workshop barely the size of a parking space, where the owner, Hakim, was hand-hammering a vine pattern into a copper tray, his strikes too fast to follow with the eye. He glanced up, smiling, and said in a mix of French and Arabic, 'This tray takes three days,' then pointed to an engagement tray hanging on the wall — commissioned, he explained, for a family marrying off their daughter nearby. Traditionally, before a bride's wedding, her family orders a full set of brass trays and tea glasses as a symbol of the household's standing and hospitality.

Around four in the afternoon, the call to prayer rang out from the minaret of the Koutoubia Mosque, and almost every vendor on the street set down their tools at once. Some unrolled small prayer mats right there on the ground, facing Mecca. Hakim was no exception — he set his half-finished tray gently on the workbench and gestured for me to wait five minutes. I stood still and watched the entire alley go quiet, nothing but the call to prayer echoing between the red-brick walls. That sudden stillness taught me more about this city's aesthetics than any museum piece could — that beauty here has never been purely visual, but is cut into shape by the daily rhythm of faith. When prayer ended, Hakim handed me a glass of scalding, sugary mint tea and said it was simply the custom of hospitality: 'You came to my shop, so you are my guest.' I finished the tea before realizing I hadn't actually bought anything yet.

Here, art is never hung on a wall waiting to be admired — it simmers in a soup pot, is hammered into copper, and is sung into the night air of the square.

Dusk at Jemaa el-Fna: Flavors and Stories

At half past six I returned to Jemaa el-Fna, which by then was undergoing its nightly transformation: the daytime juice stands and snake charmers packed up, replaced by hundreds of numbered food carts lined up in rows, charcoal fires lit, the smoke thick with cumin and the sweetness of mint tea drifting across the whole square. I found the locally recommended Stall No. 32 and ordered a bowl of babbouche, snail soup — the vendor ladled tiny snails from a broth simmered with fennel, cloves, and at least eight other spices, the liquid finishing with a faint licorice sweetness, the snails themselves chewy and thoroughly soaked in flavor. A bowl cost about fifteen dirhams, and I finished it in minutes before ordering a second.

Next I stopped at the neighboring cart for a lamb tagine. The clay lid lifted to release steam thick with dried apricot and cinnamon, the lamb falling apart at the touch of a fork, sitting on a bed of caramelized onions soaked in meat juices, eaten with a still-warm round loaf of kesra bread for about seventy dirhams. In the center of the square, a circle of Gnaoua musicians clanged iron qraqeb castanets in a hypnotic, rapid-fire rhythm, drawing a thick ring of onlookers. I learned later that this musical tradition, rooted in West African and trans-Saharan history, began as ritual music for spiritual healing and has since evolved into the square's most everyday form of improvised performance. Storytellers, elderly men spinning tales, monkey handlers, and women painting henna moved through the crowd. UNESCO listed the square's 'oral and performative traditions' as Intangible Cultural Heritage back in 2001, and as I sat on a small stool sipping fresh-squeezed orange juice for four dirhams, it struck me that this entire square is Marrakech's grandest, most unticketed gallery.

Final Night Beneath the Red Walls

At nine that evening I climbed to a rooftop terrace overlooking the square, ordered a mint tea, and watched the lights and smoke below blur into a single orange haze. The minaret of Koutoubia glowed a warm gold against the night sky, and somewhere in the distance a drum kept a faint, steady beat. By the end of this trip, I understood that Marrakech's design language was never a planned cityscape — it is layer upon layer of hand-carried tradition: tile-cutters, dyers, coppersmiths, cooks, musicians, each holding to a rhythm that hasn't changed in centuries. On my last night, I stayed on that terrace until nearly midnight, watching the city's red slowly fade into deep brown, and I already knew I would miss that particular color for a very long time.
