中文

朋友問我京都好不好玩,我通常會反問:你是想看金閣寺前面兩百個人舉自拍棒的樣子,還是想聽見竹葉被風吹過的聲音?這趟五天四夜的旅程,我把手機地圖上那些五顆星的景點全部收起來,改成問旅館櫃檯的老闆娘、深夜拉麵店的老闆、還有在巷口澆花的歐巴桑:「你自己假日會去哪裡走走?」答案帶我走進了一個完全不同的京都——一個沒有導覽旗、沒有排隊人龍,卻處處是細節的城市。

清晨五點半的黑谷:真如堂與金戒光明寺的寂靜

第一天清晨五點半,天色還帶著一點靛藍,我從岡崎附近的旅館走出來,沿著白川的小水道往東走。京都盆地的空氣在夏末的清晨特別乾淨,帶著一點露水混合青草的味道。走上一段緩坡,金戒光明寺的黑色山門就出現在眼前——這裡是幕末京都守護職會津藩本陣所在,遊客多半只知道附近的哲學之道,卻不知道再往上走五分鐘,有一片幾乎沒有人知道的墓地高台,可以俯瞰整個京都市街的屋瓦起伏。

我沿著碎石小徑爬上去時,只聽見自己鞋底踩在砂礫上的聲音,還有遠處一隻烏鴉的叫聲。真如堂的三重塔在晨光裡染成淡橙色,一位穿著作務衣的僧人正拿著竹掃把清掃庭院,竹葉刷過石板的沙沙聲幾乎像是某種節拍。我在階梯上坐了將近二十分鐘,看著京都塔的尖頂在薄霧中若隱若現,附近一戶人家飄來煮味噌湯的氣味,才驚覺自己竟然是這片高地上唯一的旅人。

真正的京都,不在清水舞台的人潮裡,而在你願意早起五點半、走上一段沒有指標的坡道之後。

徒步秘境路線:鞍馬到貴船的西山古道實走紀錄

如果只能推薦一條「怎麼玩」的路線,我會毫不猶豫選擇鞍馬到貴船的山徑,而不是嵐山竹林。第二天上午九點,我從出町柳站搭乘叡山電鐵,車票是紙本的舊式車票,單程約420日圓,車程大約30分鐘。電車行經市原、二ノ瀬時,窗外的楓葉隧道還是綠色的,售票口的站務員笑著提醒我:「秋天紅葉季這條線會塞得動不了,現在最舒服。」

抵達鞍馬車站時,巨大的天狗面具雕像張著嘴迎接我,整個站前廣場空蕩蕩的,只有一隻貓在階梯上曬太陽。從仁王門進入鞍馬寺需要支付300日圓的「愛山費」,我選擇不搭纜車,徒步走本殿金堂的參道,大約30分鐘的石階,兩旁是高聳的杉木林,空氣裡full是柏樹的清香,偶爾能聽見溪水在山谷底部流動的聲音。走到本殿金堂前廣場時,視野忽然開闊,可以俯瞰整片山谷,幾乎沒有其他遊客。

真正的秘境路段是從本殿後方繼續往奧之院方向前行的西山古道,這條路連接鞍馬與貴船兩地,全長大約2.5公里,單程步行時間約1到1.5小時。途中會經過傳說源義經幼年修行的「木の根道」,樹根縱橫交錯地爬滿地面,踩起來要特別小心,尤其我遇上突然一陣午後陣雨,石階瞬間變得濕滑,只能緊抓著繩索扶手一步步下山,雨鞋或至少防滑登山鞋是必要的裝備。整段路程幾乎沒有便利商店或補給站,建議自備水和一點行動糧。從西門走出後下切到貴船神社本宮,朱紅色的燈籠沿著石階兩側一路排下來,是京都最著名的打卡點之一,但因為我是下午兩點多從山裡走下來,現場只有三、四個人,和照片裡人擠人的景象完全不同。整趟路線含休息時間大約花了三個半小時,加上交通費和入山費,總花費不超過1500日圓,卻是我整趟京都行程裡最難忘的一段。

走到貴船神社結社、奧宮一路參拜下來,溪谷兩旁在夏季會搭起川床料理的座席,木造平台懸空架在貴船川上方,水聲就在腳下嘩嘩作響。我原本以為這種川床餐廳一定要提前一個月訂位,結果在「貴船 ひろ文」問了平日下午三點的空位,居然還有,兩人份的流水麵套餐加上鮎魚鹽燒,大約每人5000日圓,坐在竹簾下吃著冰涼的流水麵,溪水的涼意順著木頭平台的縫隙滲上來,是盛夏裡最奢侈的清涼。

巷弄裡的地藏盆:與田中婆婆的一段對話

搭電車回到一乘寺站已經是下午三點半,我原本只是想找間書店打發時間,卻在後街轉角撞見一整條巷子被紅白相間的紙燈籠布置起來。地面上用粉筆畫著跳格子的圖案,幾張矮桌拼在路中央,桌上擺滿了糖果、彈珠汽水和小玩具。一位鄰居婆婆田中さん正踩著矮凳,把一尊約半個人高的地藏菩薩石像頭上的舊圍兜換成新縫的紅色よだれかけ(口水兜)。

她告訴我,這是「地蔵盆」,每年農曆七月末、陽曆多在8月23、24日前後,京都幾乎每一條巷弄都會自發舉辦,是守護孩童與街道的地藏菩薩祭典,完全由町內會的居民自己張羅,沒有觀光局介入,外地遊客幾乎不會知道。孩子們玩著福引(抽獎)和輪投げ套圈圈,巷口飄來大人們現煎的炒麵香氣,油鍋滋滋作響混著醬汁焦香。田中婆婆笑著遞給我一顆糖果,說地藏菩薩最喜歡小孩子笑,「如果你家附近沒有地藏,小孩子晚上會怕黑」,她一邊說一邊仔細地把舊圍兜摺好收進木盒,那是她婆婆那一代傳下來的,每年夏天要拆下來清洗、重新縫製一次。這種代代相傳的小小手工儀式,比任何寺廟的官方法會都更讓我感受到京都人生活裡對「界線」與「守護」的細膩態度——巷口的地藏,不是觀光財,而是真真切切住在他們生活裡的鄰居。

地藏菩薩不在觀光地圖上,牠住在巷口,由一整條街的媽媽們輪流為牠縫製圍兜。

深夜食堂的煙硝味:新福菜館本店的黑醬油拉麵

晚上七點四十分,我搭電車回到京都車站,特意繞到車站北側、七条大宮附近一間看起來毫不起眼的老店——新福菜館本店。店門口停著兩、三輛計程車,司機們正大口扒著碗裡的炒飯,顯然是熟客才知道的深夜食堂。店內是昭和年代的裝潢,牆上貼滿泛黃的老照片,收銀台後方一位阿姨手腳俐落地同時顧著三、四個爐火。

我點了招牌的中華そば,700日圓左右,再加點一份炒飯,約650日圓。湯頭端上來的瞬間我愣了一下——顏色是深沉近乎黑色的醬油系高湯,香氣濃厚卻不死鹹,喝一口才發現底蘊其實是清爽的雞骨與豚骨混合湯底,只是被醬油上色得特別深。麵條是偏細的直麵,咬下去帶著恰到好處的彈性,叉燒切得薄卻分量十足,吸飽湯汁後帶著微焦的香氣。炒飯則完全相反,米粒粒粒分明,鑊氣十足,搭配偏清爽的醬油調味,和濃郁的拉麵形成絕妙對比,一鹹重一清爽,難怪本地計程車司機和貨運工人會把這裡當成深夜補給站。整頓飯加起來不到1400日圓,卻讓我明白京都的美食不只有懷石與抹茅,還有這種屬於勞動階層、深夜依然燈火通明的庶民滋味。

尾聲:祇王寺的青苔與離開前的體悟

最後一天下午,我搭乘嵐電來到嵐山,卻沒有往竹林小徑的方向走,而是繼續往西北的奧嵯峨前進。這一帶遊客明顯稀少許多,沿路是茅草屋頂的老民家,街道名字就叫「嵯峨鳥居本」,保存著江戶時期的街景。走約二十分鐘後,抵達了祇王寺——一座與竹林僅相隔十五分鐘步程,卻幾乎沒有旅行團造訪的小寺,收300日圓的拜觀料。

推開柴門的瞬間,眼前是一整片厚實濃密的青苔庭園,陽光透過茅草屋頂的窗格灑落在苔蘚上,形成一格一格柔和的光斑。這裡是平安時代平清盛寵妃祇王出家隱居之地,傳說她因失寵而剃髮為尼,寺內至今保留著描述這段哀傷故事的木像。我在廊下坐了將近半小時,聽著寺方人員用竹掃把輕輕清理落葉,那種安靜幾乎讓人忘記自己身處全世界最熱門的觀光城市之一。

離開祇王寺,走回嵯峨鳥居本的老街,夕陽把茅草屋頂染成金褐色,燈籠一盞盞被商家點亮。我在路邊的小茶屋喝了一杯冰抹茶,老闆娘用不太流利的英文加上比手畫腳跟我聊了幾句,說她從小在這條街長大,看著竹林那邊的遊客一年比一年多,卻很慶幸自己家門口這條路始終沒什麼人知道。收拾行李準備隔天一早離開京都時,我忽然明白,這座城市真正迷人的地方,從來不是那些被拍過千萬次的畫面,而是願意早起、願意繞路、願意跟陌生人多聊兩句之後,才會被輕輕掀開的那一層日常。

ENGLISH

Whenever friends ask if Kyoto is worth visiting, I always answer with a question of my own: do you want to see two hundred people holding selfie sticks in front of Kinkaku-ji, or do you want to hear the sound of wind moving through bamboo leaves before anyone else is awake? On this five-day trip, I closed the five-star pins on my map and instead asked the innkeeper, the ramen shop owner, and the elderly woman watering flowers on the corner: 'Where do you go on your day off?' Their answers led me into a completely different Kyoto — one without tour flags or queues, but full of quiet, specific detail.

5:30am in Kurodani: The Silence of Shinnyo-do and Konkai-komyo-ji

On my first morning, I stepped out of my ryokan near Okazaki at 5:30am, when the sky still carried a deep indigo hue. Walking along the small Shirakawa canal, the late-summer air felt unusually clean, carrying a faint scent of dew mixed with cut grass. After a short uphill walk, the dark wooden gate of Konkai-komyo-ji appeared before me — this was once the headquarters of the Aizu domain's Kyoto guard during the late Edo period. Most visitors only know the nearby Philosopher's Path, unaware that five minutes further up the hill lies an almost unknown cemetery plateau overlooking the entire tiled rooftop expanse of Kyoto.

Climbing the gravel path, I could hear nothing but my own footsteps and a distant crow. The three-story pagoda of Shinnyo-do glowed pale orange in the morning light, and a monk in work robes swept the courtyard with a bamboo broom, the rhythmic scraping of bamboo against stone almost like a metronome. I sat on the stone steps for nearly twenty minutes, watching Kyoto Tower's spire drift in and out of the morning haze, the smell of miso soup wafting from a nearby house, before realizing I was the only visitor on this entire hilltop.

The real Kyoto is not found on the Kiyomizu viewing platform among the crowds — it's found on an unmarked slope you only reach by waking up at 5:30am.

A Full Trail Report: Hiking from Kurama to Kifune

If I could recommend only one 'how to actually do it' route, I would choose the mountain trail from Kurama to Kifune over the Arashiyama bamboo grove without hesitation. At 9am on my second day, I took the Eizan Electric Railway from Demachiyanagi Station — an old-fashioned paper ticket, about 420 yen one way, roughly 30 minutes of travel. As the train passed through Ichihara and Ninose, the maple tunnel outside the window was still green; the station attendant laughed and told me, 'This line gets completely jammed during the autumn leaves — right now is the most comfortable time to ride.'

Arriving at Kurama Station, a giant tengu mask sculpture greeted me with its mouth wide open, and the entire plaza in front was empty except for a cat sunbathing on the steps. Entering Kurama-dera through the Nio Gate requires a 300 yen 'mountain protection fee.' I skipped the cable car and walked the main approach to the Hondo Kongo-do, about 30 minutes of stone steps flanked by towering cedar trees, the air thick with the scent of cypress, occasionally punctuated by the sound of a stream flowing somewhere below in the valley. Reaching the plaza in front of the main hall, the view suddenly opened up over the entire valley, with almost no other visitors in sight.

The real hidden section begins behind the main hall, continuing toward Oku-no-in on the Nishiyama Old Trail, which connects Kurama and Kifune — about 2.5 kilometers total, roughly 1 to 1.5 hours one way. Along the way, you pass the legendary 'Root Path of Trees,' where the young Minamoto no Yoshitsune is said to have trained, tree roots crisscrossing the ground so densely you have to watch every step, especially since I got caught in a sudden afternoon downpour that made the stone stairs treacherous, forcing me to grip the rope handrail all the way down. Non-slip hiking shoes, or at minimum rain boots, are essential. There are no convenience stores or supply points along the entire route, so bringing your own water and snacks is a must. Descending through the west gate into Kifune, red lanterns line both sides of the stone steps leading to the main shrine — one of Kyoto's most photographed spots — but since I arrived around 2pm after coming down from the mountain, there were only three or four other people there, nothing like the crowded images online. The whole trek, including rest stops, took about three and a half hours, and with transportation and entrance fees combined, cost less than 1,500 yen — yet it remains the most unforgettable stretch of my entire trip.

Continuing past Kifune Shrine's Yui-no-Yashiro and inner shrine, restaurants set up kawadoko riverside dining platforms suspended directly over the Kifune River in summer, the sound of rushing water audible right beneath your feet. I had assumed these platforms required reservations a month in advance, but when I asked at Hirobun on a weekday afternoon around 3pm, there was still space available. A two-person nagashi somen (flowing noodle) set with grilled ayu fish cost about 5,000 yen per person; sitting under the bamboo blinds eating chilled noodles as coolness seeped up through the gaps in the wooden platform was the ultimate luxury of a Kyoto summer.

A Neighborhood Jizo Festival: A Conversation with Grandmother Tanaka

By the time I took the train back to Ichijoji Station, it was already 3:30pm. I had only meant to kill time browsing a bookstore, but instead stumbled upon an entire back alley strung with red-and-white paper lanterns. Chalk hopscotch grids covered the pavement, several low tables had been pulled together in the middle of the road, piled with candy, marble soda, and small toys. An elderly neighbor, Mrs. Tanaka, was standing on a stool, changing the old red bib on a stone Jizo statue about waist-high, replacing it with a newly sewn one.

She explained that this was Jizo Bon, held around August 23rd and 24th every year, when nearly every neighborhood alley in Kyoto organizes its own celebration to honor the Jizo bodhisattva who protects children and the streets. It's entirely organized by the residents' association, with no involvement from tourism boards, and visitors from outside almost never know it happens. Children played fukubiki lottery games and ring toss, while the smell of yakisoba sizzling on a griddle drifted from a stall run by neighborhood dads, soy-sauce aroma mixing with charred noodles. Mrs. Tanaka handed me a piece of candy with a smile and told me the Jizo loves seeing children laugh — 'if there's no Jizo near your house, children get scared of the dark at night' — while she carefully folded the old bib and placed it in a wooden box, one that had been passed down from her mother-in-law's generation. Every summer, it gets taken down, washed, and re-sewn. This small, generational handmade ritual made me feel Kyoto's delicate sense of 'boundary' and 'protection' in daily life far more than any official temple ceremony could — the neighborhood Jizo isn't a tourist attraction, but a genuine, living neighbor in people's everyday lives.

The guardian Jizo doesn't appear on any tourist map. It lives at the end of the alley, dressed each summer in a bib sewn by the mothers of the street.

Midnight Diner: The Black Soy Ramen at Shinpuku Saikan

At 7:40pm, I took the train back to Kyoto Station and deliberately wandered to the north side, near Shichijo-Omiya, to a completely unassuming old shop called Shinpuku Saikan. Two or three taxis were parked outside, drivers hunched over bowls of fried rice — clearly a spot only regulars would know about. Inside, the Showa-era decor was intact, walls covered in yellowed old photographs, and behind the counter a woman moved briskly between three or four burners at once.

I ordered the signature chuka soba, about 700 yen, plus a plate of fried rice for around 650 yen. The moment the bowl arrived, I paused — the broth was a deep, almost black soy-sauce color, its aroma intense but not overly salty. Taking a sip revealed a surprisingly clean chicken-and-pork bone base beneath the dark coloring. The noodles were thin and straight, with just the right amount of bounce, and the char siu was sliced thin but generous, soaking up the broth with a faintly charred edge. The fried rice was the complete opposite — every grain distinct, full of wok hei, seasoned lightly with soy sauce, creating a perfect contrast to the rich, dark ramen. No wonder taxi drivers and dock workers treat this place as their midnight refuel stop. The whole meal came to under 1,400 yen, and it reminded me that Kyoto's food culture isn't only kaiseki and matcha sweets — it's also this kind of working-class dish, kitchen lights still blazing well past dark.

Final Afternoon: The Moss of Gio-ji and What I Learned Before Leaving

On my last afternoon, I took the Randen tram to Arashiyama but skipped the bamboo path entirely, walking instead toward Oku-Saga in the northwest. Visitors thinned out noticeably here, the road lined with thatched-roof farmhouses along a street called Saga Toriimoto, preserving the streetscape of the Edo period. After about twenty minutes on foot, I arrived at Gio-ji — a small temple just fifteen minutes from the bamboo grove, yet almost entirely untouched by tour groups, with a 300 yen entry fee.

The moment I pushed open the wooden gate, a thick, dense carpet of moss spread out before me. Sunlight filtered through the lattice windows of the thatched roof, casting soft square patches of light onto the moss below. This was where Gio, a favored concubine of the powerful warlord Taira no Kiyomori during the Heian period, took Buddhist vows and lived in seclusion after losing his favor; the temple still houses wooden statues depicting this melancholy tale. I sat under the veranda for nearly half an hour, listening to the caretaker gently sweep fallen leaves with a bamboo broom, the silence so complete it was easy to forget I was standing in one of the most visited cities in the world.

Leaving Gio-ji, I walked back along Saga Toriimoto as the setting sun turned the thatched roofs golden brown, lanterns lighting up one by one along the shopfronts. I stopped at a small roadside teahouse for a glass of iced matcha, and the owner, chatting with me in broken English and gestures, told me she'd grown up on this very street, watching the crowds near the bamboo grove multiply year after year, yet feeling grateful that the path in front of her own door had remained largely unknown. Packing my bags that night before leaving Kyoto the next morning, I finally understood: this city's real charm was never in the images photographed a million times over, but in the layer of everyday life that only reveals itself to those willing to wake up early, take the longer way around, and stop to talk with a stranger for a few extra minutes.
