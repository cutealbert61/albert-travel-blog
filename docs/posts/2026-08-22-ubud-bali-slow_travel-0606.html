中文

抵達烏布的第七天,我終於把手機的鬧鐘從「六點半」往前調成「五點二十分」。不是為了趕行程,而是想在遊覽車與導遊旗子出現之前,先聽聽這座小鎮真正醒來的聲音。我住在加冷小巷(Jalan Karna)一間只有六間房的家庭民宿,女主人瑪德每天清晨會在門口的小神龕擺上供品,那股椰子油混著茉莉花的氣味,幾乎是我對烏布最初的記憶。這篇日誌,想記錄的不是烏布有哪些景點,而是我用一整天的步調,笨拙地學著像當地人一樣生活的過程。

清晨五點半:市場甦醒的聲音

推開房門的時候,天還沒全亮,巷子裡瀰漫著一層薄霧,混著剛升起的線香煙味。隔壁院子傳來公雞打鳴,再遠一點,是甘美朗樂團在練習,鑼鼓聲斷斷續續,像是還沒睡醒。我沿著加冷巷往大馬路走,途中經過三、四戶人家的門口,都有女人蹲在地上,手裡捏著椰葉編成的小方盒——那是「貢薩里」(canang sari),每天早晨的供品。她們的動作快而熟練,像我們刷牙洗臉一樣自然,完全不覺得這是件「儀式性」的大事。

六點整,我走進烏布傳統市場(Pasar Ubud),就在大廟對面、拉雅烏布路(Jalan Raya Ubud)與蒙孔路交叉口。這裡白天賣的是給觀光客的圍巾和木雕,但清晨六點到八點,是屬於當地人的市場,賣的是真正要下鍋的東西。竹簍裡堆著紫紅色的紅毛丹、綠皮的蛇皮果、還有整簍還沾著泥土的南薑與薑黃根。我在一個賣油炸點心的小攤前停下,老闆娘是位皮膚曬得很深、笑起來眼尾有很深皺紋的女人,自我介紹叫瓦揚阿嬤(Ibu Wayan)。她用竹籤幫我夾起兩塊剛炸好的香蕉餅(pisang goreng),外皮酥脆帶著焦糖香,咬下去裡面的香蕉軟得幾乎融化,兩塊加上一杯用小塑膠杯裝的黑咖啡,總共只要八千印尼盾,不到台幣十五元。

旅行久了才明白,真正的在地感不是找到「隱藏景點」,而是願意在別人的日常時間表裡,笨拙地插一個位置。

市場深處的濕貨區飄著魚腥與丁香菸(kretek)混合的氣味,老闆們一邊秤菜一邊用峇里語聊天,速度快得我完全聽不懂,但那種嘈雜卻不刺耳的節奏,讓我第一次覺得自己不是「來看市場」,而是「路過別人真實的早晨」。七點半左右,遊客三三兩兩開始出現,舉著手機拍照,攤販的表情微妙地切換成營業用的笑容,我知道,屬於在地人的市場時間,差不多要結束了。

山脊上的慢走:Campuhan Ridge Walk 實走記錄

離開市場後我先回民宿沖了個澡,七點四十分再度出門,這次的目的地是坎普漢山脊步道(Campuhan Ridge Walk)。從加冷巷走到步道入口大約十五分鐘,沿著拉雅坎普漢路(Jalan Raya Campuhan)一路下坡,會先經過一座橋,橋下是烏斯河西支(Wos Barat)與東支(Wos Timur)交會的谷地,傳說中這正是「Campuhan」這個字的由來——河流相會之處。橋邊有座建於九世紀的古隆勒巴廟(Pura Gunung Lebah),廟門口有賣礦泉水和防蚊液的小攤,我在這裡花了一萬五千印尼盾買了一瓶水,提醒自己等一下的路上完全沒有遮蔭。

步道本身不收門票,入口就在廟的左後方,一段水泥階梯往上,接著是一整片起伏的草丘小徑,兩側長滿了叫做alang-alang的白茅草,風一吹整片山頭跟著搖晃,像海浪一樣。我大約走了四十五分鐘才抵達一般遊客會折返的觀景台,再往前是比較少人走的路段,經過幾戶還在飼養牛隻的人家,我甚至遇到一位牽著三頭牛過馬路的老先生,他對著我笑了笑,用簡單的英文說「早安,散步嗎?」我點點頭,他便自顧自地牽著牛,慢悠悠地往山坡下走去,一點也不趕時間。

整條步道來回大約九公里,如果像我一樣中途在Karsa Kafe停下來喝杯冰咖啡、拍拍照、跟牛對視發呆,大概會花上兩個半小時。實際的注意事項是:一定要在早上八點前出發,九點以後太陽會直曬整片草原,完全無遮蔽物,我後段幾乎是頂著大太陽走完的,汗濕透了整件襯衫;路面偶爾坑洞或濕滑,建議穿有抓地力的鞋子而非拖鞋;沿路有幾間咖啡座會有機車伕主動問要不要「ojek」載一程回去,單趟大約五萬印尼盾,體力不夠或想省時間的人可以考慮。走到後段,我的小腿已經隱隱發痠,但眼前那片層層堆疊的綠色山谷,還有偶爾飛過的白鷺鷥,讓我覺得這趟流汗流得很值得。

正午的家常味:在 Ibu Mangku 吃一碗焦香雞飯

中午十二點半,我搭了一趟Grab機車,花約十五分鐘、一萬五千印尼盾,前往烏布北邊的克德瓦坦村(Kedewatan),找一家在地人口耳相傳、比觀光客常去的Ibu Oka烤乳豬更「家常」的小店——Nasi Ayam Kedewatan Ibu Mangku。店面其實只是路邊一棟不起眼的藍色平房,幾張塑膠桌椅擺在騎樓下,牆上掛著已經泛黃的家族老照片,老闆娘曼谷阿嬤本人年紀大了,多半坐在收銀台後面,實際掌廚的是她的女兒和媳婦。

我點了招牌的「Nasi Ayam」套餐,一份約三萬五千印尼盾,店員俐落地用香蕉葉墊底,鋪上白飯,再依序疊上手撕的巴東雞絲、印尼式香料燉雞(betutu風味,薑黃、南薑、蝦醬慢煮入味)、炸得金黃酥脆的雞皮、水煮蛋、涼拌時蔬「urap」拌著現刨椰絲,最後淋上一大勺紅蔥辣椒醬「sambal matah」。第一口下去,香料的層次先是薑黃的土質香氣,接著南薑的辛香竄出來,雞皮咬下去卡滋作響,sambal matah的生辣與檸檬葉香氣讓整碗飯瞬間醒過來,urap的椰香則負責在最後收尾,讓整體不會太過油膩。我配了一杯冰檸檬汁(es jeruk),酸甜解膩,坐在吊扇底下,聽著隔壁桌一家四口用峇里語有說有笑,那一刻覺得比起任何米其林餐廳的擺盤,這一碗用香蕉葉包著、還帶著葉子清香的家常飯,更接近我想記住的峇里島味道。

吃完飯,我多留了十分鐘跟老闆娘的女兒聊天,她說這家店開業超過三十年,以前只有本地人知道,這幾年因為部落客介紹才漸漸有觀光客騎車特地繞過來,但用餐時間、份量和價格幾乎沒有變過。她笑著說:「觀光客常常太急,吃完就走,其實坐久一點,風景不會不見。」這句話後來一直留在我腦海裡,某種程度上,它就是這趟旅行想要傳達的核心。

午後的巷弄:貢薩里與神明的日常對話

下午兩點多,我沿著烏布西側的佩內斯坦村(Penestanan)小巷閒晃,沒有特定目的地,只是想避開午後最曬的時段,躲進畫廊與稻田交錯的巷弄裡乘涼。走到一半,我看到一位穿著紗龍、頭髮盤起的中年婦人坐在自家庭院台階上,面前擺著一堆椰葉、鮮花與竹籤,正在摺疊供品。我停下腳步看了一會兒,她抬頭對我笑了笑,用簡單的英文問我要不要坐下來試試看。她叫瑪德,是三個孩子的媽媽,白天在市場賣布,傍晚回家還要準備一家人隔天要用的貢薩里。

她教我把新鮮的椰子葉裁成方形小盒,用竹籤固定四角,再依照方位放上不同顏色的花瓣:白色朝東給濕婆,紅色朝南給梵天,黃色朝西給毘濕奴,藍或綠色朝北給毘紐天,中間再放一小撮白飯與一根點燃的線香。她說這是「感謝的供品」,每天早晚各獻一次,感謝神明也安撫看不見的靈,「不是求什麼,是提醒自己今天也要記得感謝。」我笨手笨腳摺了三個,大小歪七扭八,她卻很認真地誇獎我摺得不錯,還順手塞了兩顆自製的椰糖球給我當點心。

聊著聊著,她提到三天後她家族的廟要辦「奧達蘭」(odalan)——每一座峇里島的廟宇,都會依照峇里曆(Pawukon曆,一個週期210天)慶祝自己的「生日」,那天全村的女人會頭頂著層層疊起的水果供塔走進廟裡,男人則負責搭建臨時的祭壇與甘美朗演奏。她熱情地邀請我屆時再回來,「觀光客很少知道這個,但這才是我們真正的過年。」她也提到去年的「寧靜日」(Nyepi),全島關燈、禁止外出與工作整整二十四小時,連機場都關閉,只有負責巡邏的「佩查蘭」(pecalang)可以上街,「那天晚上抬頭看,星星多到嚇人,因為整座島都沒有光害。」聽她說著這些,我忽然明白,峇里島人常掛在嘴邊的「Tri Hita Karana」——人與神、人與人、人與自然的三方和諧——並不是寫在旅遊手冊上的口號,而是像瑪德手上這幾片椰葉一樣,真實地被摺進日常裡。

他們的信仰不在教堂或典禮上,而在每天清晨蹲下身,把一片椰葉摺成小盒子的十秒鐘裡。

傍晚的水宮:雷貢舞與烏布的夜晚

傍晚五點半,天光開始轉成蜜糖色,我走到烏布皇宮後方的水神廟——蓮花池神廟(Pura Taman Saraswati)。廟前那片睡蓮池此刻剛好逆著光,粉紫色的蓮花與石雕的守護神像在夕陽下鍍上一層金邊,連空氣裡的線香味都好像變得溫柔了一些。我花了十萬印尼盾買了一張晚上七點半的雷貢舞(Legong)表演票,就在廟埕的露天舞台上演出,舞台背景正是那片蓮花池。

表演開始前,甘美朗樂團已經在調音,金屬編磬與竹製甘美朗交錯敲擊的聲音,一開始有點刺耳,聽久了卻像被一層一層織進夜色裡。舞者出場時穿著金線刺繡的緊身服飾,頭上插著顫巍巍的雞蛋花頭飾,她們的眼神隨著手指開合快速轉動,那種細膩到近乎誇張的肢體語言,講述的是關於王子與公主的古老愛情故事,即使聽不懂峇里語旁白,光是看那雙眼睛的表情變化,就足以感受到裡頭的嫉妒、掙扎與哀傷。夜風帶著雞蛋花與線香混合的氣味吹過觀眾席,我旁邊坐著一對從澳洲來的老夫妻,阿嬤悄悄跟我說,她二十年前來過一次烏布,「那時候的猴林路(Monkey Forest Road)還沒有這麼多店,但這個舞,一模一樣,一點都沒變。」

表演結束已經快九點,我沿著猴林路慢慢走回民宿,白天擁擠的街道此刻安靜下來,只剩幾家小酒吧透出昏黃的燈光。路邊一個賣薑汁飲(bandrek)的小攤還亮著燈,我花了一萬印尼盾買了一杯,滾燙的薑味混著椰糖的甜,一路暖到胃裡。走回房間前,我看到民宿門口那盞小神龕上,瑪德今早擺的貢薩里已經被風吹得有點歪斜,花瓣也蔫了,但線香還留著一絲餘燼的味道。這一整天,我沒有進去任何一座需要排隊拍照的景點,卻覺得比過去任何一次旅行都更靠近這座小鎮真正的呼吸。慢下來之後才發現,烏布從來不需要被「玩」完,它只需要被好好地、認真地過完一天。

ENGLISH

On my seventh morning in Ubud, I finally moved my alarm from 6:30 to 5:20. It wasn't to squeeze in more sights before the tour buses arrived — it was because I wanted, just once, to hear this town wake up before anyone had decided what to sell it as. I was staying in a six-room family homestay tucked down Jalan Karna, where my host Made set out offerings at the family shrine every dawn, and the smell of coconut oil mixed with frangipani had become, without my noticing, the smell I now associate with Ubud itself. This isn't a list of what to see in Ubud. It's an account of one day I spent clumsily trying to move at the town's own pace instead of mine.

5:30 AM: The Sound of the Market Waking Up

When I stepped outside, the sky hadn't fully lightened yet. A thin mist hung in the lane, mixed with the smoke of freshly lit incense sticks. A rooster crowed somewhere behind a wall, and further off I could hear a gamelan ensemble rehearsing — the metallic clang arriving in fragments, as if the music itself hadn't quite woken up either. Walking down Jalan Karna toward the main road, I passed three or four family compounds where women crouched at their gates, folding small woven-palm trays by hand. These were canang sari, the daily offerings, and the women made them with the unthinking efficiency of someone brushing their teeth — nothing ceremonial in their posture at all, just Tuesday.

At six o'clock sharp I walked into Pasar Ubud, the traditional market directly across from the Ubud Royal Palace, at the junction of Jalan Raya Ubud and Jalan Mongkeg. By day this building sells sarongs and wood carvings to tourists, but between six and eight in the morning it belongs entirely to locals, selling things actually meant for the stove. Bamboo baskets overflowed with maroon rambutan, green-skinned snake fruit, and turmeric and galangal roots still crusted with soil. I stopped at a stall selling fried snacks, run by a deeply tanned woman with laugh lines carved deep around her eyes who introduced herself as Ibu Wayan. She speared two pieces of freshly fried pisang goreng — banana fritters — onto a bamboo skewer for me: the outside crackled with caramelized sugar, and inside, the banana had gone almost molten. Two fritters and a small plastic cup of thick black coffee cost me eight thousand rupiah, less than fifty cents.

It took me years of travel to understand that authenticity isn't a hidden spot you discover — it's the willingness to awkwardly insert yourself into someone else's ordinary schedule.

Deeper into the market, the wet-goods section smelled of fish and clove cigarettes, kretek, and vendors weighed vegetables while gossiping in Balinese too fast for me to catch a single word. But the noise had a rhythm to it that wasn't harsh at all — for the first time on this trip I felt less like I was viewing a market and more like I was simply passing through someone else's real morning. By half past seven, tourists began appearing in twos and threes, phones raised, and I watched the vendors' expressions shift, almost imperceptibly, into their business smiles. I understood then that the locals' hour at the market was closing.

The Ridge at Sunrise: Walking Campuhan, Step by Step

After the market I went back to shower, then left again at 7:40, this time heading for the Campuhan Ridge Walk. It took about fifteen minutes on foot from Jalan Karna to the trailhead, walking downhill along Jalan Raya Campuhan. The road crosses a bridge over the valley where the Wos Barat and Wos Timur rivers meet — supposedly the literal origin of the name Campuhan, meaning 'where the waters merge.' Beside the bridge stands the ninth-century Pura Gunung Lebah temple, and at its gate a small stall sells bottled water and mosquito spray. I bought a bottle for fifteen thousand rupiah, reminding myself there would be zero shade ahead.

The trail itself is free — there's no ticket booth, no gate. The entrance sits just behind and to the left of the temple: a short concrete staircase leads up into a rolling landscape of grassy hills covered in alang-alang, tall wild grass that moves in waves whenever the wind picks up, genuinely resembling an ocean swell. It took me roughly forty-five minutes to reach the lookout point where most casual visitors turn back. Past that, the trail thins out considerably, running past a few homesteads that still keep cattle. At one point I met an old man leading three cows across the path; he smiled and said, in simple English, 'Morning, walking?' I nodded, and he ambled on without a hint of urgency, cows in tow, disappearing down toward the valley.

The full loop runs about nine kilometers round trip. With a stop at Karsa Kafe for iced coffee, a few photos, and an extended staring contest with a cow, the whole thing took me about two and a half hours. A few practical notes worth remembering: leave before eight in the morning, because past nine the sun hits the entire ridge directly with zero shade — I finished the last stretch drenched through my shirt. The path has occasional potholes and slick patches, so proper shoes matter more than sandals. Along the route, a few cafés have motorbike drivers who'll offer an 'ojek' ride back down for about fifty thousand rupiah, useful if your legs give out before your enthusiasm does. By the final kilometer my calves were burning, but looking out over the layered green valley, with the occasional white egret gliding past, the sweat felt entirely worth it.

Midday, the Taste of Home: A Bowl of Smoky Chicken Rice at Ibu Mangku

At half past noon I took a Grab motorbike, about fifteen minutes and fifteen thousand rupiah, north of central Ubud to the village of Kedewatan, hunting for a warung locals had told me about — quieter and more unassuming than the famous, tourist-heavy Ibu Oka babi guling near the palace. It's called Nasi Ayam Kedewatan Ibu Mangku. The place itself is an unremarkable blue roadside building with a handful of plastic chairs under the awning, faded family photographs on the wall, and an elderly woman — Ibu Mangku herself — usually seated near the register while her daughters and daughters-in-law run the actual kitchen.

I ordered the signature nasi ayam set, about thirty-five thousand rupiah. The server lined a banana leaf with rice, then layered it swiftly with hand-shredded spiced chicken, betutu-style stewed chicken deeply infused with turmeric, galangal, and shrimp paste, crisp golden fried chicken skin, a boiled egg, a scoop of urap — steamed vegetables tossed with fresh grated coconut — and finished it with a generous spoonful of sambal matah, a raw shallot-and-lemongrass chili relish. The first bite hit with the earthy warmth of turmeric, followed by the sharper bite of galangal; the chicken skin cracked satisfyingly between my teeth, and the sambal matah's raw heat and citrus fragrance woke the whole dish up instantly, while the coconut in the urap rounded everything out so it never felt greasy. I paired it with es jeruk, iced lime juice, sharp and sweet against the richness. Sitting under a slow ceiling fan, listening to a family of four at the next table laughing in Balinese, I found myself thinking that this banana-leaf-wrapped plate, still faintly smelling of the leaf itself, came closer to the flavor I wanted to remember from Bali than anything plated on white porcelain ever could.

Tourists eat and leave in a hurry, she told me — but if you sit a little longer, the view doesn't disappear.

After finishing, I stayed an extra ten minutes talking with the owner's daughter. She told me the warung had been open more than thirty years, known only to locals until a few travel bloggers found it in recent years and tourists started riding out specifically to try it — but the hours, portions, and prices had barely changed. She laughed and said something that stuck with me for the rest of the trip: tourists always eat too fast and leave, but if you just sit a little longer, the view isn't going anywhere. In some way, that single sentence became the thesis of this entire day.

Afternoon in the Alleys: Canang Sari and the Everyday Conversation with the Gods

Past two in the afternoon, I wandered the small lanes of Penestanan, a village on Ubud's western edge, with no destination in mind — mostly trying to escape the harshest heat by ducking into the shaded alleys between art galleries and rice paddies. Halfway down one lane, I noticed a middle-aged woman in a sarong, hair pinned up, sitting on her porch steps surrounded by palm leaves, flowers, and bamboo picks, folding offerings. I paused to watch, and she looked up and smiled, asking in simple English if I wanted to sit and try it myself. Her name was Made, mother of three, who sold fabric at the market by day and came home each evening to prepare the next day's canang sari for her whole household.

She showed me how to cut fresh coconut leaf into a small square tray, pin the corners with bamboo picks, then place petals of different colors according to direction: white to the east for Shiva, red to the south for Brahma, yellow to the west for Vishnu, blue or green to the north for Vishnu's counterpart, with a pinch of rice and a lit incense stick in the center. This, she explained, was an offering of gratitude, given morning and evening — not a request for anything, but a daily reminder to remember to be thankful, and a way of appeasing the unseen spirits alongside the gods. I folded three, clumsily, corners uneven, and she praised my effort anyway, pressing two homemade coconut sugar candies into my palm as a snack.

As we talked, she mentioned that her family temple would hold its odalan in three days — every temple in Bali celebrates its own 'anniversary' according to the 210-day Pawukon calendar, a day when the village women walk in carrying towering fruit offerings balanced on their heads while the men build temporary altars and play gamelan. She invited me, warmly, to come back for it: 'Tourists rarely know about this one, but this is our real new year.' She also told me about last year's Nyepi, the Day of Silence, when the entire island goes dark for twenty-four hours — no lights, no work, no travel, even the airport closed, with only the traditional security patrols, the pecalang, allowed on the streets. 'That night, you look up and there are so many stars it's frightening,' she said, 'because the whole island has no light pollution.' Listening to her, I finally understood that the phrase Balinese people repeat so often — Tri Hita Karana, harmony between people and gods, people and people, people and nature — isn't a slogan printed in a guidebook. It's something folded, literally, into a piece of coconut leaf every single morning, the way it was folding in Made's hands right in front of me.

Evening at the Water Palace: Legong Dance and Ubud After Dark

At half past five, the light began turning honey-colored, and I walked to Pura Taman Saraswati, the water temple behind the Ubud Royal Palace. The lotus pond in front of it caught the low sun directly; the pale purple lotus flowers and the stone guardian statues seemed gilded at the edges, and even the incense in the air felt softened by the light. I paid one hundred thousand rupiah for a seven-thirty ticket to a Legong dance performance staged on an open-air platform in the temple courtyard, with the lotus pond itself as the backdrop.

Before the show, the gamelan ensemble was still tuning — the interlocking strikes of metal gongs and bamboo instruments sounded almost dissonant at first, but the longer I listened, the more it felt like something being woven, layer by layer, into the coming dark. The dancers entered in gold-threaded costumes, frangipani flowers trembling in their headdresses, their eyes darting in sharp coordination with their fingers. That hyper-precise, almost exaggerated body language told an ancient story of a prince and princess, and even without understanding a word of the Balinese narration, the shifting expressions in the dancers' eyes alone carried jealousy, struggle, and grief clearly enough. A night breeze carrying frangipani and incense drifted across the seats. Beside me sat an elderly Australian couple; the woman leaned over and told me quietly that she'd first come to Ubud twenty years ago. 'Monkey Forest Road didn't have half these shops back then,' she said, 'but this dance — it's exactly the same. Not one thing has changed.'

The performance ended close to nine, and I walked slowly back along Monkey Forest Road, empty now compared to its daytime crowds, only a few small bars still glowing amber in the dark. A roadside cart selling bandrek, a hot ginger drink, was still lit up; I bought a cup for ten thousand rupiah, the scalding ginger and palm sugar warming me all the way down. Before heading back to my room, I noticed the small shrine at the homestay gate — the canang sari Made had set out that morning had been blown slightly askew by the wind, its petals wilting, though a faint trace of incense smoke still lingered around it. I hadn't entered a single site that required queueing for a photograph all day, and yet I felt closer to this town's actual pulse than on any trip I'd taken before. Slowing down, I finally understood: Ubud was never a place meant to be finished. It only asks to be lived, properly, for one whole day at a time.
