中文

抵達的那個晚上,計程車在麥地那(Medina)外圍就停了下來,司機指著眼前一條僅容一人側身通過的巷子說,裡面到不了。我拖著行李箱,在幾乎沒有路燈的土黃色巷弄裡走了快十分鐘,聞著空氣裡混雜的孜然、驢糞與茉莉花的氣味,才終於敲開一扇毫不起眼、油漆斑駁的木門。門一打開,裡面竟是滿牆手工鑲嵌的zellige彩磚,天井灑下的月光落在噴水池上——那一刻我才明白,這座城市把最美的東西都藏在最不起眼的立面之後。這趟旅程,我給自己的任務很單純:不看沙漠團,不趕景點清單,只用五天時間,專心把馬拉喀什當成一本立體的設計圖鑑來讀。

清晨六點:里亞德天井裡的幾何課

第一天清晨六點,我被喚拜聲吵醒,索性不睡了,光著腳走到我住的里亞德(Riad,傳統合院式老宅改建的旅館)天井。晨光還很斜,從天井上方的方形開口直直落下,照在牆面拼貼的八角星紋zellige磚上,顏色是深綠、赭紅與牛奶白的組合,每一片磚都是工匠手工敲鑿成型,邊緣帶著細微的不規則,反而讓整片牆看起來像會呼吸。老闆娘Fatima端著薄荷茶走過來告訴我,這棟房子有一百二十年歷史,磚是從Fes找師傅訂做的,因為「馬拉喀什的師傅做雕花石膏比較厲害,Fes的磚工比較細」——光是這句話,就讓我意識到摩洛哥的工藝地圖遠比我想像的細緻,每座城市都有自己專精的手藝分工。

我坐在天井邊喝完那杯茶,才注意到頭頂的雪松木橫梁上刻著阿拉伯書法紋樣,漆成暗紅與金色,已經斑駁,卻完全沒有要修復的意思。Fatima說這叫「有生命的老」,修得太新反而失禮。這句話我後來一路帶在心裡,因為它幾乎解釋了整座麥地那的美學邏輯——不追求完美無瑕,而是讓時間的痕跡成為裝飾的一部分。

馬拉喀什不把美攤在陽光下,它把美鎖進門後、藏進牆縫、刻進你不會抬頭看的橫梁裡。

上午九點:巴伊亞宮的光影迷宮,實際走一趟

喝完茶,我八點半出發前往巴伊亞宮(Bahia Palace),想搶在旅行團大批湧入前先進去。從我住的里亞德(位於Kaat Benahid區附近)步行過去大約二十五分鐘,一路穿過賣五金與二手電器的Mouassine市集邊緣,再繞進猶太區Mellah的窄巷,路面從柏油變成碎石又變成磨得發亮的石板,腳踩上去的觸感一路在變化。我九點整抵達售票口,票價當時是七十迪拉姆(約新台幣二百三十元左右),現場排隊人不多,大概等了十分鐘。

一進大門,我先愣住——不是因為金碧輝煌,而是因為安靜。整座十九世紀末建成的宮殿以一連串庭院串聯,每個庭院的地磚拼貼圖案都不重複,牆面雕花石膏(gebs)細到像蕾絲,天花板則是彩繪雪松木(zouak),藍綠色調在不同時段的光線下會轉變深淺。我在「大理石庭院」(Cour d'Honneur)待了快四十分鐘,只是坐在陰影邊緣看光線怎麼隨著太陽升高,一格一格爬過地磚的幾何紋樣。要注意的是,宮殿裡沒有太多文字說明牌,建議提前查好每個廳室的背景,不然容易走馬看花;另外中庭沒有遮蔽,建議十一點前造訪,避開正午曝曬。整趟參觀我大約花了兩小時十五分鐘,出來時外頭已經聚集了三個旅行團,慶幸自己提早出門。

正午時分:馬約雷勒花園裡的鈷藍實驗

中午我搭了一段十五迪拉姆的小黃計程車(Petit Taxi)前往新城區的馬約雷勒花園(Jardin Majorelle)。這座花園原本是法國畫家Jacques Majorelle於一九二〇年代設計,後來由設計師Yves Saint Laurent與伴侶Pierre Bergé買下修復。整座園區的建築塗上一種被稱為「馬約雷勒藍」的高飽和鈷藍色,在正午陽光下幾乎刺眼,卻和竹林、仙人掌、陶盆的橙黃形成一種近乎粗暴卻有效的對比。我站在其中一道藍牆前拍照,一位同樣在拍照的法國遊客笑著跟我說:「這個藍色比地中海還藍。」

花園裡附設的柏柏爾博物館(Musée Berbère)我覺得比花園本身更值得細看——展出的柏柏爾族傳統服飾、銀飾與地毯,直接說明了摩洛哥設計語彙的根源:幾何紋樣多半來自織品與紋身圖案,而非單純裝飾。隔壁的YSL美術館由巴黎建築事務所Studio KO操刀,外牆用磚砌出經緯交織的紋理,像是把傳統織品的編織邏輯直接翻譯成建築立面——這也是我這趟旅程裡,唯一一處讓我真切感受到「傳統工藝正在被當代摩洛哥建築師重新詮釋」的地方。門票含花園與兩館合計約新台幣八百多元,不便宜,但我覺得作為理解這座城市設計美學的入門,值得。

傍晚時分:染坊巷弄裡遇見的老匠人

傍晚五點左右,我走進麥地那深處的染坊市集(Souk Sebbaghine,染布市集),巷弄兩側懸吊著剛染好、還在滴水的羊毛線,顏色從藏紅花黃到石榴紅層層疊疊掛成一片天棚,陽光穿過線團縫隙灑下來,地面全是碎花般的光斑。空氣裡有一股強烈的礦物與植物混合的氣味,後來才知道那是石榴皮、藏紅花與靛藍粉混合染料的味道。

一位年紀約六十多歲、手掌染得幾乎全黑的老匠人看我盯著染缸看太久,直接遞了根木棍讓我試著攪動染缸。他叫Hassan,染這行做了四十年,是跟他父親學的。我們用簡單的法文加比手畫腳聊了起來,他說下週五是他小兒子的婚禮,新娘要在婚禮前一晚做「henna之夜」(Laylat al-Henna),請專門畫henna的婦人在新娘手上畫滿幾何與花卉紋樣,這些圖案其實跟他染布用的紋樣系統是同一套邏輯——都是先用網格打底,再疊加對稱花紋。他還提到每週五中午全家一定會聚在一起吃couscous,這是老一輩摩洛哥人雷打不動的規矩,「就算平常再忙,週五couscous不能少」。這段對話讓我第一次意識到,馬拉喀什的裝飾藝術從來不是博物館裡的靜態展品,而是活在婚禮、活在每週五餐桌上的日常語言。

這裡的每一道紋樣都在被使用著——畫在新娘手上,織進地毯裡,煮進週五的couscous香氣裡。

夜幕降臨:德吉瑪廣場的煙火氣與說書人

晚上七點半,天色剛暗下來,我走進德吉瑪廣場(Jemaa el-Fnaa)。白天這裡只是個空曠廣場,入夜後卻像變魔術一樣,上百個流動攤位同時亮起白熾燈泡,煙從烤肉架上直直竄起,混著孜然、烤肉、薄荷與汗水的氣味撲面而來,耍蛇人的笛聲、說書人的吼聲、鼓聲全部疊在一起,吵得讓人清醒。

我找了編號第一區靠近清真寺那排、當地人排隊比較多的攤位坐下,點了tanjia(用陶罐悶煮數小時的燉牛肉,加了大量孜然與蒜頭,肉軟到用麵包一撥就化開)、一碗babbouche(蝸牛湯,湯底加了茴香與八種香料,喝起來帶著淡淡的甘草尾韻,一開始有點抗拒,喝完卻想再來一碗)、還有一份哈里拉湯(harira),濃稠的番茄扁豆湯配上一顆椰棗,整套下來花了約六十迪拉姆(不到新台幣二百元),攤主是個叫Youssef的中年男人,一邊煮一邊用中文跟我說「你好」,他說每年都有台灣遊客來找他,因為Youssef的攤位被寫進某本旅遊指南裡。

吃完飯我沒有立刻離開,而是繞到廣場中央,那裡圍著一圈觀眾聽一位白鬍子老先生說書,這種傳統表演叫「halqa」,說書人不用麥克風,純靠聲音的抑揚頓挫抓住聽眾,講到激動處全場會突然爆出笑聲或驚呼。二〇〇一年聯合國教科文組織把德吉瑪廣場列為「人類口述與非物質文化遺產」,而halqa說書正是核心項目之一——只是懂阿拉伯方言的觀眾越來越少,老說書人擔心這門手藝會斷在他這一代。我聽不懂內容,卻站在人群外圍看了快二十分鐘,單純被那種聲音的節奏和圍觀者的表情吸引,那一刻我覺得,這廣場本身就是一座活的表演藝術館,不需要牆,不需要門票。

深夜的屋頂:把一整天的線條收進眼底

晚上十點,我爬上一家可以俯瞰廣場的屋頂咖啡館,點了一杯薄荷茶坐下。庫圖比亞清真寺(Koutoubia Mosque)的宣禮塔在遠處打著暖黃燈光,那座塔的比例——高度是底邊寬度的五倍——後來被公認為北非伊斯蘭建築的黃金比例範本,連我今天下午在YSL美術館看到的磚砌立面比例,某種程度上都是從這座塔的邏輯延伸出來的。我把手機裡拍的照片一張張滑過去:里亞德的天井磚、巴伊亞宮的石膏雕花、馬約雷勒的鈷藍牆、染坊的彩色羊毛、廣場的煙霧與燈光,忽然覺得這些畫面其實是同一套語言的不同方言——都是關於如何用重複、對稱與手工誤差,把「秩序」和「生活感」同時放進同一個畫面裡。喝完那杯茶,廣場的喧鬧聲依然從樓下傳上來,我知道自己明天還會再回到這裡,因為五天,顯然不夠把這座城市的紋樣全部看懂。

The taxi dropped me at the edge of the Medina just before midnight, and the driver simply pointed at an alley barely wide enough for one person and said he couldn't go further. Dragging my suitcase through the almost unlit ochre lanes for nearly ten minutes, breathing in a mix of cumin, donkey dung, and jasmine, I finally knocked on an unremarkable wooden door with peeling paint. When it opened, the wall inside was covered floor to ceiling in hand-set zellige tilework, moonlight pooling on a small fountain in the courtyard. That was the moment I understood: this city hides its most beautiful things behind its plainest faces. My plan for the trip was simple — skip the desert tours, ignore the checklist, and spend five days reading Marrakech as a three-dimensional catalogue of design.

Six in the Morning: A Geometry Lesson in the Riad Courtyard

I woke at six to the call to prayer and didn't bother going back to sleep. Barefoot, I walked to the courtyard of the riad I was staying in. The light was still low, falling straight down through the square opening above the patio onto a wall of eight-pointed zellige stars in deep green, ochre red, and milk white. Each tile had been hand-chiseled, its edges slightly irregular, which somehow made the whole wall feel like it was breathing. Fatima, the woman running the riad, brought over a glass of mint tea and told me the house was a hundred and twenty years old, and the tiles had been custom-ordered from craftsmen in Fes, because 'Marrakech craftsmen are better at carved plaster, Fes craftsmen are finer with tiles.' That single sentence told me the map of Moroccan craft is far more specific than I'd assumed — every city has its own specialty.

Finishing my tea, I noticed the cedar beams overhead carved with Arabic calligraphy, painted in faded red and gold, clearly never restored. Fatima called this 'living age' — to restore it too perfectly would be disrespectful. I carried that phrase with me for the rest of the trip, because it seemed to explain the aesthetic logic of the entire Medina: not a pursuit of flawlessness, but letting the marks of time become part of the decoration.

Marrakech doesn't lay its beauty out in the sun. It locks it behind doors, tucks it into wall cracks, carves it into beams you'd never think to look up at.

Nine in the Morning: Walking the Light-and-Shadow Maze of Bahia Palace

After tea, I set out at eight-thirty for Bahia Palace, hoping to beat the tour groups. From my riad near Kaat Benahid, it took about twenty-five minutes on foot, skirting the edge of the Mouassine market where vendors sell hardware and secondhand electronics, then winding through the narrow lanes of the Mellah, the old Jewish quarter. The ground shifted underfoot from asphalt to gravel to stone polished smooth by decades of footsteps. I reached the ticket booth right at nine; entry was seventy dirhams at the time, and the line took about ten minutes.

Walking through the main gate, I stopped not because of any dazzling gold, but because of the silence. This late-nineteenth-century palace unfolds as a sequence of courtyards, each with a different tile pattern underfoot, walls carved in lace-fine plaster (gebs), ceilings painted in cedar (zouak) with blue-green tones that shift depth depending on the hour. I spent nearly forty minutes in the Cour d'Honneur, just sitting at the edge of the shade watching the light climb square by square across the geometric floor patterns as the sun rose. One thing worth noting: there's almost no explanatory signage inside, so it helps to research each room beforehand; the central courtyards are also completely exposed, so I'd recommend visiting before eleven to avoid the midday sun. My full visit took about two hours and fifteen minutes, and by the time I left, three tour groups had already gathered outside — I was glad I'd left early.

Midday: A Cobalt Experiment at Jardin Majorelle

At noon I took a fifteen-dirham petit taxi to the new town to visit Jardin Majorelle. Originally designed in the 1920s by French painter Jacques Majorelle, the garden was later restored by designer Yves Saint Laurent and his partner Pierre Bergé. The buildings throughout are painted in a saturated cobalt now known as 'Majorelle blue,' almost blinding under the noon sun, set against bamboo groves, cacti, and orange ceramic pots in a contrast that feels almost aggressive yet somehow works. Standing in front of one of the blue walls taking photos, a French tourist beside me laughed and said, 'This blue is bluer than the Mediterranean.'

I found the adjoining Musée Berbère more compelling than the garden itself — the traditional Berber clothing, silver jewelry, and carpets on display made clear where Morocco's design vocabulary actually comes from: most of the geometric motifs originate in textiles and tattoo patterns rather than pure ornament. Next door, the Yves Saint Laurent Museum, designed by the Paris firm Studio KO, has an exterior brick facade woven in a warp-and-weft texture, essentially translating the logic of traditional weaving directly into architecture. It was the one place on this trip where I truly felt traditional craft being reinterpreted by contemporary Moroccan architecture. Combined admission for the garden and both museums ran to roughly the equivalent of thirty US dollars — not cheap, but worth it as an entry point into understanding the city's design aesthetics.

Late Afternoon: Meeting an Old Craftsman in the Dyers' Alleys

Around five in the afternoon I walked into the Souk Sebbaghine, the dyers' market deep in the Medina. Wool skeins, still dripping from freshly dyeing, hung across the lanes in layers from saffron yellow to pomegranate red, forming a canopy the sunlight filtered through, scattering flecks of colored light across the ground. The air carried a strong mineral-and-plant smell — pomegranate rind, saffron, and indigo powder mixed together.

An artisan in his sixties, his hands stained nearly black, noticed me lingering too long by his dye vat and simply handed me a wooden pole to try stirring it myself. His name was Hassan, and he'd been dyeing for forty years, having learned from his father. Using a mix of simple French and gestures, we talked, and he mentioned his youngest son's wedding was the following Friday. The night before, the bride would have her 'Laylat al-Henna,' where a woman skilled in henna application would cover her hands in geometric and floral patterns — patterns that, he pointed out, follow the exact same logic as the ones he dyes into cloth: a grid laid down first, then symmetrical motifs layered on top. He also mentioned that every Friday at noon, without fail, the whole family gathers for couscous — an unbreakable rule for the older generation, 'no matter how busy, Friday couscous doesn't get skipped.' That conversation was the moment I realized Marrakech's decorative art was never a static museum piece — it lives in wedding henna, in carpets, in the smell of Friday's couscous.

Every pattern here is still in use — painted on a bride's hands, woven into a carpet, simmered into the smell of Friday's couscous.

Nightfall: The Smoke and Storytellers of Jemaa el-Fnaa

At seven-thirty, just as the sky was darkening, I walked into Jemaa el-Fnaa. By day it's just an open square; after dark it transforms as if by magic — hundreds of mobile stalls light up their bare bulbs at once, smoke rises straight off the grills, and the smell of cumin, grilled meat, mint, and sweat hits you all at once. Snake charmers' flutes, storytellers shouting, drums — all layered together into a noise that jolts you awake.

I sat down at a stall in the row closest to the mosque, where I noticed more locals queuing than tourists, and ordered tanjia — beef slow-cooked for hours in a sealed clay urn with heavy cumin and garlic, the meat soft enough to fall apart with a piece of bread — a bowl of babbouche, snail soup with a broth spiced with fennel and eight different spices, finishing with a faint licorice note I resisted at first but wanted more of by the last spoonful — and a bowl of harira, the thick tomato-lentil soup served with a date on the side. The whole meal cost about sixty dirhams, under seven US dollars. The stall owner, Youssef, greeted me in Mandarin — 'ni hao' — and said Taiwanese travelers find his stall every year because it got written up in some guidebook.

I didn't leave right after eating. Instead I wandered toward the center of the square, where a crowd had gathered around a white-bearded old man telling stories — a tradition called halqa. He used no microphone, relying purely on the rise and fall of his voice to hold the audience, and at the dramatic moments the crowd would burst into laughter or gasps all at once. In 2001, UNESCO listed Jemaa el-Fnaa itself as a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity, and halqa storytelling is one of its central practices — though fewer and fewer listeners still understand the dialects used, and the old storytellers worry the craft may not survive their generation. I didn't understand a word, but I stood at the edge of the crowd for nearly twenty minutes, drawn purely by the rhythm of his voice and the faces of the people listening. In that moment, the square itself felt like a living performance museum — no walls, no admission fee required.

Late Night on a Rooftop: Gathering the Day's Lines Into View

At ten that night I climbed to a rooftop café overlooking the square and ordered a glass of mint tea. In the distance, the Koutoubia Mosque's minaret glowed under warm floodlight — its proportions, a height five times its base width, are now considered the golden ratio of North African Islamic architecture, and I realized the brick facade I'd seen that afternoon at the Yves Saint Laurent Museum extended, in some sense, from that same logic. I scrolled back through the day's photos — the riad's courtyard tiles, the carved plaster of Bahia Palace, the cobalt walls of Majorelle, the dyers' colored wool, the smoke and lights of the square — and it struck me that these were all dialects of the same language: how to use repetition, symmetry, and the imperfection of the human hand to hold both order and everyday life in a single frame. I finished my tea while the noise of the square still rose from below, already certain I'd come back tomorrow — five days was clearly not enough to read all of this city's patterns.
