中文

飛機降落在艾爾普拉特機場時是傍晚六點半,機艙門一打開,一股混著海水鹽味與曬熱柏油的空氣撲面而來,那是我對這座城市九月的第一個印象。原本我以為盛夏的人潮已經散去,巴塞隆納會安靜下來,但搭上機場快線A2巴士進城的路上,司機廣播裡提到這週是「La Mercè」,巴塞隆納守護聖母梅爾切的節日週,我才意識到自己撞上了整座城市一年最喧鬧的七天。

放下行李已是晚上八點多,天色還亮著,地中海的九月太陽要到快九點才肯落下。我沿著蘭布拉大道(La Rambla)往海邊走,梧桐樹葉在頭頂沙沙作響,街頭藝人塗成銀色的雕像在夕陽裡發著光,空氣裡飄著剛炸好的丘羅斯混著肉桂糖的甜味。轉進哥德區的巷子,牆上已經貼滿了節慶海報,寫著人塔表演、火跑遊行、煙火晚會的時間表——我才意識到,原來這趟旅行不是我計畫好的,而是這座城市替我排的。

九月的巴塞隆納,夏末的邀請帖

巴塞隆納的九月不是觀光季的尾聲,而是這座城市卸下防備、對著自己人和陌生人一起放聲大笑的那一週。

蘭布拉大道到聖雅克廣場:一整天追著人塔跑

隔天早上我七點半就醒了,並不是被鬧鐘吵醒,而是窗外傳來遠處鼓聲和風笛聲的排練聲。我查了節目表,發現當天下午五點在市政廳前的聖雅克廣場(Plaça Sant Jaume)有castells人塔表演,是整個La Mercè節慶裡最重要的傳統項目之一——這種疊人塔的技藝源自加泰隆尼亞內陸的塔拉戈納地區,已經被列為聯合國非物質文化遺產。

為了佔到好位置,我下午三點就從住宿的El Born區出發,沿著Carrer de la Princesa走到Via Laietana,再穿過哥德區窄巷抵達廣場,全程走了大概二十五分鐘。廣場上早已圍起護欄,人群比我想像中密集三倍,我後來才知道當地人會提早一小時來卡位,而且會特別站在靠近護欄卻不擋視線的側邊角落——我跟一位帶著兩個小孩的巴塞隆納媽媽學到這招,她說「站正中間人最多,側邊反而看得清楚」。

下午五點整,鼓聲響起,穿著不同顏色隊服的colles(人塔隊伍)開始堆疊,最底層是壯漢們手臂交纏成「pinya」基座,中層的人踩著彼此的肩膀往上爬,最頂端往往是七、八歲的小孩,穩穩站上塔頂舉起手臂謝幕的那一刻,整個廣場的人會同時吸一口氣然後爆出掌聲。我全程沒有買票,這是免費的市民活動,但如果想要更靠近舞台,有些旅行社會賣觀景台座位,大約每人25到35歐元。整場表演加上等待,我在廣場站了快三個小時,腿很痠,但那種全場屏息又同時歡呼的節奏感,是我在任何博物館都感受不到的巴塞隆納脈動。

波格利亞市場與Bar Pinotxo的午後時光

看完人塔已經是晚上七點多,肚子早就餓得咕咕叫,但我學到一個教訓:La Mercè節慶期間很多市場攤位提早收攤去參加遊行,所以我把重頭戲留在第三天早上——專程八點半就到波格利亞市場(Mercat de la Boqueria),那時候遊客還沒湧入,市場裡瀰漫著新鮮沙丁魚的海味、切開的伊比利火腿油脂香,還有現榨柳橙汁灑在地上的酸甜氣味。

我直接走到市場中段那家永遠排隊的Bar Pinotxo,老闆家族的人在吧台前煎著東西,我在吧台邊找到一個空位坐下,點了一份煎小卷佐豆子(sepionets amb mongetes)跟一杯冰涼的Vermut,一份小卷大約12歐元,Vermut是3.5歐元。小卷外皮煎得焦脆,咬下去卻是柔軟帶點回甘的海味,配上溫熱的白豆吸滿了橄欖油與蒜香,吃到最後我用麵包把盤底醬汁刮乾淨,老闆看到笑著遞給我第二片麵包,說「這才是對的吃法」。

吧台旁邊坐著的是一位每天固定來吃早午餐的當地老先生,他跟我說La Mercè那幾天,市場裡的老饕反而更愛清晨來,因為傍晚遊行一開始,整條蘭布拉大道會封街,連走路都困難。他建議我隔天可以去El Xampanyet吃晚餐——那是一間開業快百年的卡瓦酒吧,牆上還貼著老海報,我後來去了,點了鯖魚醃菜(escabetx)跟一杯冰香檳酒Cava,一份小點大概2.5到4歐元不等,配上微甜氣泡的卡瓦酒,那種站著喝酒聊天的隨性感,才是巴塞隆納人下班後真正的日常。

在波格利亞市場學到的第一件事:好味道不需要裝飾,只需要新鮮跟耐心排隊的勇氣。

火跑之夜:與當地奶奶Dolors的對話

La Mercè節慶第三天晚上十點,我特地換上長袖外套跟包住脖子的圍巾,因為當晚有correfoc(火跑)——一群裝扮成惡魔的表演者,舉著會噴火花的煙火棒,在狹窄的哥德區街道裡邊跑邊甩,觀眾則要站在旁邊接受火花的洗禮,這是整個節慶裡最狂野也最需要膽量的儀式。

我站在Carrer de Ferran街邊,身旁是一位七十多歲的奶奶Dolors,她穿著特意準備的舊棉外套跟頭巾,笑著跟我說她從六歲就開始參加火跑,「以前沒有這麼多規則,現在他們會提醒大家戴帽子蓋頭髮,但那種被火花濺到手臂、聞到硫磺味的刺激感,幾十年來沒有變過。」她告訴我火跑的傳統來自中世紀對抗惡靈的儀式,「惡魔」代表混亂與試煉,而聖梅爾切正是拯救巴塞隆納脫離瘟疫與摩爾人侵略的守護聖母,九月二十四日就是紀念她的正日。

鼓聲越來越密集,煙火棒點燃的瞬間空氣裡瀰漫著濃濃的硫磺味跟燒焦的紙屑味,火花打在我的手臂上像被熱沙輕輕彈了一下,不痛卻讓人心跳加速,整條街的人一起發出尖叫又大笑的聲音,那種集體亢奮很難用言語形容。Dolors在我耳邊喊著:「這就是巴塞隆納人放掉一整年壓力的方法!」表演結束後,街上瀰漫著硝煙味跟濕黏的汗水味,地上鋪滿了燒焦的紙屑,清潔隊員已經準備好水車,凌晨前就會把整條街刷洗乾淨,彷彿什麼都沒發生過。

蒙特惠奇的煙火尾聲,以及我對四季節慶的建議

節慶最後一晚,我搭公車再轉纜車上蒙特惠奇山(Montjuïc),晚上九點半,山頂國家宮前的魔幻噴泉(Font Màgica)廣場已經被人群佔滿,大家席地而坐,手裡拿著路邊小攤買的罐裝啤酒跟三明治,等待十點整的煙火秀跟最終的piromusical(音樂煙火)表演。

煙火綻放的瞬間,整座山谷被染成金色跟紫色,底下是燈火通明的巴塞隆納市區,遠處還能看到聖家堂的塔尖在夜色裡發光。煙火結束後,巴塞隆納人會跳起沙丁娜舞(sardana)——大家手牽手圍成一圈,伴著風笛跟小鼓緩慢起舞,舞步簡單卻帶著一種莊重的儀式感,連我這種外國人被拉進圈子裡,也能很快跟上腳步。

如果你問我什麼時候來巴塞隆納最好,我會說答案取決於你想要哪種巴塞隆納。四月二十三日的聖喬治節(Sant Jordi)是最溫柔浪漫的時刻,蘭布拉大道跟哥羅街會擺滿玫瑰花跟書攤,情侶互送一本書跟一朵玫瑰,春天的陽光跟橙花香氣讓整座城市像被輕輕擁抱;八月的Festes de Gràcia則是巷弄裡最有創意的一週,格拉西亞區的居民會用回收材料把自己的街道裝飾成海底世界或森林場景,參加街頭裝飾比賽,那種鄰里之間比創意的溫馨感,跟九月La Mercè的全城狂歡完全是兩種節奏。而九月的La Mercè,正是我體驗到的,巴塞隆納人卸下遊客濾鏡,把整座城市交還給自己人,又順手拉你一起加入的那種毫無保留的熱情。

如果春天的巴塞隆納教你戀愛,那麼九月的巴塞隆納,教你怎麼跟一整座城市一起燃燒。

離開的那天清晨六點,我拖著行李走過還殘留著煙火硝煙味的蘭布拉大道,清潔車正沖洗著昨晚的碎紙屑,一位麵包店老闆已經拉起鐵門,烤爬滿杏仁片的可頌香氣飄了出來。城市又恢復日常的呼吸,但我知道,那一週我曾經真正跟這座城市一起心跳過。

ENGLISH

The plane touched down at El Prat around six-thirty in the evening, and the moment the cabin door opened, a gust of salt air mixed with sun-baked tarmac hit me square in the face. That was my first impression of Barcelona in September. I had assumed the summer crowds would be thinning out, that the city would be settling into a quieter rhythm. But on the A2 airport bus into town, the driver's radio mentioned something called La Mercè, the week-long festival honoring the city's patron saint. I realized, somewhat helplessly, that I had landed in the middle of the loudest seven days of Barcelona's entire year.

By the time I dropped my bags it was past eight, and the sky was still bright — the Mediterranean sun in September doesn't bother setting until nearly nine. I walked down La Rambla toward the sea, plane leaves rustling overhead, a silver-painted human statue glowing in the low light, the sweet smell of fresh churros and cinnamon sugar drifting through the air. Turning into the alleys of the Gothic Quarter, I found walls already plastered with festival posters — human tower performances, fire-runs, fireworks schedules. This trip, I realized, wasn't the one I had planned. It was the one the city had scheduled for me.

September in Barcelona: An Invitation at Summer's Edge

September in Barcelona isn't the tail end of tourist season — it's the one week the city lets its guard down and laughs, loudly, with locals and strangers alike.

From La Rambla to Plaça Sant Jaume: A Day Spent Chasing Human Towers

I woke at seven-thirty the next morning, not to an alarm but to the distant sound of drums and gralla pipes being rehearsed somewhere down the street. Checking the festival program, I found that at five that afternoon, castells — human towers — would be performed in Plaça Sant Jaume, right in front of the city hall. This is one of the most important traditions of the entire La Mercè festival, a discipline that originated in the inland region of Tarragona and has since been recognized by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage.

To secure a decent spot, I left my apartment in El Born at three in the afternoon, walking down Carrer de la Princesa to Via Laietana, then cutting through the narrow lanes of the Gothic Quarter — about twenty-five minutes total. The square was already fenced off, and the crowd was three times denser than I'd expected. I later learned locals arrive at least an hour early and deliberately position themselves near the barrier but off to the side, where the view is clearer than dead center. A Barcelona mother standing next to me with two kids taught me this trick: 'Everyone crowds the middle. Stand to the side and you'll actually see something.'

At exactly five o'clock the drums picked up, and colles — tower teams dressed in different colored shirts — began to build. At the base, burly men and women linked arms into a tightly packed foundation called the pinya. Climbers scrambled up over shoulders and backs, and at the very top, almost always a child no older than seven or eight, who would stand steady for a few seconds and raise an arm in salute. The entire square inhaled at once, then erupted. The whole thing is free to watch as a public event, though some agencies sell grandstand seating for roughly 25 to 35 euros. Between waiting and watching, I stood in that square for nearly three hours, legs aching, but that collective held breath followed by an explosion of cheering was a pulse of the city I never felt in any museum.

An Afternoon at La Boqueria and Bar Pinotxo

By the time the towers came down it was past seven, and my stomach was growling, but I'd learned a lesson: during La Mercè, many market stalls close early so vendors can join the parades. So I saved the real meal for the third morning, arriving at Mercat de la Boqueria at eight-thirty sharp, before the tourist waves rolled in. The market smelled of fresh sardines, the oily richness of sliced Ibérico ham, and the sharp sweetness of orange juice splashed across the tiled floor.

I walked straight to Bar Pinotxo, the perpetually busy counter halfway through the market, where a member of the founding family was still frying away behind the bar. I found one open stool and ordered sepionets amb mongetes — pan-seared baby squid with white beans — along with a chilled glass of vermut. The squid came to about 12 euros, the vermut 3.50. The squid was crisp on the outside, tender and faintly sweet on the inside, and the warm white beans had soaked up all the olive oil and garlic in the pan. By the end I was wiping the plate clean with bread, and the owner, catching me in the act, laughed and handed me a second piece. 'That's the correct way to eat this,' he said.

Sitting beside me was an elderly regular who came in for brunch every single day. He told me that during La Mercè, market regulars actually come earlier than usual, because once the evening parades begin, La Rambla gets sealed off and even walking becomes difficult. He recommended I try El Xampanyet for dinner the next night — a cava bar nearly a century old, its walls still covered in faded old posters. I went. I ordered pickled mackerel, escabetx, and a glass of cold cava, small plates running 2.50 to 4 euros each. Standing at the counter, drinking lightly sweet, fizzing cava while chatting with strangers — that, more than any sit-down dinner, felt like the real after-work ritual of Barcelona.

The first lesson I learned at La Boqueria: good flavor doesn't need decoration, only freshness and the patience to stand in line.

Night of Fire: A Conversation with Dolors

On the third night of La Mercè, at ten o'clock, I put on a long-sleeved jacket and wrapped a scarf around my neck, because that evening was the correfoc — the fire-run. Performers dressed as devils carry sparking fireworks through the narrow streets of the Gothic Quarter, running and swinging them close to the crowd, and spectators are expected to stand right there and take the sparks as a kind of baptism. It's the wildest, most nerve-testing ritual of the entire festival.

I stood on Carrer de Ferran next to a woman in her seventies named Dolors, dressed in a deliberately old cotton jacket and a headscarf. She laughed and told me she'd been running this fire-run since she was six years old. 'There weren't so many rules back then. Now they remind everyone to cover their hair, but the thrill of sparks landing on your arm, the smell of sulfur — that hasn't changed in decades.' She explained that the tradition traces back to medieval rituals warding off evil spirits, with the devils representing chaos and trial, while Our Lady of Mercè is the patron saint who is said to have saved the city from plague and Moorish invasion — September 24th being her feast day.

The drumming grew denser, and the moment the fireworks were lit the air filled with the heavy smell of sulfur and burning paper. Sparks landed on my arm like a light flick of hot sand — not painful, but enough to make my heart race — and the whole street screamed and laughed in unison, a collective euphoria hard to put into words. Dolors shouted into my ear, 'This is how Barcelona lets go of a whole year of stress!' When it ended, the street smelled of gunpowder and sweat, the ground littered with scorched confetti, and water trucks were already lined up, ready to scrub the street clean before dawn as if nothing had happened at all.

Fireworks over Montjuïc, and My Advice on Barcelona's Four Seasons

On the last night of the festival, I took a bus and then the cable car up to Montjuïc. By nine-thirty, the plaza in front of the Magic Fountain, Font Màgica, was already packed, everyone sitting on the ground with canned beer and sandwiches from the nearby kiosks, waiting for the ten o'clock fireworks and the final piromusical, a synchronized music-and-fireworks show.

When the fireworks burst, the whole valley turned gold and violet, the city glittering below, and in the distance the spires of Sagrada Família caught the light. Afterward, Barcelonans formed circles and danced the sardana — hands joined, moving in slow, deliberate steps to the sound of pipes and small drums. The steps are simple but carry a kind of quiet solemnity, and even a foreigner like me, pulled into the circle, could pick it up within a few turns.

If you ask me when to visit Barcelona, my answer depends on which version of the city you want. April 23rd, Sant Jordi's Day, is the gentlest and most romantic time — La Rambla and Carrer dels Comtes fill with rose stalls and book stands, couples exchange a book and a single rose, and the spring sunlight and orange-blossom scent make the whole city feel like a soft embrace. August's Festes de Gràcia is the most inventive week, when neighbors in the Gràcia district transform their streets into underwater worlds or forest scenes using recycled materials, competing for the best-decorated block — a warm, communal creativity that moves at a completely different pace from September's citywide frenzy. And September's La Mercè, the one I lived through, is the week Barcelona drops its tourist filter entirely and hands the city back to its own people — then, almost as an afterthought, pulls you in too.

If spring in Barcelona teaches you how to fall in love, September teaches you how to burn together with an entire city.

On the morning I left, at six o'clock, I dragged my suitcase down a La Rambla still faintly smelling of spent fireworks, a street-cleaning truck washing away the last of the confetti, a bakery owner already rolling up his shutters, the smell of almond croissants drifting out into the cool air. The city had gone back to its ordinary breathing. But I knew that for one week, my own heartbeat had actually kept time with the city's.
