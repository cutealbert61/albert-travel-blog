中文

清晨五點半的京都,我拉開旅館房間的紙拉門,窗外還是一片墨藍色,連烏鴉都還沒開始叫。這是我第三次造訪這座城市,前兩次都在伏見稻荷的千本鳥居下跟著旅行團人潮龜速前進,拍了跟明信片一模一樣的照片就離開,回家後翻照片,竟想不起當下聞到什麼、聽到什麼。這一次,我決定把行程表丟掉,改成問民宿老闆娘、居酒屋老闆、還有在惠文社書店打工的女孩:「你們自己放假都去哪裡?」得到的答案,幾乎沒有一個是教科書上會寫的地名。於是有了這趟旅程。

清晨五點半,貴船到鞍馬:比千本鳥居更安靜的千年山徑

從出町柳站搭上叡山電鐵,車廂裡只有我跟兩位揹著登山杖的歐巴桑,窗外的鴨川支流漸漸退成一條銀線。三十分鐘後在貴船口站下車,單程票價四百七十日圓。清晨的空氣冷得讓人清醒,沿著貴船川往上走二十分鐘,兩側是還沒開始營業的川床料理棚架,夏天時這裡擠滿在河面上用餐的遊客,但六點鐘的此刻只剩水聲潺潺,連老闆都還在裡頭掃地。

貴船神社的紅色燈籠沿著石階一路往上排開,晨光還沒完全爬進來,燈籠底下的石階泛著濕氣的光。神社參拜免費,但我多花了兩百日圓求了一支「水占卜」籤詩——把白紙浸到神社境內的靈泉裡,字才會慢慢浮現,這是貴船神社獨有的儀式,據說水占卜起源自這裡奉祀的水神。從貴船神社側邊有一條通往鞍馬寺的登山道,入山需要在鞍馬寺本殿處奉納三百日圓「愛山費」,山路是原始的杉木林,樹根盤根錯節裸露在步道上,傳說是天狗修行之地,叫做「木の根道」。走這段路大約需要一小時,我全程只遇到一位牽著柴犬散步的當地阿伯,他跟我點頭致意,狗尾巴搖得比我還開心。

抵達鞍馬寺本殿時,山谷間的霧氣還沒散,底下的鞍馬川傳來水聲,完全聽不到任何遊覽車引擎聲。下山可以選擇纜車(單程兩百日圓,實際上是奉獻性質而非正式收費營運),也可以像我一樣繼續走山路下到鞍馬車站,再搭叡電回出町柳。整趟行程含交通、拜觀、纜車大約花費一千五百日圓,來回將近三個半小時,建議穿好走的鞋、自備水,山上完全沒有便利商店,而且步道在下午五點後就會封閉。

京都最好的風景,常常不在門票裡,而在你願意早起的那三十分鐘。

出町柳的味道:清晨排隊三十分鐘,只為一顆還溫熱的豆餅

回到出町柳站已經接近九點,我沿著河合橋走向出町商店街,還沒轉進巷口就先看到排隊的人龍——出町ふたば的豆餅,是京都人從阿嬤那一代就在吃的日常點心。排隊的清一色是提著竹籃的婆婆媽媽跟騎腳踏車來的上班族,幾乎看不到觀光客的臉孔,大家安靜地滑手機或聊天,店家木造招牌被歲月磨得發亮。

排了大約半小時,終於買到一顆還溫熱的豆餅,兩百日圓。外皮是帶著淡淡鹹味的麻糬,裡頭嵌滿整顆整顆的赤豌豆,咬下去先是麻糬的軟糯黏牙,接著豆子的鹹香跟紅豆內餡的甜味在嘴裡打架,那種鹹甜交錯的層次,是便利商店大福完全模仿不來的手感。我捧著豆餅走到鴨川與高野川的交會處,在三角洲的石階坐下,一邊吃一邊看晨跑的人跟練太極拳的老先生,河水在腳邊分岔成兩條,陽光終於整片灑下來。出町ふたば每週三公休,不定期也會臨時休息,想吃到最新鮮的豆餅,建議上午十點前抵達。

詩仙堂與一乘寺:讀書人的秘密角落

吃完豆餅,我搭公車轉往一乘寺,先鑽進惠文社一乘寺店翻書,店內選書偏冷門,還附設藝廊跟生活雜貨區,店員推薦我一本京都在地攝影師的獨立出版物,我買下當紀念品,一本一千八百日圓。接著步行十分鐘到詩仙堂,拜觀費五百日圓,踏進玄關那一刻,「咚」的一聲鹿威し敲擊石頭的聲響從庭院深處傳來,清脆又悠長,是整間屋子唯一的聲音。

坐在鋪著榻榻米的廣間,望著修剪成雲朵形狀的杜鵑花叢,一位京都本地的老先生就坐在我斜對角,闔眼靜坐了將近二十分鐘,期間只有那一聲聲鹿威し規律地敲著時間。相較於金閣寺前的人潮推擠,這裡安靜得讓人有點不好意思講話,連呼吸都放輕。

安靜,原來也是一種需要練習才能聽懂的京都語言。

嵯峨野深處,化野念佛寺:與觀光巴士擦身而過的寂靜

下午兩點,我特地避開嵯峨野竹林最擁擠的時段,搭公車到嵯峨釋迦堂前站,遠遠就看到遊覽車一台接一台卸下遊客往竹林湧去。我反其道而行,往北走過二尊院、祇王寺,沿著嵯峨鳥居本這條保留茅草屋頂茶屋的老街繼續走,大約二十分鐘後抵達化野念佛寺,拜觀費五百日圓。

寺內的「賽之河原」聚集了約八千尊大小不一的石佛與地藏像,原本是散落在嵯峨野荒地的無名塚,明治時期才被集中安置在此,每年八月會舉行「千灯供養」,點起數千支蠟燭祭祀亡靈,場面莊嚴到讓人起雞皮疙瘩。我去的當下是平日午後,現場只有一位老僧人在掃落葉,竹葉沙沙作響,風吹過石佛的縫隙發出低鳴,那種寂靜跟稍早竹林入口的喧鬧簡直是兩個世界。

祇園白川的黃昏:一場地藏盆教我的規矩

傍晚五點半,我沿著白川南通散步,柳樹垂進水面,倒影被路燈染成淡黃色,遊客明顯比花見小路少了一半。走進巷弄深處,意外撞見一場「地藏盆」——附近町內的小型地藏尊前掛滿彩色布幔跟提燈,幾個孩子圍坐在小桌前玩套圈圈遊戲,幾位婆婆媽媽忙著分裝糖果餅乾。一位站在旁邊乘涼的阿姨看我一臉好奇,主動用簡單的英文夾雜日文跟我解釋:地藏盆是每年八月底,關西地區特有的社區祭典,感謝守護孩童的地藏菩薩,由町內居民輪流張羅供品,請和尚來誦經一次,整條巷子的大人小孩都會出來聚一聚,「這不是給觀光客看的祭典」,她笑著補充,「連很多外地日本人都沒聽過」。

我小心地站在巷口沒有靠太近拍照,她提醒我地藏盆是社區私人的場合,拍孩子的臉最好先問過家長。轉出巷子沒多久,遇上一位穿著正式和服的舞妓匆匆走過,幾位觀光客追著她伸手機拍照,擋住整條巷子的去路,舞妓低著頭加快腳步閃避,那畫面跟剛才地藏盆裡的溫暖形成強烈對比——同一條白川,兩種截然不同的京都。

先斗町巷尾的小酒館:寫給還沒抵達的你

晚上八點,我鑽進先斗町一條窄得兩人交錯要側身而過的小巷,找到一間只有六個座位的立飲屋,點了一杯角瓶兌水的Highball跟三串烤雞肉串,一共大約一千六百日圓。老闆是土生土長的京都人,在這裡站了將近四十年吧台,他一邊烤肉串一邊跟我說,真正的京都從來不在明信片封面上,而是巷子後面那條更窄的巷子。我啜著微苦的Highball,想起清晨的杉木林、還溫熱的豆餅、詩仙堂裡那聲鹿威し,還有地藏盆巷口那位阿姨的笑容,忽然覺得,這趟旅程比前兩次加起來都更靠近京都一點點。

觀光客走大路,京都人走小巷——而最好的故事,永遠藏在小巷盡頭那盞昏黃的燈下。

離開先斗町時已近十點,巷口的燈籠一盞盞亮著,我知道自己還會再回來,只是下一次,大概又會被某個居酒屋老闆指去另一個誰也沒聽過的地方。

ENGLISH

It was five-thirty in the morning when I slid open the paper screen of my ryokan window and found Kyoto still the color of ink, not even the crows awake yet. This was my third visit to this city, and the first two had ended the same way — shuffling behind tour groups under the vermilion gates of Fushimi Inari, taking the exact photo everyone else takes, then flying home and realizing I couldn't remember a single smell or sound from the trip. This time I threw out the itinerary and started asking instead: the woman running my guesthouse, the owner of a tiny izakaya, a girl working the register at Keibunsha bookstore — where do you go on your day off? None of the answers matched anything in a guidebook. This is the trip that came out of that question.

5:30 AM, Kibune to Kurama: A Thousand-Year Trail Quieter Than Any Torii Gate

I caught the Eizan Electric Railway from Demachiyanagi Station, sharing the carriage with only two elderly women carrying hiking poles. Thirty minutes later, for a fare of about 470 yen, I stepped off at Kibuneguchi Station into air so cold it snapped me fully awake. The walk up along the Kibune River took twenty minutes, past the wooden platforms where restaurants build their famous riverside dining decks in summer — empty now except for one owner sweeping the floor before opening.

Red lanterns lined the stone steps up to Kifune Shrine, the morning light not yet strong enough to reach the ground beneath them. Entry is free, but I paid 200 yen for a water fortune slip — a paper that reveals its message only after you float it on the shrine's sacred spring, a ritual unique to this shrine dedicated to the water deity. A trail beside the shrine leads over the mountain to Kurama-dera, where hikers leave a 300-yen 'mountain protection fee' at the temple. The path cuts through raw cedar forest, roots exposed and knotted across the ground in a section locals call the Root Path, said to be where mountain spirits once trained. It took about an hour, and the only soul I passed was an old man walking his Shiba dog, who nodded at me while his dog's tail wagged with far more enthusiasm than either of us.

By the time I reached the main hall of Kurama-dera, mist still hung in the valley below, and the only sound was the river far beneath — no tour bus engines, no crowd noise, nothing. Getting down, you can take the funicular for a symbolic 200 yen, or walk the trail all the way to Kurama Station as I did, then catch the train back. All in, the whole loop — transport, temple donations, funicular — cost about 1,500 yen and took roughly three and a half hours. Wear real hiking shoes, carry your own water since there are no convenience stores on the mountain, and note the trail gates close at five in the evening.

The best views in Kyoto are rarely behind a ticket booth — they're waiting in the thirty minutes you were willing to wake up early for.

Demachiyanagi: A Half-Hour Queue for One Warm Rice Cake

By nine, I was back near Demachiyanagi, walking toward the Demachi shopping arcade, and spotted the line before I even turned the corner — the queue for Demachi Futaba, a mochi shop Kyoto grandmothers have been visiting since long before I was born. Everyone in line carried bamboo baskets or had bicycles parked nearby; there wasn't a single tourist face among them. People scrolled their phones quietly or chatted in low voices, and the wooden sign above the door had the worn shine of decades of hands pushing it open.

After about thirty minutes, I finally held a still-warm mame-mochi in my hand for 200 yen. The outer layer is lightly salted rice cake, studded all over with whole red field peas, and biting through it gives you first the soft chewy pull of the mochi, then the salty crunch of the beans fighting with the sweetness of the red bean paste inside — a layered contrast no convenience-store daifuku could ever fake. I carried mine to the delta where the Kamo and Takano Rivers meet, sat on the stone steps, and ate while watching joggers and an old man practicing tai chi as the sun finally spilled fully across the water. The shop is closed on Wednesdays and occasionally without notice, so arriving before ten in the morning is your best bet for a fresh batch.

Shisen-do and Ichijoji: A Reader's Secret Corner

After the mochi, I took a bus to Ichijoji and ducked into Keibunsha, an independent bookstore known for its unusual curation, complete with a small gallery and lifestyle goods corner. A staff member recommended a photo book by a local Kyoto photographer, which I bought for 1,800 yen. Ten minutes' walk brought me to Shisen-do, where the 500-yen entry fee opens onto a garden so quiet that the moment I stepped into the entryway, a single crack of the shishi-odoshi — the bamboo deer-scarer — echoed from somewhere deep in the garden, sharp and resonant, the only sound in the whole building.

Sitting on the tatami in the main hall, looking out at azalea bushes trimmed into cloud shapes, an elderly Kyoto man sat diagonally across from me, eyes closed for nearly twenty minutes, with only that periodic clack of the deer-scarer marking time. Compared to the shoving crowds outside Kinkaku-ji, this place was quiet enough that I felt almost embarrassed to breathe too loudly.

Silence, it turns out, is a language of Kyoto you have to practice listening to before you can hear it.

Deep in Sagano: Adashino Nenbutsu-ji, Steps Away from the Tour Buses

At two in the afternoon, I deliberately avoided the busiest hours at the Arashiyama bamboo grove, getting off the bus at Saga Shakado-mae and watching, from a distance, bus after bus unloading crowds heading straight for the bamboo. I went the opposite direction — north past Nison-in and Gio-ji temples, along Saga Toriimoto, a preserved street of thatched-roof teahouses, and after about twenty minutes arrived at Adashino Nenbutsu-ji, entry 500 yen.

Inside, roughly eight thousand small stone Buddha and Jizo statues are gathered in a field called Sai-no-Kawara — originally scattered, unclaimed graves across Sagano, consolidated here during the Meiji era. Every August the temple holds a candle ceremony called Sento Kuyo, lighting thousands of candles for the dead, said to be overwhelming to witness. On the ordinary weekday afternoon I visited, there was only one old monk sweeping fallen bamboo leaves, the rustle of leaves and a low moan of wind through the gaps between statues — a stillness that felt like an entirely different world from the noisy bamboo grove entrance twenty minutes away.

Dusk on the Shirakawa Canal: What a Jizo-bon Festival Taught Me

At five-thirty, I wandered along Shirakawa Minami-dori, willow branches trailing into the water, their reflections dyed pale yellow by the streetlamps, with noticeably fewer tourists than on nearby Hanamikoji. Deeper into a side alley, I stumbled onto a small Jizo-bon gathering — a neighborhood's little Jizo statue draped in colorful fabric and paper lanterns, children sitting around a low table playing ring-toss games, and a few older women handing out candy and crackers. A woman standing nearby, noticing my curiosity, explained in a mix of simple English and Japanese that Jizo-bon is a distinctly Kansai neighborhood festival held every late August, thanking the Jizo bodhisattva for protecting children — residents take turns preparing offerings, a monk comes once a year to chant sutras, and the whole street's families gather. 'This isn't for tourists,' she smiled, 'even a lot of Japanese from other regions have never heard of it.'

I stayed at the mouth of the alley rather than stepping closer, and she gently reminded me that Jizo-bon is a private community occasion — better to ask parents before photographing any children. Just past that alley, a maiko in full formal dress hurried by, a cluster of tourists chasing her with phones held high, blocking the entire lane. She kept her head down and quickened her pace to get past them — a scene sharply at odds with the warmth I'd just witnessed, two entirely different versions of Kyoto living on the same canal.

A Nightcap at the End of Pontocho

By eight that evening, I squeezed into one of Pontocho's alleys so narrow two people have to turn sideways to pass, and found a standing bar with only six seats. I ordered a whisky highball and three skewers of grilled chicken, about 1,600 yen altogether. The owner, born and raised in Kyoto, had been standing behind that same counter for nearly forty years. Grilling the skewers, he told me that the real Kyoto never shows up on any postcard — it's the alley behind the alley. Sipping my slightly bitter highball, I thought back through the cedar forest at dawn, the still-warm mochi, the crack of the deer-scarer at Shisen-do, and the smile of the woman at the Jizo-bon gathering, and realized this trip had brought me closer to Kyoto than my previous two visits combined.

Tourists take the main road; Kyoto's own people take the side alley — and the best stories are always waiting under the dim lantern at the very end of it.

It was nearly ten by the time I left Pontocho, lanterns lighting up one by one along the alley behind me. I already know I'll come back — only next time, some izakaya owner will probably point me toward somewhere even I haven't heard of yet.
