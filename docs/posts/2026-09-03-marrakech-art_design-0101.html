中文

清晨六點,天色還泛著一層淺灰,我從麥地那深處一間叫Dar Zaman的老宅走出來,巷弄裡只剩下清潔工用水管沖刷石板路的聲音。水氣混著昨夜殘留的炭火與孜然味道,在空氣裡發酵成一種很難形容、卻絕對屬於摩洛哥的氣味。出發前我讀了不少介紹,說這裡是香料之城、撒哈拉的門戶,但真正走進巷弄才發現,這座城市對我而言首先是一座活的美術館——手工鑿刻的灰泥雕花、幾何拼貼的釉面磚、深淺不一的赭紅色夯土牆,每一寸牆面都在說話。接下來六天,我沒有排博物館清單,而是讓自己被建築、色彩與工匠的手帶著走,結果意外拼出一趟比任何行程表都完整的藝術之旅。

清晨的麥地那——牆面上的幾何學

七點半,Souk Semmarine還沒開張,鐵捲門一片片拉起的金屬聲取代了白天的人聲鼎沸,我趁這個空檔鑽進了離廣場不遠的Ben Youssef古蘭經學院。院子中央是一方淺淺的倒影水池,晨光斜斜打在四面牆上,我第一次近距離看清楚什麼叫zellige——那些以手工切割的小片釉面磚,拼成八角星形的khatim sulayman圖案,師傅告訴我這個圖案在伊斯蘭美學裡代表無限,因為星芒可以無止盡地向外延伸,象徵真主的無邊無際。牆面下段是磚,中段是灰泥雕花(gebs),上段才是雪松木刻的可蘭經文字,三種材質、三種工序,卻在幾百年前由不識字的工匠一鑿一鑿完成。入場門票50迪爾漢,我在裡面待了將近一個半小時,直到遊覽團的喧鬧聲從門口湧進來,才心不甘情不願地離開。

這座城市不靠畫框展示藝術,它把藝術直接砌進牆裡、踩在腳下、刻進天花板的陰影裡。

馬約爾花園與聖羅蘭的藍色秘境

離開老城,我特別想記錄下實際的路線,因為出發前查資料時被各種眾說紛紜搞得有點焦慮。我從麥地那的Riad走到新城區Gueliz的馬約爾花園(Jardin Majorelle),全程步行大約25分鐘,穿過Avenue Mohammed V的車水馬龍,說實話比想像中好走,路上還經過好幾間法式風格的甜點店。我提前三天在官網訂了合併套票(花園150迪爾漢加上YSL博物館100迪爾漢),早上八點五十分抵達,開門時間是九點,現場已經排了將近四十人的隊伍,幸好我有預先購票的QR code,直接走另一條快速通道,省下至少半小時。一走進花園,那種鈷藍色瞬間把我震住——這是畫家Jacques Majorelle在1930年代自行調配的顏色,後來被正式命名為Majorelle Blue,塗滿了整座花園裡的建築與陶盆。竹林、仙人掌園、錦鯉池交錯著,鳥叫聲此起彼落,我大概逛了一個小時,才走到隔壁由法國建築事務所Studio KO操刀的YSL博物館。館體外牆用磚紅色的水泥格柵拼出類似織品經緯的紋理,白天光線透過格柵灑進中庭,像被切割過的陽光。館內禁止拍照,展出聖羅蘭從1962到2002年間受摩洛哥色彩啟發的手繪設計稿,配著低沉的柏柏爾傳統音樂,整個空間安靜得像座聖殿。整趟行程含排隊、參觀花園與博物館共花了兩個半小時,我的建議是:務必避開中午十二點到下午兩點這段時間,人潮與高溫都會讓體驗大打折扣。

巴伊亞宮:雪松木上的伊斯蘭幾何

下午三點,我又回到麥地那,前往1866年為攝政大臣Si Moussa興建的巴伊亞宮。這座宮殿沒有正立面的氣勢,反而是靠著一進又一進的庭院層層堆疊出戲劇性——走進去之前完全猜不到裡面藏著多大的空間。四位妻子各自的居所圍繞著一座種滿橘子樹的中庭,空氣裡飄著淡淡的橙花香混著鴿子拍翅膀的聲音。最讓我停下腳步的是天花板:雪松木雕刻的穹頂(muqarnas)層層堆疊出蜂窩狀的立體結構,漆著番紅花黃與薄荷綠,午後的陽光穿過鏤空木窗(mashrabiya)灑在腳下的釉面磚地板上,光影隨著雲朵飄過不斷變形,我站在原地看了快十分鐘,直到後面遊客催促才挪步。入場票70迪爾漢,我特別選在開門後的九點半到,比起下午擠滿旅行團的狀況安靜太多,如果只能選一個時段參觀,我會建議清晨。

廣場上的滋味,染坊巷的星期五

傍晚六點半,我準時報到Jemaa el-Fnaa廣場,這時候攤販正一個個推著木頭餐車進場,炭火點燃的煙霧混著孜然、烤肉油脂的香氣瀰漫整個廣場。我找到一個攤主自稱Said的攤位坐下,先來一碗harira扁豆番茄湯,10迪爾漢,濃稠帶點檸檬酸香,配著一片剛烤好的msemen千層薄餅沾蜂蜜,外酥內軟。接著點了一份烤kefta肉串,35迪爾漢,肉質多汁帶著孜然與紅椒粉的辛香,附上一小碟醃漬橄欖與辣醬harissa。廣場另一頭有位老先生守著一大鍋冒著蒸氣的蝸牛湯(babbouche),湯頭用茴香、肉桂與十幾種香料熬煮,我鼓起勇氣點了一小碗,5迪爾漢,湯喝起來像藥膳,蝸牛肉Q彈,配著旁邊敲鼓賣藝人的節奏,整頓晚餐加上一杯5迪爾漢的現榨柳橙汁,總共不到80迪爾漢,卻是我這趟旅程吃過最有戲劇張力的一餐。

隔天早上我特地去了城郊的染坊街區(Tanneries),還沒走近就聞到刺鼻的氣味,一位皮革工坊老闆Hassan遞給我一把薄荷葉說「拿著這個貼近鼻子,會好很多」。他解釋這裡的皮革至今仍用鴿糞軟化、天然植物染料上色,染缸按顏色分區排列,像一塊塊調色盤攤在陽光下,工人赤腳踩在染缸邊緣的畫面比任何照片都震撼。離開染坊,我在附近一間小地毯店被老闆Youssef邀去喝薄荷茶,他一邊倒茶一邊解釋,在摩洛哥拒絕別人遞來的茶是很失禮的事,哪怕只是象徵性喝一口。他也提到每逢星期五中午,店家會提早打烊,全家人聚在一起吃庫斯庫斯,這是延續好幾代的家庭儀式,不是觀光噱頭,而是他們一週裡最重要的一頓飯。那天剛好是星期五,街上果然有不少店提早拉下鐵門,喚拜聲從附近清真寺傳來,整條巷子突然安靜下來,像被按了暫停鍵。

他們不是為了觀光客表演生活,那杯薄荷茶、那頓星期五的庫斯庫斯,是這座城市呼吸的節奏。

夜色中的說書人

晚上九點,我最後一次回到Jemaa el-Fnaa,白天賣果汁的推車已經換成一圈圈圍坐的人群,中間是說書人(halqa)用抑揚頓挫的阿拉伯語講著我聽不懂卻能感受到起伏的故事,旁邊還有格納瓦(Gnawa)樂手敲著三弦琴與鐵片鈴鐺,節奏低沉像心跳。畫henna的婦女蹲坐在攤位旁,手法俐落地在遊客手背上畫出藤蔓花紋,煤氣燈的暖黃光線把整個廣場染成一種舞台般的氛圍。我坐在Café de France的二樓露台,點了一杯薄荷茶,俯瞰整片喧鬧,忽然明白為什麼馬拉喀什被稱作一座沒有牆的美術館——它的藝術不在玻璃展櫃裡,而在染坊工人的手上、廣場說書人的聲音裡、還有那一片怎麼拍都拍不膩的鈷藍色花園牆。離開的前一晚,我又走回巷弄深處,摸著那些冰涼粗糙的夯土牆,忽然捨不得走了。

ENGLISH

It was six in the morning, the sky still a pale grey, when I stepped out of Dar Zaman, the old riad tucked deep in the medina where I'd been staying. The alley was empty except for a cleaner hosing down the cobblestones, and the damp air mixed with last night's leftover charcoal smoke and cumin into something I still can't quite name, but which I now recognize instantly as the smell of Morocco. Before this trip I'd read all the usual descriptions — city of spices, gateway to the Sahara — but walking these lanes for real, I realized Marrakech is, above all, a living museum. Hand-chiseled stucco, geometric tilework, ochre-red rammed earth walls that shift colour with the light. Every surface here seems to be speaking. Over the next six days I skipped the museum checklists and simply let architecture, colour, and craftsmanship pull me along instead, and somehow ended up with a fuller itinerary than any guidebook could have planned.

Dawn in the Medina: A Lesson in Geometry

At seven-thirty the metal shutters of Souk Semmarine were still rattling open one by one, the daytime roar not yet arrived, so I ducked into the Ben Youssef Madrasa nearby while it was still quiet. At the centre of the courtyard sits a shallow reflecting pool, and the early light hit the walls at an angle that let me finally see, up close, what zellige actually is — hand-cut glazed tile fragments assembled into eight-pointed star patterns called khatim sulayman. A craftsman lingering by the entrance told me the star represents infinity, its points extending outward without end, a symbol of the boundlessness of God. The lower walls are tile, the middle band carved stucco, the upper register cedar wood inscribed with Quranic script — three materials, three separate trades, each chiseled by hand centuries ago by craftsmen who likely couldn't read the very words they were carving. Entry cost fifty dirhams, and I stayed nearly ninety minutes, reluctant to leave even as a tour group's chatter finally pushed me toward the door.

This city doesn't hang its art behind glass. It builds it into the walls, embeds it underfoot, carves it into the shifting shadows of the ceiling.

Jardin Majorelle and Saint Laurent's Blue Refuge

Leaving the old town, I want to record the actual logistics here, because the conflicting advice I'd found online before the trip had left me mildly anxious. I walked from my riad to Jardin Majorelle in the newer Gueliz district, about twenty-five minutes on foot along the busy Avenue Mohammed V, passing several French-style patisseries along the way — easier than I'd expected. I'd booked a combined ticket online three days ahead, one hundred fifty dirhams for the garden plus one hundred for the Yves Saint Laurent Museum next door. I arrived at ten to nine, doors opening at nine, and already found a line of some forty people; my pre-booked QR code let me skip straight through a separate lane, saving at least half an hour. Stepping inside, the cobalt blue hit me immediately — a shade the painter Jacques Majorelle mixed himself in the 1930s, later officially named Majorelle Blue, coating every structure and ceramic pot in the garden. Bamboo groves, cactus gardens, a koi pond, birdsong overlapping from every direction — I spent almost an hour wandering before reaching the museum, designed by the French firm Studio KO, its facade a lattice of terracotta brick woven like textile warp and weft, morning light falling through it in fractured patterns across the courtyard. Photography is forbidden inside; the galleries display Saint Laurent's hand-drawn couture sketches from 1962 to 2002, inspired by Moroccan colour, all set to the low hum of traditional Berber music, the whole space quiet as a chapel. Including the queue, garden, and museum, the full visit took two and a half hours. My one piece of practical advice: avoid the window between noon and two, when both the crowds and the heat peak at once.

Bahia Palace: Islamic Geometry Carved in Cedar

By three that afternoon I was back in the medina, at the Bahia Palace, built in 1866 for the grand vizier Si Moussa. The building has no grand facade to speak of — its drama comes instead from a sequence of courtyards that stack on top of each other, so you have no idea how much space is hidden inside until you're already deep within it. The four wives' quarters ring a central courtyard planted with orange trees, the air carrying a faint blossom scent mixed with the flutter of pigeons overhead. What stopped me in my tracks was the ceiling: cedar wood carved into honeycomb-like muqarnas domes, painted in saffron yellow and mint green, with afternoon light filtering through the pierced wooden mashrabiya screens onto the zellige floor below, the shadow patterns shifting constantly as clouds passed overhead. I stood there for a good ten minutes before a nudge from the crowd behind moved me along. Entry was seventy dirhams; I'd arrived right at nine-thirty when it opened, far quieter than the tour-bus crush that fills the courtyards by afternoon. If you can only pick one time slot, make it early morning.

Flavours of the Square, Fridays in the Tanners' Quarter

At six-thirty that evening I was back at Jemaa el-Fnaa, right as the food vendors wheeled their wooden carts into position, charcoal smoke mixing with the scent of cumin and grilling fat across the whole square. I settled at a stall run by a man who introduced himself as Said, starting with a bowl of harira — the lentil and tomato soup, ten dirhams, thick and lifted by a squeeze of lemon, alongside a freshly cooked msemen flatbread dipped in honey, crisp outside, soft within. Next came a plate of grilled kefta skewers for thirty-five dirhams, juicy and heavy with cumin and paprika, served with a small dish of pickled olives and harissa. Further along, an older man tended a steaming pot of babbouche, the snail soup simmered with fennel, cinnamon, and a dozen other spices — I worked up the nerve to try a small bowl for five dirhams; it tasted almost medicinal, the snail meat springy, all of it set to the rhythm of a drummer performing nearby. The whole meal, plus a five-dirham glass of fresh orange juice, came to under eighty dirhams total, and it remains the most theatrical dinner of the entire trip.

The next morning I headed to the Tanneries quarter on the edge of the city. I smelled it before I saw it, and a leather workshop owner named Hassan handed me a sprig of mint, saying, hold this close to your nose, it helps. He explained that hides here are still softened using pigeon droppings and coloured with natural plant dyes, the dye pits arranged by colour like a giant palette laid out under the open sky, workers standing barefoot at the rim of each vat — a sight far more striking in person than in any photograph. Afterward, in a small carpet shop nearby, the owner Youssef invited me in for mint tea, explaining as he poured that refusing an offered cup of tea in Morocco is considered genuinely impolite, even a symbolic sip matters. He mentioned that every Friday around noon, shops close early so families can gather for couscous, a ritual passed down across generations, not staged for tourists but simply the most important meal of their week. It happened to be a Friday; shutters were already coming down along the street, the call to prayer drifting from a nearby mosque, and the whole lane fell suddenly quiet, as if someone had pressed pause.

They aren't performing their lives for visitors. That glass of mint tea, that Friday couscous, is simply the rhythm this city breathes by.

Storytellers in the Dark

At nine that night I returned to Jemaa el-Fnaa one last time. The juice carts of daytime had given way to circles of seated listeners, at the centre of each a halqa storyteller weaving Arabic I couldn't understand but could still feel rise and fall, while nearby Gnawa musicians struck their sintir lutes and iron castanets in a low, heartbeat rhythm. Women squatted beside henna stalls, tracing vine patterns onto tourists' hands with quick, practiced strokes, gas lamps casting the whole square in a warm amber glow like a stage set. I sat on the upper terrace of Café de France with a glass of mint tea, looking down over the chaos, and understood at last why people call Marrakech a museum without walls — its art isn't kept behind glass but lives in the hands of the tannery workers, the voice of the square's storytellers, and that impossibly photogenic cobalt-blue garden wall. On my last night, I walked once more into the depths of the alleyways and pressed my palm against the cool, rough rammed-earth walls, suddenly reluctant to leave at all.
