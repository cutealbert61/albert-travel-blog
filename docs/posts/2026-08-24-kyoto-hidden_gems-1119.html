中文

清晨五點半的京都車站還籠罩在灰藍色的霧氣裡,我背著相機包走出旅館,想在這座城市被觀光巴士喚醒之前,找到只有京都人自己才知道的角落。這不是我第一次來京都,但這次我把清水寺、伏見稻荷、嵐山的行程表全部撕掉,改跟著在地朋友久美子留給我的一張手繪地圖走——上面標注的地方,連Google地圖都給不出正確的中文譯名。

黎明前的哲學之道:法然院與大豊神社的寂靜

哲學之道這個時間點是空的,連常見的貓都還沒出來巡邏。我沿著疏水道往南走,水聲比腳步聲還大,轉進一條幾乎沒有招牌的小徑,盡頭是大豊神社。這座神社小得不起眼,鳥居斑駁,但殿前蹲坐著石雕的狐狸、猴子與老鼠,是京都少數同時供奉三種動物使者的神社,平常連在地人都要特意繞路才會經過。我站在空無一人的境內,聞到潮濕青苔與線香混合的氣味,只有風吹過杉樹葉的沙沙聲。

再往前十分鐘腳程,是法然院。清晨六點半,山門還沒正式開放參觀,但通往本堂的白砂壇——那兩座隨季節變換圖案的白砂堆——已經被一位穿著藏青色作務衣的老先生仔細耙過,線條乾淨得像剛畫上去的水墨。我小聲問他能不能拍照,他點點頭,只說了一句「静かにね」(安靜地拍喔)。那一刻我突然明白,京都真正的美,往往不在景點本身,而在這種被日常維護出來的秩序感裡。

京都的美,常常藏在觀光地圖折角處那些沒有名字的巷弄與清晨裡。

鞍馬貴船秘境古道健行全記錄

吃過神馬堂的烤麻糬當早餐後,我從出町柳駅搭上叡山電鐵前往鞍馬,單程車資470日圓,車程約35分鐘。這是一節單節的木造小電車,秋天時窗外會鑽進一段紅葉隧道,連車廂燈都會特意調暗讓乘客欣賞,但我去的是初夏,窗外是層層疊疊的新綠,濕潤的山氣直接從半開的車窗灌進來。

抵達鞍馬駅是早上八點十分,月台上迎接我的是一座巨大的天狗面具雕像。往鞍馬寺方向走五分鐘,在仁王門買了愛山費500日圓,決定不搭纜車,直接徒步走山道上去——這是在地登山客才會選的路線,觀光客九成都搭纜車直達本殿。石階陡而濕滑,兩旁是幾百年的杉木林,樹幹粗得三人合抱不了,途中經過由岐神社,十月的「鞍馬の火祭」就是從這裡出發,是京都三大奇祭之一,場面盛大但外地遊客知道的不多。

走到本殿金堂大約花了40分鐘,喘得不輕,但視野豁然開朗,可以俯瞰整個鞍馬山谷。真正的秘境路段在後頭:從本殿後方繼續走「奥の院参道」,這段路幾乎沒有觀光客,只有兩位牽著柴犬的在地登山客跟我擦身而過。路上經過「背比べ石」與供奉巨杉的鞍馬寺奧之院魔王殿,林間安靜到能聽見自己的心跳與遠處溪水聲。整段山徑下坡到貴船側,總共走了將近一個半小時,鞋子和褲管都被泥土噴濺,建議務必穿防滑登山鞋,雨後路段會非常濕滑。

下山後就是貴船神社,朱紅色燈籠沿著石階兩側排開,我抽了一支水占みくじ——把籤紙浮在御手洗川的水面上,字跡才會顯現,價格200日圓。整趟行程含電車、入山費、抽籤,大約花費不到1500日圓,卻是我這趟京都行走得最扎實、也最安靜的一段路。

大原山間食堂的一碗味噌鍋物

隔天下著細雨,我搭巴士到大原,目的地不是遊客都會去的三千院正門,而是往三千院方向的小路上一間叫「芹生」的山菜料理店,躲在楓樹後面幾乎沒有招牌。推開木拉門,裡頭是老式圍爐裏,炭火燒得正旺,店裡只有一位在地阿嬤和她的女兒忙進忙出。我點了招牌的山菜味噌鍋定食,價格2200日圓。

鍋物端上桌時還在咕嘟咕嘟地滾,湯頭是白味噌熬煮的,帶著微微的甜,裡頭有大原自家栽種的水菜、蕨菜與生麩,豆腐是現點現切的絹豆腐,入口即化。配菜的紫蘇漬しば漬け酸得剛好,解了味噌的濃膩。窗外雨聲淅瀝,爐火劈啪作響,阿嬤用京都腔跟我閒聊,說這道鍋物是她婆婆傳下來的做法,冬天用來招待上山參拜三千院的信眾,「観光客はあんまり来ないけどね」(觀光客不太會來這裡呢),她笑著說。那頓飯我吃了將近一個小時,是這趟旅程裡吃得最慢、也最暖的一餐。

這座城市教會我,真正的寂靜不是沒有聲音,而是每一種聲音都被好好地聽見。

北野天滿宮天神市與賣漬物阿嬤的對話

京都人有句話說「今日は天神さんやし」,意思是每個月25號北野天滿宮的天神市集開市,是京都人生活裡的固定行程,不是為了觀光,而是為了買菜、買骨董、買舊和服。我特地選在25號早上八點抵達,參道兩側已經擺滿上千個攤位,烤栗子的香氣混著線香味飄在空氣裡,老太太們一個個蹲在攤前挑揀舊布料,動作熟練得像每個月都來報到——事實上她們確實每個月都來。

我在一個賣漬物的小攤前停下,攤主是位滿頭白髮的阿嬤,面前擺著京都名產すぐき(酸莖漬)。她遞給我一小片試吃,酸香帶著發酵的鮮味,跟便利商店賣的完全是兩種東西。她告訴我,すぐき是冬天才醃的漬物,要用石頭壓上好幾天,現在賣的是去年冬天留下的存貨。她又說,每年一月25號的「初天神」是一年裡最熱鬧的一場市集,家家戶戶會來買開運小物,「錦市場是給觀光客看的,天神さん才是我們京都人的市場」,她說這句話時語氣裡帶著一點驕傲。那一刻我才明白,同樣是市集,觀光客與在地人走的其實是完全不同的節奏。

上七軒巷弄盡頭的最後一杯

旅程最後一晚,我走進上七軒——京都現存最古老的花街,比祇園還低調,遊客也少了大半。傍晚六點多,紅燈籠一盞盞亮起,我遠遠瞥見一位舞妓踩著細碎的步伐匆匆趕場,木屐敲在石板路上發出清脆的聲響,轉眼就消失在某扇格子門後。

巷弄深處有一間叫「さらさ西陣」的咖啡館,是由一間昭和時代的老錢湯(公共澡堂)改建而成,牆上還保留著當年的彩色磁磚壁畫,拱形天花板挑高,燈光昏黃。我點了一杯手沖咖啡跟一份起司蛋糕,約1000日圓,坐在原本應該是浴池的位置上,聽著隔壁桌一對京都老夫婦用京都腔慢慢聊天。老闆告訴我,這一帶晚上常常能聽見西陣織的織布機聲,是這一區從室町時代延續至今的紡織業傳統,「白天是織物の音、晚上是三味線の音」,他這樣形容上七軒的一天。

走出咖啡館時已近九點,巷子安靜得只剩下我自己的腳步聲。回頭望向那一整排低矮的町家與微微透光的紙門,我忽然覺得,這趟旅程真正帶走的不是任何一張照片,而是那種被允許慢下來、跟著這座城市呼吸節奏走的感覺。京都從來不缺人潮,缺的只是願意在清晨五點半就出門的人。

ENGLISH

At five-thirty in the morning, Kyoto Station was still wrapped in a bluish-grey haze. I slung my camera bag over my shoulder and stepped out of the hotel, determined to find the corners of this city that only its own residents know, before the tour buses woke it up. This wasn't my first trip to Kyoto, but this time I tore up my itinerary of Kiyomizu-dera, Fushimi Inari, and Arashiyama, and instead followed a hand-drawn map left to me by my local friend Kumiko — a map with place names that Google Maps couldn't even translate correctly.

Before Dawn on the Philosopher's Path: The Silence of Honen-in and Otoyo Shrine

The Philosopher's Path was completely empty at that hour — even the neighborhood cats hadn't started their rounds yet. I walked south along the canal, the sound of water louder than my own footsteps, then turned off onto a narrow lane with almost no signage. At the end of it sat Otoyo Shrine, a modest, weathered structure easy to miss. What makes it remarkable is what crouches before its main hall: stone-carved statues of a fox, a monkey, and a mouse — one of the very few shrines in Kyoto guarding three different animal messengers at once, so obscure that even locals sometimes take a detour just to visit it. Standing alone in the empty courtyard, I breathed in the mingled smell of damp moss and incense, with nothing but wind rustling through the cedar leaves.

Ten minutes further on foot brought me to Honen-in. At six-thirty, the temple gate wasn't officially open for visitors, but the two white sand mounds leading up to the main hall — patterns that change with the seasons — had already been meticulously raked by an elderly man in a dark indigo work coat. The lines were so crisp they looked freshly painted in ink. I asked quietly if I could take a photo; he simply nodded and said, 'Shizuka ni ne' — quietly, please. In that moment I understood that Kyoto's real beauty rarely lives in the monuments themselves, but in this quiet, daily-maintained sense of order.

Kyoto's beauty often hides in the nameless alleys and early mornings folded into the corners of the tourist map.

The Full Story of Hiking the Hidden Kurama–Kifune Trail

After a breakfast of grilled mochi from Kamiuma-dou, I took the Eizan Electric Railway from Demachiyanagi Station toward Kurama — a one-way fare of 470 yen and about a 35-minute ride. It's a single-car wooden train; in autumn the windows pass through a tunnel of maple leaves so vivid that the conductor dims the interior lights for passengers, but I rode through in early summer, when layers of fresh green pressed in through the half-open windows along with the damp mountain air.

I arrived at Kurama Station at ten past eight, greeted on the platform by a massive tengu mask statue. A five-minute walk brought me to the Niō-mon gate of Kurama-dera, where I paid the 500-yen mountain entry fee and decided to skip the cable car — the choice most tour groups make — and hike the trail on foot instead, the route locals actually prefer. The stone steps were steep and slick, flanked by centuries-old cedars too wide for three people to encircle with their arms. Along the way I passed Yuki Shrine, the starting point of the Kurama Fire Festival each October — one of Kyoto's three great 'strange festivals,' spectacular in scale yet barely known outside the region.

The climb to the main hall, Honden Kongo, took about 40 minutes and left me winded, but the view opened up over the entire Kurama valley. The real hidden stretch came after: continuing past the main hall onto the Okunoin approach, a path virtually free of tourists — I passed only two local hikers with a shiba inu the entire way. I walked past the 'height-comparison stone' and the Maō-den hall enshrining an ancient cedar deity, the forest so silent I could hear my own heartbeat and a distant stream. The descent toward Kifune took nearly an hour and a half in total, my shoes and trouser cuffs splattered with mud — sturdy, non-slip hiking shoes are essential, especially after rain.

At the bottom waited Kifune Shrine, its vermillion lanterns lined up along the stone steps. I tried a mizuura mikuji, a water fortune slip that only reveals its text when floated on the surface of the sacred Mitarashi stream — 200 yen. The whole excursion, including train fare, mountain fee, and the fortune slip, cost less than 1,500 yen, yet it was the most grounding and quiet stretch of walking I did the entire trip.

A Bowl of Miso Hot Pot in a Mountain Restaurant in Ohara

The next day brought light rain, and I took a bus to Ohara — not to the main entrance of Sanzen-in that every guidebook lists, but to a small mountain-vegetable restaurant called Seryo, tucked behind maple trees along the approach road, its signage almost nonexistent. Sliding open the wooden door, I found an old-fashioned sunken hearth with charcoal glowing steadily; only an elderly local woman and her daughter were working the small kitchen. I ordered the house specialty, a mountain-vegetable miso hot pot set for 2,200 yen.

The pot arrived still bubbling gently, its broth made from white miso, faintly sweet, filled with local water spinach, bracken fern, and namafu wheat gluten grown right there in Ohara. The tofu, cut fresh to order, dissolved on the tongue. A side of shiba-zuke pickled eggplant, sharp and perfectly sour, cut through the richness of the miso. Rain pattered outside the window, the charcoal crackled, and the elderly owner chatted with me in a soft Kyoto dialect, telling me the recipe had been passed down from her mother-in-law, originally made to welcome winter pilgrims visiting Sanzen-in. 'Tourists don't really come here,' she said with a small laugh. I lingered over that meal for nearly an hour — the slowest, warmest meal of the entire trip.

This city taught me that real silence isn't the absence of sound — it's when every sound finally gets heard.

The Kitano Tenmangu Flea Market and a Conversation with a Pickle Vendor

There's a saying in Kyoto: 'Today's Tenjin-san day' — referring to the monthly flea market held on the 25th at Kitano Tenmangu, a fixture of local life rather than a tourist event, where people come to shop for vegetables, antiques, and secondhand kimono. I arrived deliberately at eight in the morning on the 25th, when the approach was already lined with over a thousand stalls. The smell of roasted chestnuts mixed with incense hung in the air, and elderly women crouched in front of fabric stalls with practiced, unhurried movements — the kind that comes from doing this every single month for decades.

I stopped at a stall selling pickles, run by a white-haired woman selling suguki, Kyoto's famous fermented turnip pickle. She handed me a small taste — tangy, deeply fermented, worlds apart from anything sold in a convenience store. She explained that suguki is pickled only in winter, weighted down with heavy stones for days, and what she was selling now was last winter's stock. She told me the market held every January 25th, called Hatsu-Tenjin, is the busiest of the year, when families come to buy lucky charms for the new year. 'Nishiki Market is for tourists,' she said, with a hint of pride in her voice. 'Tenjin-san is the real market for us Kyoto people.' In that moment I understood that even a marketplace can move to two entirely different rhythms depending on who's walking through it.

The Last Drink at the End of a Kamishichiken Alley

On my final evening, I wandered into Kamishichiken — Kyoto's oldest geisha district, quieter and far less visited than Gion. Around six, red lanterns flickered on one by one, and I caught a glimpse of a maiko hurrying to an engagement, her wooden geta clicking crisply against the stone pavement before she vanished behind a latticed door.

Deep in the alley stood a café called Sarasa Nishijin, converted from a Showa-era public bathhouse, its walls still lined with the original colorful tile murals and the ceiling arching high overhead in dim amber light. I ordered a pour-over coffee and a slice of cheesecake for about 1,000 yen, sitting in what would once have been the bathing pool, listening to an elderly Kyoto couple at the next table chatting slowly in their local dialect. The owner told me that at night this neighborhood still hums with the sound of Nishijin-ori looms, a textile tradition dating back to the Muromachi period. 'By day it's the sound of weaving,' he said, 'by night it's the sound of the shamisen.'

By the time I stepped back outside it was nearly nine, and the alley was silent except for my own footsteps. Looking back at the row of low machiya houses, their paper doors glowing faintly from within, I realized what I was really taking home wasn't a photograph, but the feeling of being allowed to slow down and breathe at the city's own pace. Kyoto has never lacked for crowds — what it lacks are people willing to walk out the door at five-thirty in the morning.
