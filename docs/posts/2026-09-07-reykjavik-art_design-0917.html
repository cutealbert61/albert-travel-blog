中文

飛機降落在凱夫拉維克(Keflavík)機場的時候,是清晨六點十分,機艙窗外的天色還卡在深藍與鐵灰之間,像一塊還沒退火的金屬。我拖著行李走出入境大廳,冷風夾雜著海藻與微微硫磺的氣味撲上臉頰——那是我對冰島的第一個感官記憶,比任何一張明信片都更誠實。這趟旅程,我沒打算追著金圈景點跑,我想看的是雷克雅維克這座只有十二萬人口的首都,怎麼「設計」自己:它的教堂、它的音樂廳、它牆上的塗鴉,甚至它端上桌的一份熱狗,都藏著北歐人對線條、光線與材質近乎偏執的講究。接下來的四十八小時,我幾乎全靠走的,把這座城市一寸一寸讀了一遍,連鞋底磨薄的痠痛,現在回想起來都覺得值得。

破曉裡的玄武岩塔——Hallgrímskirkja與晨光中的城市輪廓

清晨七點半,我從市中心的公寓式旅館出發,沿著Skólavörðustígur這條微微上坡的彩色街道往上走。這條路本身已經是一件設計作品:兩側房子的鐵皮牆面漆成粉紫、鵝黃、灰藍,路面漆上彩虹色條紋,是紀念冰島同志遊行而留下的永久塗裝,清晨的行人不多,只有送報紙的機車呼嘯而過,輪胎壓過濕潤柏油的聲音特別清脆。走到坡頂,Hallgrímskirkja教堂的灰白尖塔毫無預警地立在眼前,74公尺高,像一整排倒插的玄武岩柱從地底被拔起,直直刺向天空。建築師Guðjón Samúelsson在1937年設計這座教堂時,靈感來自冰島南部Svartifoss瀑布旁那片天然形成的六角柱狀玄武岩——我後來真的去了那座瀑布,站在黑色岩柱前才理解,這座教堂根本不是「像」玄武岩,它就是把地質景觀直接翻譯成建築語言,連同市中心的國家劇院、大學主樓,都用同一套語彙寫成,是一整個世代冰島建築師想從丹麥殖民陰影裡,找出屬於自己土地的表情。

教堂內部意外地安靜、樸素,沒有一般歐洲教堂的金碧輝煌,牆面是裸露的灰色混凝土,唯一的裝飾是管風琴般垂直排列的巨大風管,5275支風管在晨間彩排時發出的低頻震動,我站在第三排長椅上都能感覺到胸腔在共振,那種震動比任何管風琴的音色本身還讓我起雞皮疙瘩。我花了900冰島克朗(約台幣200元)搭電梯上塔頂觀景台,八點半的雷克雅維克從高處看下去,是一片由紅、藍、綠彩色鐵皮屋頂拼成的積木城市,海灣的水面像被熨斗燙平的錫箔紙,遠方的Esja山還披著一層薄雲,風大到我必須用雙手扶住觀景台的鐵網才敢拿起手機拍照。

雷克雅維克的美,不是雕出來的,是被冰與火逼出來的一種誠實。

海灣上的冰河詩篇——Harpa音樂廳與光影美學

上午十一點,我沿著海岸線散步到Harpa音樂廳,腳下的木棧道還帶著昨夜的濕氣,海鳥在頭頂盤旋,叫聲被海風扯得又長又細。Harpa是我在冰島看過最讓人挪不開視線的建築:由丹麥建築師Henning Larsen事務所與冰島藝術家Ólafur Elíasson合作,外牆是超過一萬片六角形與五角形玄武岩晶體造型的玻璃模組,每一片角度都不同,像是把整片Svartifoss的岩柱結晶敲碎、融化,重新吹製成玻璃鱗片包覆整棟建築。中午的陽光斜斜打進來,大廳地面被切割成一塊塊菱形光斑,隨著我走動的角度,牆面顏色從琥珀色滑到鐵灰藍,像站在一個會呼吸的萬花筒裡,連地板反光都在跟著移動。

我買了一杯450克朗的黑咖啡,坐在二樓的長椅上看了快四十分鐘,看陽光怎麼隨著雲層厚薄變化,整棟建築的顏色也跟著明暗起伏——這棟樓沒有一刻是「一樣」的。館內工作人員跟我聊起,2008年金融風暴時這棟樓一度停工,鋼骨結構就這樣裸露在海邊淋了快兩年的雨,是靠著文化界人士連署陳情才續建完成。對雷克雅維克人來說,Harpa不只是音樂廳,更像是這座城市熬過經濟崩潰之後,重新相信「美」這件事值得被堅持下去的證明,他說這句話的時候語氣很輕,但我聽得出那份認真。

巷弄裡的城市肌膚——徒步走一趟街頭藝術路線

下午一點半,我從Harpa往回走進市中心,決定用自己的腳走一趟街頭藝術路線,而不是報名任何付費導覽團——其實雷克雅維克的街頭藝術地圖可以在旅客中心免費索取,或直接搜尋「Reykjavik Street Art」的線上地圖,完全免費。我的路線是這樣走的:從主要商店街Laugavegur入口開始,先往東走到Vitastígur巷口,這裡有一整棟山形屋牆面畫著北歐神話裡的巨人與海怪,顏色濃烈得像是把北極光直接潑上牆;接著沿Laugavegur走到與Frakkastígur的交叉口,轉進小巷,會看到冰島藝術家Sara Riel畫的一整棟樓高的女性肖像壁畫,眼神低垂,頭髮化成翻湧的海浪,是我這趟旅程裡最喜歡的一幅作品,站在巷口抬頭看,脖子痠了都捨不得移開視線。

再往北切進Hverfisgata這條被雷克雅維克人暱稱為「壁畫街」的道路,短短八百公尺內我數了超過十五幅大型壁畫,有澳洲藝術家Guido van Helten畫的黑白老漁夫肖像,眼角的皺紋畫得比照片還立體,也有本地塗鴉團體畫的抽象幾何海浪。整段路我走走停停,拍照、看說明牌,中途因為突然下起午後陣雨,鑽進路邊一家叫Kaffi Vínyl的黑膠咖啡館躲雨,喝了一杯650克朗的燕麥奶拿鐵,雨聲打在鐵皮屋頂上劈啪作響,跟隔壁桌黑膠唱片機放出的爵士樂混在一起,老闆一邊翻著唱片一邊跟我用破英文聊他最喜歡的塗鴉在哪一條巷子。整條路線走完大約花了兩個半小時,中間因為地圖座標偏差走錯過一次巷子,多繞了十分鐘,卻也因此意外撞見一幅沒被收錄進地圖、畫在垃圾集中處鐵門上的無名塗鴉,反而成了整趟散步裡最驚喜的畫面。

這座城市把畫廊搬到了戶外,牆壁比很多美術館還誠實。

深夜熱狗攤與海港餐桌——用味覺讀懂這座城市

晚上十點半,天色還帶著一點微光——七月的雷克雅維克幾乎不會真正天黑——我走到老港口旁那個永遠排著隊的小攤:Bæjarins Beztu Pylsur,冰島人心中「全國最好吃的熱狗」。這個木頭小亭子從1937年就開始營業,是雷克雅維克人的深夜食堂,計程車司機、下班的酒保、剛看完演出的觀眾都會擠在這裡排隊。我點了一份「all in」,價格490克朗(約台幣110元),熱狗混了羊肉,油脂比例讓肉質帶點微微的野味甜香,上面依序疊了脆炸洋蔥丁、生洋蔥碎、甜芥末醬、番茄醬,還有一種叫remoulade的酸黃瓜美乃滋醬——第一口咬下去,脆的、軟的、酸的、甜的層次幾乎同時炸開,難怪柯林頓總統當年來訪也在這裡點過一份,牆上還貼著他當年的照片,紙都泛黃了。

吃完熱狗,我又走進老港口邊的Kopar餐廳吃了一頓正式晚餐,坐在能看到漁船進港的窗邊位置,點了一份冰島螯蝦(langoustine)奶油蒜香義大利麵,4200克朗(約台幣950元),蝦肉彈牙帶著海水的甜味,盤子邊緣灑了一點乾燥海藻粉,服務生說這是向漁業致敬的擺盤設計。窗外的漁船燈火倒映在海面上,跟屋內極簡北歐風的木質桌椅、手工陶盤相互呼應——連食物的擺盤,在這裡都被當成一種設計語言在對待,連醬汁的走位都像是被丈量過。

舊魚廠裡的當代藝術,一場關於節慶的對話,與離開前的夕陽

第二天上午,我搭公車前往Grandi這個曾是漁業加工廠聚集的港區,現在改建成藝術特區。Marshall House這棟老魚罐頭工廠裡,一樓是藝術家自營的Kling & Bang當代藝術空間,免費入場,展出的是一系列用回收漁網編織成的裝置藝術,懸掛在天花板上,隨著空調氣流輕輕晃動,發出細微的塑膠摩擦聲。二樓則是Ólafur Elíasson在冰島設立的工作室分支展示間,牆上投影著北極光的光譜實驗紀錄,昏暗展間裡只有我跟另一位背包客,兩人安靜地站著,誰也沒開口說話。

在Grandi的美食市集Grandi Mathöll裡,我跟一位在市集裡賣手工陶器的年輕老闆Kristín聊了起來。她告訴我,如果我是一月底來,一定要嚐嚐Þorrablót——這是冰島人紀念祖先在嚴冬裡靠醃漬與發酵食物度過難關的節慶,餐桌上會出現發酵鯊魚肉hákarl、羊頭肉、黑麥麵包裹著的血腸,配著冰島烈酒Brennivín一口吞下,「觀光客常常皺著眉頭吞不下那口鯊魚肉,但對我們來說那個味道就是家的味道,」她笑著說,還順手拿了一小杯試喝的Brennivín請我,嗆得我眼淚都冒出來。她也提到八月的Menningarnótt文化之夜,那一晚全城博物館、藝廊、私人畫室都免費開放到深夜,街上會放煙火,「那才是雷克雅維克人真正把藝術當成生活的一天,不是給觀光客看的。」

離開前,我在傍晚九點走到海邊看Sólfar(太陽航海者)雕塑,這艘由鋼鐵打造、線條極簡近乎骨架的維京船造型雕塑,在夕陽把海面染成橘紅色時,剪影像一隻正要振翅的鳥。藝術家Jón Gunnar Árnason完成這件作品後不久便去世,雕塑本意是獻給太陽與希望——站在那裡,海風吹得我眼睛發酸,我忽然覺得,這座城市所有的設計,不管是教堂、音樂廳、塗鴉還是一份熱狗,說的其實是同一件事:在極端的自然環境裡,人還是可以,也應該,把日子過得美。

在永晝與永夜之間,雷克雅維克人選擇把美感,活成一種日常的抵抗。

ENGLISH

The plane touched down at Keflavík at ten past six in the morning, and outside the window the sky was still caught between deep blue and iron grey, like a piece of metal that hadn't cooled yet. I dragged my suitcase through arrivals and the cold air hit my face carrying a smell of seaweed and faint sulfur — that was my first sensory memory of Iceland, more honest than any postcard could be. I hadn't come to chase the Golden Circle. I wanted to understand how Reykjavik, a capital of barely a hundred and twenty thousand people, designs itself: its church, its concert hall, the murals on its walls, even the hot dog handed to you at midnight all carry that Nordic near-obsession with line, light, and material. Over the next forty-eight hours I walked almost everywhere, reading this city inch by inch, and looking back now, even the ache in my soles felt worth it.

A Basalt Spire at Dawn: Hallgrímskirkja and the City Skyline in Morning Light

At half past seven I set out from my apartment-hotel in the city centre, walking up the gently sloping, colour-painted Skólavörðustígur. The street itself is already a design object: corrugated metal facades painted in lilac, mustard, and slate blue, the pavement striped in rainbow colours as a permanent tribute to Iceland's pride parades. Few people were out yet, only a newspaper delivery scooter roaring past, its tires slapping crisply against the wet asphalt. At the top of the slope, the pale grey spire of Hallgrímskirkja appeared without warning, seventy-four metres tall, like a row of basalt columns pulled straight out of the earth and pointed at the sky. When architect Guðjón Samúelsson designed the church in 1937, he drew inspiration from the naturally formed hexagonal basalt columns beside Svartifoss waterfall in south Iceland. I later stood in front of those actual columns and finally understood: this building doesn't merely resemble basalt — it is geology translated directly into architecture, the same vocabulary used across the National Theatre and the University of Iceland's main building, a whole generation of architects searching for a native expression out of the shadow of Danish colonial rule.

The interior is surprisingly quiet and plain, none of the gilded excess of a typical European cathedral — bare grey concrete walls, the only decoration being the enormous pipe organ, its 5,275 pipes standing like a vertical forest. During the morning rehearsal, the low-frequency vibration was strong enough that I felt my ribcage resonate sitting in the third pew, a sensation more chilling than the actual tone of the organ. I paid 900 ISK (about USD 6.50) to take the lift up to the observation deck, and at half past eight, Reykjavik from above looked like a city built from blocks — red, blue, and green corrugated roofs scattered across the grid, the bay looking like tinfoil pressed flat by an iron, Mount Esja still wrapped in thin cloud in the distance. The wind was so strong up there I had to grip the wire mesh with both hands before daring to lift my phone for a photo.

Reykjavik's beauty isn't carved — it's an honesty forced out of ice and fire.

A Glacial Poem Over the Bay: Harpa Concert Hall and the Aesthetics of Light

By eleven that morning I had walked along the shoreline to Harpa Concert Hall, the boardwalk still damp from the night before, seabirds circling overhead, their cries stretched thin by the wind. Harpa is the single building in Iceland I found hardest to look away from — designed by the Danish firm Henning Larsen Architects in collaboration with Icelandic-Danish artist Ólafur Elíasson, its facade made of more than ten thousand hexagonal and pentagonal glass modules shaped like basalt crystals, each set at a slightly different angle, as if the rock columns of Svartifoss had been shattered, melted, and reblown into glass scales wrapping the entire structure. At midday the sun slanted through, cutting the lobby floor into diamonds of light, and as I moved, the wall colours slid from amber to steel blue, as though I were standing inside a kaleidoscope that breathed.

I bought a black coffee for 450 ISK and sat on a bench on the second floor for nearly forty minutes, watching how the light shifted with the thickness of the clouds outside, the whole building rising and dimming with it — never once the same colour twice. A staff member told me that during the 2008 financial collapse, construction on the building stopped entirely, its exposed steel frame left standing in the rain by the harbour for nearly two years, until a petition from the cultural community got it finished. For Reykjavikers, he said, Harpa isn't just a concert hall — it's proof that after the economy collapsed, the city chose to believe beauty was still worth insisting on. He said it quietly, but I could hear how much he meant it.

The Skin of a City in Its Alleys: Walking a Street Art Route on Foot

At half past one I walked back from Harpa into the city centre, deciding to trace a street art route on my own rather than book a paid tour — Reykjavik's street art map is free at the tourist information centre, or searchable online under 'Reykjavik Street Art,' no cost at all. My route went like this: starting from the top of the main shopping street Laugavegur, I first walked east to the corner of Vitastígur, where an entire gable wall is painted with giants and sea monsters from Norse mythology, colours so intense they look like the northern lights spilled straight onto concrete. From there I followed Laugavegur to its junction with Frakkastígur and turned down a side lane to find a building-height portrait by Icelandic artist Sara Riel — a woman's face looking down, her hair dissolving into churning waves — my favourite piece of the whole trip; I stood at the mouth of that alley with my neck craned upward far longer than I meant to.

Cutting north into Hverfisgata, the street locals nickname 'mural street,' I counted more than fifteen large-scale murals within roughly eight hundred metres: a black-and-white portrait of an old fisherman by Australian artist Guido van Helten, the creases around his eyes rendered more vividly than in any photograph, alongside abstract geometric waves painted by local graffiti crews. I stopped and started the whole way, reading the little plaques, and when a sudden afternoon shower hit, I ducked into a vinyl record café called Kaffi Vínyl, ordered an oat milk latte for 650 ISK, and listened to rain crackling against the tin roof mixing with jazz spinning from the turntable at the next table, the owner flipping through records while telling me, in broken English, which alley held his personal favourite piece. The whole route took about two and a half hours; a coordinate error on my map map sent me down the wrong lane once, costing ten extra minutes, but it led me to an uncredited, unmapped piece painted on a trash enclosure's metal shutter — accidentally the most delightful discovery of the entire walk.

This city moved its galleries outdoors, and the walls are more honest than most museums.

Midnight Hot Dogs and a Harbour Table: Tasting the City's Design Philosophy

At half past ten that night, the sky still held a faint glow — Reykjavik in July barely goes properly dark — and I walked to the perpetually queuing stall by the old harbour: Bæjarins Beztu Pylsur, the hot dog Icelanders themselves call the best in the country. This wooden kiosk has operated since 1937 and functions as Reykjavik's late-night canteen — taxi drivers, off-shift bartenders, and concertgoers all crowd in to queue. I ordered the 'all in,' priced at 490 ISK (roughly USD 3.50); the sausage is a lamb blend, its fat content giving the meat a faintly gamey sweetness, layered with crispy fried onion bits, raw diced onion, sweet mustard, ketchup, and a pickled remoulade sauce — the first bite hit crunchy, soft, sour, and sweet almost simultaneously. No wonder Bill Clinton reportedly ordered one here on his visit; a yellowing photograph of him still hangs on the wall.

After the hot dog I walked into Kopar, a proper restaurant by the old harbour, and took a window seat facing the fishing boats coming in. I ordered langoustine pasta in garlic cream sauce for 4,200 ISK (about USD 30); the shellfish was springy with a faint seawater sweetness, the plate's rim dusted with dried seaweed powder, which the server explained was a plating detail meant as a tribute to the fishing industry. Outside, the boats' lights reflected off the water, echoing the minimalist Nordic wooden furniture and handmade ceramic plates inside — even the food's plating here is treated as a design language, the sauce placed with what felt like measured precision.

Contemporary Art in an Old Fish Factory, a Conversation About Festivals, and a Sunset Before Leaving

The next morning I took a bus to Grandi, once a cluster of fish-processing plants and now reborn as an arts district. Inside Marshall House, a former fish-canning factory, the ground floor houses Kling & Bang, an artist-run contemporary space with free admission, currently showing an installation woven from recycled fishing nets suspended from the ceiling, swaying gently in the air-conditioned draft and producing a faint plastic rustle. Upstairs, a satellite exhibition space for Ólafur Elíasson's studio projected recordings of aurora light spectrum experiments onto the walls; in that dim room it was just me and one other backpacker, both of us standing in silence.

At Grandi Mathöll, the food hall in the same district, I struck up a conversation with Kristín, a young potter selling handmade ceramics at a stall there. She told me that if I ever came back in late January, I had to try Þorrablót — the midwinter festival commemorating how Icelanders' ancestors survived brutal winters on pickled and fermented food. The table brings out fermented shark meat, hákarl, sheep's head, and blood sausage wrapped in rye bread, all washed down with the local schnapps Brennivín. 'Tourists usually can't swallow the shark without wincing,' she laughed, handing me a small taster shot of Brennivín that made my eyes water instantly, 'but to us, that taste is home.' She also mentioned Menningarnótt, Culture Night in August, when every museum, gallery, and private studio in the city opens free until late, fireworks light up the streets, and, as she put it, 'that's the one day Reykjavikers actually live art as life, not perform it for tourists.'

Before leaving, I walked to the shore at nine in the evening to see Sólfar, the Sun Voyager sculpture — a stripped-down, almost skeletal steel Viking ship whose silhouette, against a sky the sunset had turned orange, looked like a bird about to take flight. Artist Jón Gunnar Árnason died shortly after finishing this piece, intended as a tribute to the sun and to hope. Standing there with the wind stinging my eyes, I suddenly understood that everything this city designs — its church, its concert hall, its murals, even a hot dog — is really saying the same thing: even in the harshest natural environment, people still can, and still should, make life beautiful.

Between endless daylight and endless dark, Reykjavikers turned beauty into a daily act of resistance.
