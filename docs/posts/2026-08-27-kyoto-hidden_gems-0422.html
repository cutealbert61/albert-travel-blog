中文

飛機降落關西機場那天,我心裡想的不是清水寺的木造舞台,也不是嵐山竹林裡摩肩擦踵的人潮——那些畫面我早已在無數次社群動態裡看到爛熟。這是我第五次踏上京都,卻是第一次決定把導覽書留在行李箱底層,只帶著一位在京都住了十二年的朋友寫給我的手繪地圖出門。她在地圖角落用鉛筆寫了一句話:「京都人的京都,從來不在觀光地圖上。」十天後我離開這座城市時,終於懂了這句話的重量。

清晨五點半,伏見稻荷的另一張臉

鬧鐘設在五點整,我幾乎是摸黑走出東福寺附近那間老式民宿的。京都的清晨很安靜,只有送報的摩托車引擎聲和遠處烏鴉的叫聲。我搭上JR奈良線的頭班車,五分鐘就到稻荷駅,月台上除了我,只有兩個像是要去上班的當地阿伯。走出車站,千本鳥居的入口在晨霧裡有種近乎陰森的美——沒有導遊的旗子,沒有排隊拍照的人龍,只有石階上薄薄一層露水,踩上去會發出細微的滑動聲。

我沿著朱紅色的鳥居隧道一路往上走,大約二十分鐘後抵達第一個岔路口「四ツ辻」,這裡視野忽然開闊,可以俯瞰整個京都盆地,晨光正從東山那頭慢慢染上屋瓦。大部分觀光客會在四ツ辻拍完照就折返,但我照著朋友的地圖繼續往上,走向較少人知道的「御劔社」與更深處的稻荷山山頂「一ノ峰」。這段路單程約再需要四十分鐘,坡度陡、階梯窄,穿著涼鞋的我後悔沒換登山鞋,但沿路狐狸石像在霧裡若隱若現,苔蘚長滿了古老的小祠堂,偶爾傳來竹葉摩擦的聲音,像是山神在低語。

在御劔社前,我遇到一位七十多歲的老先生,正對著祠堂低聲誦念,手裡拿著一串念珠。他看我駐足,主動用簡單的英文加日文跟我解釋,這裡供奉的是刀劍之神,附近居民每個月都會上山參拜一次,「觀光客只到四ツ辻,山頂才是真正的稻荷山」,他笑著說。整趟來回花了我將近兩個半小時,除了自動販賣機的一罐咖啡(約150日圓)和一炷香的隨喜功德,幾乎沒有花費。八點多下山時,山腳下的鳥居入口已經開始湧入一車車的遊覽團,那種對比讓我格外慶幸自己起了個大早。

當千本鳥居只剩下我與狐狸石像的呼吸聲,京都才終於卸下觀光地的妝容,露出屬於信仰的原色。

一乘寺,書店與寺院之間的巷弄

從伏見稻荷搭京阪電車轉叡山電鐵,大約四十分鐘後抵達一乘寺駅,這一區是京都大學生與文人聚集的老社區,巷弄裡藏著全日本文青最愛的獨立書店「恵文社一乗寺店」。木質地板踩起來會發出輕微的嘎吱聲,店員在角落安靜地包裝一本二手詩集,店裡飄著舊紙張混合線香的氣味。我在這裡待了將近一小時,翻著幾本從沒聽過的攝影集,最後買了一本京都在地攝影師拍的黑白寫真集,約2200日圓。

離開書店,沿著住宅區的窄巷步行約十分鐘,是幾乎沒有觀光客的「詩仙堂」,入場費500日圓,庭園裡的枯山水在正午陽光下泛著細白的光,竹製的「添水」每隔幾分鐘發出一聲清脆的「咚」,提醒人時間仍在流動。再往前走五分鐘是「金福寺」,寺內有一株俳句詩人與謝蕪村親手照料過的庭園,遊客稀少到我幾乎能聽見自己踩在榻榻米上的腳步聲。我在寺院旁一間只有五張椅子的喫茶店「六花」坐下,老闆娘用虹吸式咖啡壺為我手沖了一杯,咖啡香混著窗外飄進來的桂花味,她笑著說附近住了三十年,「觀光客都往嵐山跑,這條巷子安靜得像另一個京都」。

鴨川三角洲與豆餅的午後

中午時分,我搭電車回到出町柳,沿著河堤走到鴨川與高野川交會的「鴨川デルタ」。這裡有一整排烏龜與千鳥形狀的跳石,當地大學生坐在石頭上彈吉他、遛狗,幾個小孩踩水尖叫,對岸傳來練習太鼓的悶悶鼓聲。我脫了鞋,踩在被曬得溫熱的跳石上過河,河水冰涼清澈,能看見小魚游過腳邊,那一刻的悠閒感,比任何一張嵐山竹林的照片都更接近我心目中真正的京都。

過河後步行三分鐘,就是排隊人潮不算誇張、卻是京都人心中信仰般存在的「出町ふたば」。招牌商品是「名代豆餅」,一顆200日圓,外皮混著黑豆粒,鹹香的豆香先在舌尖炸開,接著才是紅豆餡細膩的甜,麻糬皮的黏韌恰到好處,不會黏牙,咬下去甚至能感覺到黑豆表皮微微的脆感。我一次買了三顆,店員用油紙包好遞給我時還提醒「今日中に召し上がってください」(請當日食用完畢),那種對新鮮度的堅持讓我瞬間理解為什麼京都人願意天天排隊。

下午我又轉搭電車回到一乘寺,鑽進拉麵街一間本地上班族才會排隊的「高安」,坐在僅有八個位子的吧台前,點了招牌豚骨醬油拉麵,約850日圓。湯頭濃稠得像化開的骨髓,表面浮著一層油光,叉燒厚實入口即化,配上偏硬的細麵條,吸溜聲此起彼落,老闆娘忙進忙出汗流浹背,還是笑著跟熟客寒暄。這一餐讓我理解,京都真正的美食現場,往往不在錦市場那些觀光化的攤位裡,而在這種只有本地人才耐得住排隊的巷弄小店。

大原,紫蘇田與寂光院的寧靜

傍晚前,我搭上從出町柳附近轉乘的京都巴士,搖搖晃晃將近一小時抵達大原。這裡海拔略高,空氣明顯轉涼,兩旁是成片的紫蘇田,風吹過時帶著一股獨特的辛香氣味。我先走進「寂光院」,寺內供奉的是建禮門院,寺院安靜得只剩下風鈴聲與蟬鳴交疊,幾乎沒有其他遊客。接著走十五分鐘到「三千院」,苔蘚庭園裡的地藏石像戴著紅色小圍兜,是當地人為夭折孩童祈福的習俗,守寺的婆婆看我盯著石像看,主動跟我解釋這個習俗的由來,語氣輕柔卻帶著一種對生死平靜的態度。

離開三千院時,我在路邊一間家庭經營的漬物小舖「志ば久」停下腳步,店主是一位七十多歲的婆婆,身上還穿著傳統的「大原女」藍染工作服。她一邊招呼我試吃紫蘇醃漬的柴漬け,一邊聊起過去大原的女性會頭頂柴薪與醃菜,徒步走進京都市區叫賣維生,這個傳統被稱為「大原女」,如今每年秋天大原地區還會舉辦「大原女まつり」重現這段歷史。她說起八月盂蘭盆節時,家家戶戶會在門口點起迎魂火,提醒祖先回家的路,而送走祖先那晚則會放水燈,「京都人對死亡沒那麼害怕,因為每年都要親手送他們一次」。這句話讓我在回程的巴士上想了很久。

有些味道不需要走遠,只需要願意在清晨六點半站進同一條隊伍裡,和京都人一起,把日子過得慢一點。

夜晚,船岡温泉與京都人的日常

回到市區已近八點,我沒有直接回旅館,而是走到北大路附近千本通上的「船岡温泉」。這是一棟大正時期留下的木造澡堂,門口雕滿唐破風屋簷與精緻的欄間木雕,主題是桃太郎和松鶴,燈光昏黃,推開拉門那一刻,肥皂與熱水蒸氣的氣味撲面而來。入湯費490日圓,我照著牆上貼的規矩,先在小板凳上徹底沖洗身體才進浴池,浴池分成藥湯、電氣風呂、露天湯好幾種,一位泡在藥湯裡的阿伯看我猶豫要不要試電氣風呂,笑著用手比劃電流的觸感提醒我「這個會麻,慢慢泡進去」。

浴池裡沒有人滑手機,大家或閉眼靜坐,或低聲閒聊今天的天氣與菜價,那種鬆弛感是白天觀光區永遠感受不到的。泡完澡走出來,我學著其他人的樣子,站在休息區的角落,一口氣喝完一瓶冰涼的水果牛乳,玻璃瓶身還帶著冰箱的霧氣,甜味在喉嚨裡化開,身體卻還殘留著溫泉的熱氣,那種冷熱交錯的滿足感,是這趟京都私房路線裡我最捨不得結束的一刻。走出澡堂,千本通的路燈把石板路照成暖黃色,遠處傳來自行車鈴聲,我忽然明白,京都真正的秘境,從來不是某個特定的地點,而是願意放慢腳步、跟著當地人的作息生活一天的那種心情。

回程班機上,我翻著在恵文社買的那本攝影集,想起老先生在御劔社前誦經的背影、六花咖啡店老闆娘手沖咖啡的專注神情、志ば久婆婆藍染工作服上的皺褶,以及船岡温泉裡那瓶果汁牛乳的冰涼觸感。這些畫面比任何一張清水寺的夕陽照片都更深刻地留在我心裡。下次如果你也要去京都,不妨試著把行程留白,留給那些沒有寫在旅遊書上的巷弄與澡堂——那才是京都人真正生活著的樣子。

ENGLISH

When my flight touched down at Kansai Airport, I wasn't thinking about the wooden stage of Kiyomizu-dera or the shoulder-to-shoulder crowds in the Arashiyama bamboo grove — I'd already seen those images a thousand times on Instagram. This was my fifth trip to Kyoto, but the first time I left the guidebook at the bottom of my suitcase and instead carried only a hand-drawn map from a friend who has lived in the city for twelve years. In the corner of the map she'd scrawled a single line in pencil: 'The Kyoto locals know is never the one on the tourist map.' Ten days later, as I left the city, I finally understood the weight of that sentence.

5:30 AM: The Other Face of Fushimi Inari

I set my alarm for five and stumbled out of my old-fashioned guesthouse near Tofuku-ji almost in darkness. Kyoto at dawn is eerily quiet — just the hum of a newspaper delivery scooter and the distant caw of crows. I caught the first JR Nara Line train, and five minutes later stepped off at Inari Station, sharing the platform with only two local men who looked like they were heading to work. Outside the station, the entrance to the thousand torii gates looked almost haunting in the morning mist — no flag-waving tour guides, no queue for photos, just a thin layer of dew on the stone steps that made a faint slipping sound underfoot.

I followed the tunnel of vermillion gates uphill, and about twenty minutes later reached the first fork, Yotsutsuji, where the view suddenly opens up over the entire Kyoto basin, morning light just beginning to gild the rooftops toward Higashiyama. Most tourists turn back here, but following my friend's map I kept climbing toward the lesser-known Mikenari Shrine and further up to the summit, Ichinomine. That stretch took another forty minutes, steep and narrow, and I regretted wearing sandals instead of hiking shoes. Along the way, stone fox statues appeared and vanished in the fog, moss coated ancient little shrines, and the rustle of bamboo leaves sounded almost like the mountain whispering.

In front of Mikenari Shrine I met a man in his seventies quietly chanting with prayer beads in hand. Noticing me linger, he explained in a mix of broken English and Japanese that the shrine honors the god of swords, and that locals climb up once a month. 'Tourists only get to Yotsutsuji,' he said with a smile. 'The real Mount Inari is up here.' The round trip took nearly two and a half hours, and aside from a 150-yen can of coffee from a vending machine and a small incense offering, it cost me almost nothing. By the time I came down past eight, tour buses were already unloading crowds at the base — a contrast that made me deeply grateful for the early start.

When the thousand torii gates hold nothing but my own breath and the silence of stone foxes, Kyoto finally sheds its tourist makeup and shows its raw, devotional face.

Ichijoji: Between Bookshops and Temple Alleys

From Fushimi Inari I transferred through the Keihan Line to the Eizan Railway, arriving about forty minutes later at Ichijoji Station, a neighborhood favored by Kyoto University students and writers. Tucked into its side streets is Keibunsha Ichijoji, arguably Japan's most beloved independent bookstore. The wooden floor creaked softly underfoot, a clerk quietly wrapped a secondhand poetry collection in the corner, and the air smelled of old paper mingled with incense. I lingered nearly an hour, flipping through photography books I'd never heard of, and eventually bought a black-and-white photo collection by a local Kyoto photographer for about 2,200 yen.

A ten-minute walk through residential alleys brought me to Shisen-do, a temple almost entirely free of tourists. Admission is 500 yen, and its dry rock garden glowed a pale white under the midday sun, punctuated every few minutes by the sharp knock of a bamboo water deer-scarer, a reminder that time was still moving. Five minutes further sits Konpuku-ji, home to a garden once tended by the haiku poet Yosa Buson; it was so quiet I could hear my own footsteps on the tatami. Nearby, I ducked into a five-seat kissaten called Rokka, where the owner brewed me a siphon coffee, its aroma mixing with the scent of osmanthus drifting in from outside. She'd lived in the neighborhood for thirty years, she told me with a laugh. 'Tourists all head to Arashiyama. This alley is as quiet as another Kyoto entirely.'

An Afternoon at the Kamogawa Delta and a Bean Mochi Pilgrimage

By noon I rode back to Demachiyanagi and walked along the riverbank to the Kamogawa Delta, where the Kamo and Takano rivers meet. A row of stepping stones shaped like turtles and plovers stretches across the shallow water; university students strummed guitars and walked dogs on the rocks, kids shrieked as they splashed, and the muffled beat of taiko drum practice drifted from the far bank. I took off my shoes and crossed the sun-warmed stones, the water below icy and clear enough to see minnows darting past my feet. That moment of unhurried ease felt closer to the Kyoto I'd imagined than any photo of the Arashiyama bamboo grove ever had.

Three minutes past the river stands Demachi Futaba, a shop that locals treat with something close to reverence, even if the queue isn't outrageous. Its signature item, the mame-mochi bean rice cake, costs 200 yen apiece. The salty crunch of black soybeans bursts first on the tongue, followed by the delicate sweetness of smooth red bean paste, the mochi skin chewy but never sticky — you can even feel the faint crispness of the soybean husks as you bite down. I bought three, and the clerk wrapped them in oiled paper with a gentle reminder to eat them the same day. That insistence on freshness explained instantly why locals are willing to queue here daily.

That afternoon I rode back to Ichijoji and squeezed into Takayasu, an eight-seat counter ramen shop favored by local office workers on Kyoto's famous 'ramen street.' I ordered the signature tonkotsu-shoyu ramen for about 850 yen. The broth was thick as melted marrow, glistening with a slick of oil, the chashu pork so tender it nearly dissolved, paired with firm, thin noodles that made the whole counter echo with slurping. The owner darted back and forth drenched in sweat, still finding time to chat warmly with regulars. That meal taught me that Kyoto's real food scene rarely lives in the tourist-heavy stalls of Nishiki Market — it lives in alleyway counters where only locals have the patience to queue.

Ohara: Shiso Fields and the Stillness of Jakko-in

Before evening fell, I boarded a Kyoto city bus near Demachiyanagi and rattled along for nearly an hour to Ohara. The elevation here is noticeably higher, the air cooler, and fields of shiso stretch along both sides of the road, releasing a distinct herbal tang whenever the wind picks up. I first walked into Jakko-in, dedicated to Empress Kenreimon-in, so quiet that only wind chimes and cicadas filled the air, with barely another visitor in sight. A fifteen-minute walk brought me to Sanzen-in, its moss garden dotted with small stone Jizo statues wearing red bibs — a local custom of prayer for children who died too young. An elderly woman tending the temple noticed me staring and explained the tradition to me herself, her voice gentle, carrying a calm acceptance of life and death that I wasn't expecting.

On my way out, I stopped at a small family-run pickle shop called Shibakyu, run by a woman in her seventies still dressed in the traditional indigo work clothes of the Ohara-me. As she offered me a taste of shiso-pickled shibazuke, she told me how women here once carried firewood and pickles on their heads and walked into Kyoto city to sell them for a living — a tradition called Ohara-me, still celebrated every autumn in a local festival that reenacts the old journey. She spoke about the Obon festival in August, when families light welcoming fires at their doorways to guide ancestors' spirits home, and float lanterns down the river on the night they send them off again. 'Kyoto people aren't afraid of death,' she said. 'Every year, we send them off with our own hands.' I thought about that sentence the entire bus ride back.

Some flavors don't require traveling far — just the willingness to stand in the same line at 6:30 in the morning, and let a day unfold at a Kyoto pace.

Night: Funaoka Onsen and the Rhythm of Ordinary Kyoto

By the time I returned to the city it was nearly eight, and instead of heading straight back to my guesthouse, I walked to Funaoka Onsen on Senbon Street near Kitaoji. It's a wooden bathhouse dating back to the Taisho era, its entrance carved with an elaborate karahafu roof and wooden transoms depicting Momotaro and cranes. The lighting inside is dim and amber, and the moment I slid open the door, the smell of soap and steam hit me all at once. Admission was 490 yen. Following the house rules posted on the wall, I scrubbed thoroughly on a low stool before entering the baths, which range from herbal soaks to an electric bath to an open-air pool. An older man soaking in the herbal tub noticed my hesitation about the electric bath and, laughing, mimed the sensation of a mild current. 'It tingles,' he said. 'Ease yourself in slowly.'

No one in the bath was scrolling on a phone. People sat with eyes closed or chatted quietly about the weather or the price of vegetables — a kind of ease you never feel in the daytime tourist districts. Stepping out afterward, I did what everyone else did: stood in the corner of the lounge and drank an entire bottle of chilled fruit milk in one go, the glass bottle still fogged from the fridge, sweetness spreading down my throat while my body still radiated the bath's heat. That collision of hot and cold was the moment on this entire hidden-Kyoto route I was most reluctant to let end. Walking out, the streetlights along Senbon Street cast the stone pavement in warm amber, a bicycle bell rang somewhere in the distance, and I finally understood: Kyoto's real hidden gem was never a single location. It was the willingness to slow down and live, for one day, on a local's schedule.

On the flight home, I flipped through the photo book I'd bought at Keibunsha, remembering the old man chanting in front of Mikenari Shrine, the focused expression of the kissaten owner at Rokka pouring my coffee, the creases in the indigo work clothes worn by the pickle-shop grandmother at Shibakyu, and the cold glass bottle of fruit milk at Funaoka Onsen. Those images stayed with me far more vividly than any sunset photo of Kiyomizu-dera ever could. If you're planning your own trip to Kyoto, leave some blank space in your itinerary — save it for the alleys and bathhouses that never make it into the guidebooks. That's where Kyoto actually lives.
