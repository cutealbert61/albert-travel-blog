中文

飛機降落在El Prat機場那個早晨,天空是那種洗過的藍,乾淨到有點不真實。拖著行李箱走出航廈時,我腦子裡其實只裝著兩件事:高第的教堂,還有傳說中永遠人聲鼎沸的市場。但巴塞隆納給我的第一堂課,卻是在抵達後的第三個清晨,一個我原本沒排進行程的小廣場——它教會我,這座城市華麗的立面底下,藏著遠比明信片複雜的傷痕與記憶。

清晨的傷痕:哥德區裡的彈孔與記憶

清晨六點半,哥德區還沒被觀光客佔領,只有麵包店剛升起鐵捲門的聲音,和石板路上偶爾滾過的垃圾車輪子。我照著地圖鑽進一條窄到只容兩人並肩的巷子,轉個彎,眼前忽然開闊——一座安靜得像被時間遺忘的小廣場,聖菲利浦內里廣場(Plaça de Sant Felip Neri)。教堂立面在晨光裡泛著蜜色,但走近一看,我愣住了:整片石牆上密密麻麻布滿了坑洞,像是被巨大的釘耙狠狠刮過。

那不是歲月風化的痕跡。旁邊一塊不起眼的小銅牌寫著:1938年1月30日,義大利空軍受佛朗哥陣營請託,對巴塞隆納執行地毯式轟炸,其中一枚炸彈直接命中這座廣場,四十二人當場喪生,大多數是在附近學校排隊躲避空襲的孩童。牆面上的坑洞,是彈殼碎片留下的傷口,將近九十年過去,巴塞隆納市政府始終沒有把它們填平。

有些城市選擇忘記傷口,巴塞隆納選擇讓它留在牆上,天天路過,天天記得。

我站在廣場中央,聽見附近小學隱約傳來孩子的嬉鬧聲——現在這裡仍然是一間學校的所在地。陽光斜斜切過廣場,把彈孔的陰影拉得又長又深。一位晨跑經過的老先生看我盯著牆看太久,停下腳步用簡單的英文說:「這是我們的記憶,不是裝飾。」他說完就慢跑離開,留我一個人站在那片彈痕累累的牆前,忽然覺得手上的相機顯得太輕率。

猶太區的沉默:El Call 與一場被遺忘的驅逐

從聖菲利浦內里廣場往南走不到五分鐘,巷弄忽然變得更窄、更暗,陽光被兩側四五層樓高的老建築切成一條細細的光帶。這裡是El Call,中世紀巴塞隆納的猶太區,名字來自希伯來文「Qahal」,意指「社群」。我在Carrer de Marlet這條巷子裡找到一塊嵌在牆角、字跡已經模糊的希伯來文石碑,上面記載著1314年的年份,那是這裡曾經是猶太會堂的證據。

導覽小冊上輕描淡寫地提到,1391年,一場席捲西班牙各城的反猶暴動蔓延到巴塞隆納,El Call在一夜之間遭到洗劫,數百人被殺,倖存者被迫改信天主教,社區從此再也沒有真正恢復元氣。一百年後,1492年天主教雙王頒布驅逐令,西班牙猶太人被要求改宗或離開,El Call的居民幾乎全數消失於官方紀錄之中。走在這條巷子裡,我一度以為自己聽錯,直到在Placa Manuel Ribé旁的一間小工作室,遇見一位修復老地圖的匠人馬克(Marc),他指著牆上一張復刻的十四世紀巴塞隆納地圖告訴我:「我們花了幾百年才敢重新承認,這裡曾經有過另一群巴塞隆納人。」

他說話時語氣很平靜,沒有控訴,只有一種近乎溫柔的遺憾。我站在那狹窄潮濕的巷弄裡,聞著石牆散發出的陳年濕氣,忽然明白,這座城市的觀光手冊很少提起這段歷史,不是因為它不重要,而是因為它太沉重,重到需要用六百年時間才能被輕輕說出口。

聖家堂:一磚一石堆疊的百年執念

如果哥德區教我認識傷痕,聖家堂教我認識的是巴塞隆納的另一種偏執——對未完成之美的耐心。我提前兩週在官網訂了早上9:30的入場時段,基本票含語音導覽是26歐元,若想爬上受難立面塔樓,要另加約14歐元,且需在購票時指定時段,人數有限。

那天早上8點50分我就到了,排隊隊伍已經蜿蜒到街角,但因為有預約時段,大約只等了十五分鐘就通過類似機場安檢的行李檢查。一進門,我整個人是被光線打懵的——正殿內部的彩繪玻璃在上午十點左右,陽光從東側灑進來,把整片石柱森林染成一片流動的金紅色與藍綠色,像走進一片會呼吸的海底森林。高第曾說教堂的柱子要像樹木一樣分岔生長,那一刻我才真正懂他在說什麼。

我選擇爬受難立面(Passion Tower)的電梯上塔,單程電梯,下樓則要走非常窄、僅容一人通過的螺旋石梯,大約365級,膝蓋不好的人建議三思。塔頂能俯瞰整個艾克薩姆普勒區(Eixample)方格狀的街廓,風很大,耳邊還能聽到底下施工吊車的鋼索聲——沒錯,聖家堂至今仍是工地,預計要到2026年高第逝世百年才可能真正完工。走下塔後,我特地繞去地下室墓室,高第本人就安葬在那裡,墓室很小,遊客不多,反而顯得莊重。整趟行程,從排隊到走出紀念品店,我大約花了兩個半小時。

高第從沒想過自己能親眼看見完工的那天,他蓋的不是一座建築,而是一種留給後人的信仰。

波蓋利亞市場:一口下肚的巴塞隆納日常

逛完聖家堂已經下午一點多,我搭地鐵L5轉L3到Liceu站,鑽出地鐵口就是聞名的波蓋利亞市場(Mercat de la Boqueria)。市場入口處水果攤把石榴、無花果、切好的芒果堆成鮮豔的小山,空氣裡混著海鮮攤位的鹹腥味和現榨柳橙汁的甜香。我直接繞到市場中段那間永遠圍滿人的吧檯——Bar Pinotxo,創始人Juanito已經退休,現在多由他兒子Jordi掌廚,但那股江湖地位絲毫未減。

吧檯只有大概二十個高腳椅位,我運氣不錯,等了十分鐘就有位子。點了一份鷹嘴豆燉血腸(cigrons amb botifarra negra)、一份炙烤茄子甜椒沙拉(escalivada)配鹹鱈魚,再加一杯冰鎮卡瓦氣泡酒(cava),總共大約19歐元。血腸燉鷹嘴豆端上桌時還在冒煙,豆子燉得綿密幾乎入口即化,血腸切片外皮微焦,咬下去是扎實的胡椒與丁香氣味,和卡瓦的酸氣泡在嘴裡剛好中和。廚師就在我面前不到一公尺的距離現炒現做,鑄鐵鍋與鏟子碰撞的聲音、油爆蒜頭的香氣,還有隔壁客人跟廚師用加泰隆尼亞語互相調侃的笑聲,整個吧檯像一個永遠不會冷場的廚房派對。我旁邊坐著一位看起來是附近上班族的中年女士,她一個人靜靜喝著白酒配鯷魚,看我拿手機拍照拍個不停,笑著用英文說:「觀光客都拍照,我們只是想吃頓飯。」那句話讓我瞬間有點不好意思,但也讓我更確定,這裡的美味從來不是表演給遊客看的。

格拉西亞的屋頂與玫瑰:碉堡、節慶與尾聲燈火

旅程最後一個傍晚,我特地留給格拉西亞區(Gràcia)。從Fontana地鐵站出來,沿著Travessera de Dalt一路往上坡走,大約二十五分鐘,兩腿開始有點痠時,眼前忽然出現一片裸露的紅土坡地與斑駁水泥結構——卡梅爾碉堡(Bunkers del Carmel)。這裡原本是西班牙內戰時期為了對抗那些轟炸哥德區的飛機所建的高射砲陣地,如今砲台早已撤走,只剩下混凝土平台,卻意外成了本地人最愛的免費看夕陽地點。我到的時候大約傍晚七點半,已經有一群年輕人帶著啤酒和吉他坐在邊緣,夕陽把整座巴塞隆納連同遠方地中海染成一片橘紅,聖家堂的塔尖在天際線上像根細細的針。

下山後我走進格拉西亞的巷弄,正好撞見居民在為八月的格拉西亞大節(Festa Major de Gràcia)提前布置——雖然還沒到日期,但幾條街已經開始搭鷹架、掛紙紮裝飾的骨架。一位在陽台上綁彩帶的婦人告訴我,格拉西亞每年八月中會有超過十七條街道各自用回收材料搭出主題裝飾,街坊互相較勁,誰家最有創意誰就是那年的驕傲,「這不是市政府辦的活動,是我們自己辦的。」她說這話時語氣裡有種不容置疑的自豪。她又提到四月23日的聖喬治節(Sant Jordi),那天全城書店與玫瑰攤位會擺滿蘭布拉大道,男生送女生玫瑰,女生回送一本書,傳統源自屠龍騎士聖喬治的傳說,也巧合地是塞萬提斯與莎士比亞的忌日。她說她先生每年都會在那天送她一本詩集,「這座城市炸過、搶過、也窮過,但我們從沒停止過送花跟送書。」

那晚我沒有直接回旅館,而是繞到附近一間還亮著燈的舊書攤,老闆正在收拾攤位,見我駐足便隨手遞來一本西班牙文詩集,笑說「不懂也沒關係,巴塞隆納的書從來不是拿來看完的,是拿來記得的。」我買下那本詩集,走回旅館的路上,城市的燈一盞盞亮起,聖家堂的吊車還在遠處運作,哥德區的彈孔還在牆上,而格拉西亞的巷子裡已經飄著準備迎接八月節慶的興奮氣息。這座城市把炸彈的傷疤與送花的浪漫收進同一個口袋,毫不違和地天天拿出來過日子——這大概就是巴塞隆納教我的最後一課。

ENGLISH

The plane touched down at El Prat that morning under a sky so freshly washed blue it looked almost fake. Dragging my suitcase out of the terminal, my head was full of exactly two things: Gaudí's cathedral, and the market everyone kept telling me I had to eat at. But Barcelona's first real lesson came on my third morning, in a small square I hadn't even planned to visit — and it taught me that beneath this city's postcard-perfect facades lie scars and memories far more complicated than any brochure lets on.

Wounds at Dawn: Bullet Scars in the Gothic Quarter

At six thirty in the morning, the Gothic Quarter still belonged to no one but the bakers rolling up their shutters and the occasional garbage truck rattling over cobblestones. Following my map through an alley barely wide enough for two people to pass, I turned a corner and the space suddenly opened up into a small, oddly silent square — Plaça de Sant Felip Neri. The church facade glowed honey-gold in the early light, but up close I stopped short: the entire stone wall was pitted with hundreds of small craters, as if something enormous had raked its claws across it.

These weren't marks of weathering. A modest plaque nearby explained: on January 30, 1938, Italian bombers, flying in support of Franco's forces, carried out a saturation bombing raid on Barcelona. One bomb struck this exact square, killing forty-two people instantly — most of them children sheltering at a nearby school. The pockmarks in the wall are shrapnel wounds. Nearly ninety years later, the city has never filled them in.

Some cities choose to forget their wounds. Barcelona chose to leave them on the wall, to walk past them every single day, and remember.

I stood in the middle of the square listening to the faint sound of children's laughter drifting from the school still operating there today. The morning sun cut long shadows across the bullet scars. An elderly man jogging past noticed me staring too long and stopped to say, in simple English, 'This is our memory, not decoration,' before jogging on. I stood there alone in front of that battered wall, suddenly feeling that the camera in my hand was far too casual a tool for what I was looking at.

The Silence of El Call: A Forgotten Expulsion

Less than five minutes south of the square, the alleys grow narrower and darker, sunlight reduced to a thin ribbon between four- and five-story buildings. This is El Call, Barcelona's medieval Jewish quarter, its name derived from the Hebrew 'qahal,' meaning community. On Carrer de Marlet I found a weathered Hebrew inscription embedded in a corner wall, dated to 1314, evidence that a synagogue once stood there.

The guidebooks mention, almost in passing, that in 1391 a wave of anti-Jewish riots swept across Spain and reached Barcelona, gutting El Call overnight. Hundreds were killed, survivors forced to convert, and the community never fully recovered. A century later, in 1492, the Catholic Monarchs issued the Alhambra Decree, demanding Spain's Jews convert or leave — and El Call's residents largely vanished from official records after that. Standing in that narrow lane, I thought I'd misheard, until I met Marc, a craftsman restoring old maps in a small studio near Plaça Manuel Ribé. Pointing to a reproduction of a fourteenth-century map of Barcelona on his wall, he told me, 'It took us centuries to admit there was once another Barcelona living here.'

He said it calmly, without accusation, just a quiet, almost tender regret. Standing in that damp, narrow alley breathing in the smell of ancient stone, I understood why this history rarely appears in tourist pamphlets — not because it's unimportant, but because it's heavy enough that it took six hundred years before anyone could say it out loud.

Sagrada Família: A Century-Long Obsession Built Stone by Stone

If the Gothic Quarter taught me about scars, Sagrada Família taught me about Barcelona's other obsession — patience for beauty that refuses to be finished. I'd booked a 9:30am slot online two weeks in advance. The basic ticket with audio guide ran about 26 euros; climbing the Passion Tower cost an extra 14 euros and required booking a specific time slot with limited capacity.

I arrived at 8:50am to a line already curling around the corner, but thanks to the timed entry, I only waited about fifteen minutes to pass through an airport-style security check. The moment I stepped inside, the light knocked me sideways — around ten o'clock, sunlight pours through the stained glass on the eastern side, washing the entire forest of columns in flowing gold, red, blue and green, like walking through a breathing underwater forest. Gaudí once said the columns should branch like trees; standing there, I finally understood exactly what he meant.

I chose the elevator up the Passion Tower — a one-way ride up, followed by a descent down an extremely narrow spiral staircase of roughly 365 steps, single file only, not recommended for anyone with knee trouble. From the top, the entire grid of the Eixample district spreads out below, wind whipping past, construction cranes still audible groaning somewhere beneath — yes, Sagrada Família remains, quite literally, a construction site, not expected to be complete until 2026, the centenary of Gaudí's death. After descending, I detoured into the crypt, where Gaudí himself is buried. It's small, quiet, and far less crowded than the rest of the building, which somehow made it feel more solemn. From queue to gift shop, the whole visit took about two and a half hours.

Gaudí never expected to live to see it finished. What he built wasn't just a building — it was a faith he left behind for others to carry.

La Boqueria Market: One Bite of Barcelona's Ordinary Life

By the time I left Sagrada Família it was past one in the afternoon. I took the metro, L5 then transferring to L3, and emerged at Liceu station right at the entrance of the famous Mercat de la Boqueria. Fruit stalls near the entrance stacked pomegranates, figs, and sliced mangoes into bright little pyramids, the air a mix of briny seafood and freshly squeezed orange juice. I headed straight for the always-packed counter in the middle of the market — Bar Pinotxo. Founder Juanito has retired, and his son Jordi now runs the kitchen, but the legendary reputation hasn't dimmed one bit.

The counter seats maybe twenty people on high stools; I got lucky and only waited ten minutes. I ordered chickpeas stewed with black pudding (cigrons amb botifarra negra), a roasted eggplant and pepper salad with salt cod (escalivada), and a chilled glass of cava — about 19 euros total. The chickpea stew arrived still steaming, the beans so soft they nearly dissolved on the tongue, the black pudding sliced and slightly charred on the outside, bursting with pepper and clove on the first bite, the cava's acidity cutting straight through the richness. The chef worked less than a meter in front of me, the clang of a cast-iron pan, the sizzle of garlic hitting hot oil, and the sound of regulars trading jokes with him in Catalan — the whole counter felt like a kitchen party that never runs out of energy. The middle-aged woman beside me, clearly a local on her lunch break, sipped white wine with anchovies in silence, watching me photograph everything, before smiling and saying in English, 'Tourists take pictures. We just want to eat.' It stung a little, but it also confirmed something: the food here was never a performance for visitors.

Gràcia's Rooftops and Roses: Bunkers, Festivals, and the Last Lights

I saved my final evening for Gràcia. From Fontana metro station I walked uphill along Travessera de Dalt for about twenty-five minutes, my legs starting to ache, when suddenly a bare red-earth slope and weathered concrete structure appeared ahead — Bunkers del Carmel. These were originally anti-aircraft gun positions built during the Civil War to defend against the very same planes that bombed the Gothic Quarter. The guns are long gone, leaving only concrete platforms that have become, unofficially, the city's favorite free sunset spot. I arrived around 7:30pm to find a group of young people already sitting at the edge with beer and a guitar. The setting sun turned all of Barcelona, and the Mediterranean beyond it, a deep burnt orange, the spire of Sagrada Família standing on the skyline like a thin needle.

Walking down into Gràcia's back streets afterward, I stumbled onto residents already scaffolding early decorations for August's Festa Major de Gràcia, months before the actual date. A woman tying ribbons on her balcony explained that each August, more than seventeen streets in the neighborhood build elaborate decorations out of recycled materials, competing against each other for creative glory. 'This isn't something the city hall organizes,' she said, with unmistakable pride. 'It's ours.' She also mentioned Sant Jordi, the April 23rd festival where the entire city fills with book stalls and rose vendors along La Rambla — men give women roses, women give men books, a tradition rooted in the legend of the dragon-slaying knight Saint George, and, coincidentally, the anniversary of both Cervantes' and Shakespeare's deaths. Her husband, she said, gives her a book of poetry every year without fail. 'This city has been bombed, looted, and gone hungry,' she told me, 'but it has never once stopped giving flowers and books.'

I didn't head straight back to the hotel that night. Instead I wandered to a small secondhand bookstall still lit up nearby, where the owner, packing up for the day, handed me a book of Spanish poetry and said with a grin, 'It's fine if you don't understand it. Books in Barcelona were never meant to be finished — they're meant to be remembered.' I bought it, and walked back with the city's lights coming on one by one, cranes still working somewhere near Sagrada Família, bullet scars still sitting quietly on a wall in the Gothic Quarter, and the streets of Gràcia already humming with anticipation for a festival months away. This city carries its bomb scars and its rose-giving romance in the very same pocket, pulling both out daily without contradiction — and that, in the end, was the last and best lesson Barcelona taught me.
