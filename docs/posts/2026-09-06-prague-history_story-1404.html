中文

凌晨五點五十分,布拉格舊城的石板路還浸在夜色裡。我一個人從住處走出來,呼出的白氣在路燈下散開又消失。空氣裡混著潮濕石灰、隔夜啤酒與剛出爐麵包的味道,這股味道後來成了我在布拉格每個早晨確認自己真的醒著的方式——因為眼前的一切,實在太像一場精心佈置的舞台劇。

我要去的地方,是舊城廣場。天還沒亮,廣場空得只剩下我和幾隻鴿子,還有一座從1410年就矗立在這裡、據說藏著一個殘忍故事的天文鐘。

黎明的天文鐘:一則關於眼睛的傳說

導遊書上只寫「布拉格天文鐘,建於1410年,歐洲現存最古老的天文鐘」,但真正讓我在原地站了快二十分鐘的,是站在鐘下賣咖啡的老先生隨口說的一句話。他說,傳說市議會怕鐘匠哈努什(Hanuš)日後為別的城市打造更精美的鐘,毀了布拉格的獨一無二,於是找人把他弄瞎。哈努什請求最後一次靠近鐘的內部機械,他伸手進齒輪深處——鐘停了,據說他也倒下了,天文鐘因此沉默了將近百年。

六點整,鐘樓上方的小窗打開,一位真人吹號手探出頭,朝著空無一人的廣場吹了一段中世紀曲調,聲音在石牆間彈回來,孤獨又莊嚴。我站在原地,忽然明白這座城市最迷人的地方,從來不是它有多美,而是美的背後總藏著一筆帳。

這座城市從不掩飾傷口,它只是把傷口鑲進了金框裡,讓你一邊讚嘆一邊心驚。

查理大橋:蛋、國王與河上的光

八點半,我沿著卡爾洛瓦街(Karlova)七拐八彎走向查理大橋,途中被兩間紀念品店和一間烘焙坊的肉桂捲香氣拖慢了腳步。查理大橋建於1357年,傳說皇帝查理四世特別選定7月9日凌晨5點31分動工,因為那組數字1-3-5-7-9-7-5-3-1從頭到尾唸都是回文,他相信這樣的橋才夠堅固。更有趣的是另一則傳說:為了讓橋的砂漿更堅固,皇帝下令附近村莊繳交雞蛋摻入建材,結果有個叫維爾瓦利(Velvary)的村子怕雞蛋在運送途中碰碎,竟先把雞蛋煮熟才送來——這批「熟蛋橋」的段落至今仍是導遊們最愛拿來逗笑遊客的橋段。

九點的晨光斜斜灑在橋上三十座巴洛克雕像上,遊客還不多,我摸了摸聖約翰內波穆克(St. John of Nepomuk)雕像底座那塊被摸得發亮的青銅浮雕——傳說他在1393年因拒絕向國王洩漏王后的告解內容,被人從這座橋上扔進伏爾塔瓦河淹死,後來成了橋梁與船夫的守護聖人。當地人說摸一下能保佑重返布拉格,我摸的時候手心是冰的,河面上剛好有一艘遊船緩緩劃過,漾開一圈圈金色的水紋。

城堡裡的一扇窗:三十年戰爭的起點與塔頂的287階

十點半,我搭22號電車從馬拉斯特拉納站(Malostranská)上山,在Pražský hrad站下車,順著人潮走進馬提亞斯門(Matthias Gate),剛好趕上中午十二點的整點衛兵交接儀式,銅管樂隊吹奏,遊客擠在鐵欄杆前拍照,場面比想像中更盛大也更觀光化。

穿過第二庭院,聖維特大教堂(Katedrála svatého Víta)的尖塔幾乎要刺穿天空。我排隊二十分鐘買了聯票(當時Circuit B約250克朗),再另外花250克朗買了南塔登頂票——這是我覺得布拉格城堡最值得的一筆花費。塔內的螺旋樓梯窄得只容一人側身通過,287階,爬到三分之二我開始懷疑人生,腿在發抖,石壁摸起來冰涼粗糙,偶爾有下樓的人擦身而過還得互相側身讓路。爬到頂端那一刻,紅色屋瓦鋪滿眼前,伏爾塔瓦河像一條銀色緞帶,風很大,吹得人幾乎站不穩,但那一刻的視野讓前面十五分鐘的喘不過氣全部值回票價。

下塔之後,我走進舊皇宮(Starý královský palác),站在拉迪斯拉夫大廳旁那間看似普通的房間裡,導覽員說,就是這裡,1618年5月23日,新教貴族把兩名天主教總督與一名秘書從窗口扔了出去,足足摔了十七公尺,落在城堡護城河邊的一堆糞肥和垃圾上,三人竟奇蹟生還。天主教徒說是天使接住了他們,新教徒則笑說多虧那堆糞。這起被稱為「第二次布拉格擲窗事件」的鬧劇,竟直接點燃了往後三十年、席捲整個歐洲的三十年戰爭。我站在那扇窗邊往下看,護城河早已鋪成草坪,很難想像一場改變歐洲命運的戰爭,起點竟是這麼荒謬的一幕。

走出皇宮,我拐進黃金巷(Zlatá ulička),22號那間藍色小門的房子,是卡夫卡在1916到1917年間借住寫作的地方,門口窄得我幾乎得側身才能探頭進去看一眼那張小書桌。整個城堡區我大概逛了三個多小時,腳程加上排隊,建議至少留半天時間才不會走馬看花。

猶太區的黃昏:哥雷姆、墓碑與燭光的記憶

下午三點,我沿著河岸走回舊城,拐進猶太區約瑟夫城(Josefov)。老猶太墓園(Starý židovský hřbitov)裡墓碑歪斜擁擠,一塊疊著一塊,導覽說由於中世紀猶太人被限制只能葬在這塊狹小土地上,墓穴最多疊了十二層,地表可見的墓碑就有一萬兩千多塊,實際埋葬的人數可能超過十萬。最古老的一塊屬於詩人阿維格多.卡拉(Avigdor Kara),立於1439年。

老猶太墓園旁的舊新猶太會堂(Staronová synagoga)外牆有一道通往閣樓的梯子,至今仍在,卻永遠沒有人被允許爬上去。傳說十六世紀的猶大.羅耶拉比(Rabbi Judah Loew)用伏爾塔瓦河邊的黏土捏出一個哥雷姆(Golem),賦予生命保護飽受迫害的猶太社區,直到某個安息日前,拉比取出牠口中寫著「emet」(真理)的咒語紙條,哥雷姆的身軀便安放在這間會堂的閣樓裡,再也沒有人敢把它取下——傳說二戰期間曾有納粹軍官試圖闖入閣樓,結果離奇死亡。真假難辨,但站在梯子下抬頭望,我確實起了一身雞皮疙瘩。

穿過猶太區,我在平卡斯猶太會堂(Pinkasova synagoga)裡看見牆上密密麻麻手寫的77297個名字,每一個都是二戰中遇難的捷克猶太人。這些名字在1960年代曾被共產政權以「牆面潮濕受損」為由粉刷抹去,直到1990年代才由畫家重新一筆一筆寫回牆上。我在其中一面牆前站了很久,找到一個和我一位朋友同姓的名字,忽然覺得歷史書裡的數字有了重量。

夜色下的啤酒館:svíčková與一位老闆娘的往事

傍晚五點半,我沿著河岸走到民族大道(Národní třída),路邊一面不起眼的青銅浮雕牆吸引了我——那是紀念1989年11月17日的紀念碑,浮雕是一雙雙伸向天空的手。牆下擺著幾十根還在燃燒的蠟燭,一位頭髮花白的先生正彎腰添上一根新的。他自我介紹叫帕維爾,英文帶著濃濃捷克腔,說他1989年那晚就在這條街上,當時他是大學生,警察用棍棒驅散學生的和平示威,消息一夜之間傳遍全國,連鎖引發了後來被稱為「絲絨革命」的政權崩解,幾個星期後,劇作家哈維爾就成了新總統。他說每年這一天,布拉格人都會回到這裡點一根蠟燭,「不是為了紀念一場勝利,是提醒自己自由有多容易被拿走。」這句話我記到現在。

他們不是在悼念過去,而是在提醒彼此:別讓遺忘,成為下一場悲劇的開端。

晚上七點,我走進1499年就開始營業、布拉格現存最古老的自釀酒館U Fleků。深色木樑、滿牆壁畫,長條木桌擠滿了觀光客與本地人,侍者端著托盤穿梭,用粉筆在杯墊上畫記號算酒錢,手風琴樂手挨桌演奏。我點了招牌的奶油醬燒牛肉(svíčková na smetaně,約265克朗),端上來時肉片浸在淡橘色的根莖蔬菜奶油醬裡,上面點綴一坨鮮奶油、幾顆蔓越莓和一片檸檬,配著吸飽醬汁的麵包丸子(knedlík)。第一口下去,醬汁濃郁帶點根莖蔬菜的自然回甘,鮮奶油的微酸和檸檬的清爽剛好切開油膩感,麵包丸子綿軟得像海綿一樣把醬汁全吸了進去。配的是這裡獨家的13度黑啤酒,麥芽香濃厚帶點焦糖尾韻,苦味很輕,完全不像我印象中的黑啤酒那樣厚重。

負責我這桌的老闆娘雅娜,聊起這間店已經傳到第五代,她笑著說捷克人喝啤酒有自己的執念,「溫度差一度我們都喝得出來,啤酒對我們來說,是比教堂還神聖的東西。」我笑著問她那教堂呢,她聳聳肩:「教堂用來許願,啤酒館用來說真話。」

晚上九點多,我散步回舊城廣場,天文鐘在夜色裡打著金色的燈,整點的號手又探出頭吹了一段曲子,廣場上這次擠滿了舉著手機的遊客。我站在人群後面,想起哈努什的傳說、想起那扇扔出總督的窗、想起牆上七萬七千個名字,還有帕維爾手裡那根蠟燭。布拉格從不假裝自己只有美麗這一面,它把每一道疤都留在原地,讓你自己走過去,自己看清楚。

ENGLISH

At 5:50 in the morning, the cobblestones of Old Town Prague were still soaked in darkness. I walked out of my apartment alone, my breath turning white under the streetlamps before dissolving into the cold air. The street smelled of damp limestone, stale beer, and fresh bread from some unseen bakery — a smell that, over the following days, became my way of confirming I was really awake, really here, because everything in front of me looked far too staged to be real.

I was heading to Old Town Square. The square was empty except for me, a few pigeons, and a clock tower that has stood there since 1410, carrying a story crueler than its beauty suggests.

The Dawn Clock: A Legend About Eyes

Every guidebook says the same thing: the Prague Astronomical Clock, built in 1410, is the oldest still-working astronomical clock in Europe. But what actually kept me standing there for twenty minutes was an offhand remark from an old man selling coffee beneath it. He told me the town councillors, fearing the clockmaker Hanuš might build an even finer clock for a rival city, had him blinded so Prague's clock would remain unmatched. Hanuš, they say, asked to be led to the clock's inner mechanism one last time. He reached into the gears — and the clock stopped. Some say he collapsed right there. The clock stayed silent for nearly a century afterward.

At exactly six o'clock, a small window above the clock face opened, and a real trumpeter leaned out to play a medieval fanfare into the empty square. The sound bounced off the stone walls, lonely and solemn. Standing there, I understood what makes this city so seductive: it isn't the beauty itself, but the fact that every piece of beauty comes with a ledger of debt attached.

This city never hides its wounds — it simply frames them in gold, so you admire and flinch at the same time.

Charles Bridge: Eggs, Kings, and Light on the River

By 8:30 I was zigzagging down Karlova Street toward Charles Bridge, slowed by the cinnamon-roll smell drifting from a bakery I hadn't planned on stopping at. The bridge was commissioned in 1357 by Emperor Charles IV, who supposedly chose the exact moment to begin construction — July 9th at 5:31 a.m. — because the date and time formed the palindrome 1-3-5-7-9-7-5-3-1, a sequence he believed would make the structure indestructible. A second, more comic legend claims the emperor ordered villages near Prague to send eggs to be mixed into the mortar for strength. One village, Velvary, worried the eggs would crack in transit, sent them hard-boiled instead. Local guides still love telling tourists about the bridge supposedly built with boiled eggs.

By nine, the morning light fell at a slant across the thirty baroque statues lining the bridge, and the crowds hadn't arrived yet. I reached out and touched the worn bronze relief at the base of the Statue of St. John of Nepomuk — the priest who, in 1393, was thrown off this very bridge and drowned for refusing to reveal the queen's confession to the king. He later became the patron saint of bridges and boatmen. Locals say touching the relief guarantees your return to Prague. My palm was cold when I touched it; a tour boat glided by just then, leaving rings of golden ripples on the water.

A Window in the Castle: Where the Thirty Years' War Began, and 287 Steps to the Top

At 10:30 I took tram 22 from Malostranská station up the hill, got off at the Pražský hrad stop, and joined the crowd flowing through the Matthias Gate — arriving just in time for the noon changing of the guard, complete with a brass fanfare and a wall of raised phones.

Through the second courtyard, the spires of St. Vitus Cathedral seemed to pierce straight through the sky. After a twenty-minute queue, I bought the combined Circuit B ticket (around 250 CZK at the time) plus a separate 250 CZK ticket to climb the South Tower — the best money I spent at the castle. The spiral staircase inside is barely wide enough for one person, and climbing all 287 steps left my legs shaking by the two-thirds mark; the stone walls were cold and rough under my hand, and every so often someone descending had to squeeze past me sideways. When I finally reached the top, the city's red-tiled roofs spread out below in every direction, the Vltava curling through it like a silver ribbon, and the wind was strong enough to make me grip the railing. Every gasping step suddenly felt worth it.

Descending, I walked into the Old Royal Palace and stood in an unremarkable-looking room near Vladislav Hall. This, the guide explained, was where on May 23, 1618, Protestant nobles threw two Catholic governors and a secretary out the window — a seventeen-meter fall onto a pile of manure and rubbish in the moat below. All three survived. Catholics claimed angels had caught them; Protestants joked it was the dung heap that did the saving. This absurd little episode, now known as the Second Defenestration of Prague, ignited the Thirty Years' War, a conflict that would consume Europe for the next three decades. I looked down from that same window — the moat is a manicured lawn now — and found it almost impossible to believe that a war reshaping a continent had begun with something this ridiculous.

Walking out, I ducked into Golden Lane, where house No. 22 — a tiny blue door I practically had to lean into — was where Kafka rented a room to write between 1916 and 1917. I spent roughly three and a half hours at the castle complex altogether; anyone visiting should plan at least half a day to avoid rushing through it.

Dusk in the Jewish Quarter: The Golem, the Gravestones, and the Candles

By three in the afternoon I'd walked back along the river into Josefov, the old Jewish quarter. At the Old Jewish Cemetery, the gravestones lean against each other in impossible clusters — the guide explained that because Jewish burials were confined to this small plot for centuries, graves were stacked up to twelve layers deep. More than 12,000 stones are still visible above ground, though the actual number buried may exceed 100,000. The oldest belongs to the poet Avigdor Kara, dated 1439.

Beside the cemetery, a ladder still leans against the outer wall of the Old-New Synagogue, leading up to an attic no one is permitted to enter. Legend says that in the sixteenth century, Rabbi Judah Loew shaped a creature — the Golem — from clay along the banks of the Vltava to protect the persecuted Jewish community. Before one Sabbath, the rabbi removed the parchment reading 'emet' (truth) from its mouth, and the Golem's body has supposedly rested in that attic ever since — untouched, according to one popular story, even by a Nazi officer who tried to climb up during the war and died under mysterious circumstances. True or not, standing beneath that ladder gave me actual goosebumps.

Further in, at the Pinkas Synagogue, the walls are covered floor to ceiling with 77,297 handwritten names — Czech Jews murdered in the Holocaust. The names were painted over by the Communist government in the 1960s under the excuse of water damage, and only restored letter by letter in the 1990s. I stood in front of one wall for a long time until I found a surname matching a friend's, and suddenly the abstract number in every history textbook had weight.

Beer Hall at Night: Svíčková and a Story from the Woman Behind the Bar

At 5:30 that evening, walking along the embankment toward Národní třída, I noticed an unassuming bronze relief on the wall — a memorial to November 17, 1989, showing hands reaching upward. Dozens of candles burned beneath it, and a grey-haired man was bending down to light a new one. He introduced himself as Pavel, his English thick with a Czech accent, and told me he'd been a university student on this very street that night, when police beat down a peaceful demonstration. News of the crackdown spread across the country overnight, triggering the chain of events later called the Velvet Revolution. Within weeks, the playwright Václav Havel became president. Every year on this date, he said, Praguers still return here to light a candle — 'not to celebrate a victory, but to remind ourselves how easily freedom can be taken away.' I've thought about that sentence ever since.

They aren't mourning the past. They're reminding each other not to let forgetting become the start of the next tragedy.

At seven, I stepped into U Fleků, a brewery-pub operating continuously since 1499 — the oldest in Prague. Dark wooden beams, murals covering every wall, long communal tables crowded with tourists and locals alike, waiters weaving through with trays and chalking tallies straight onto the coasters, an accordion player working table to table. I ordered the house specialty, svíčková na smetaně (about 265 CZK) — sirloin bathed in a pale orange, creamy root-vegetable sauce, topped with a dollop of whipped cream, a few cranberries, and a lemon slice, served alongside bread dumplings soaked through with sauce. The first bite was rich with the natural sweetness of the root vegetables, cut just enough by the cream's tang and the lemon's brightness to keep it from feeling heavy; the dumpling soaked up the sauce like a sponge. I paired it with the house's own 13-degree dark lager — malty, with a caramel finish and surprisingly little bitterness, nothing like the heavy stouts I'd expected.

Jana, the woman running my section of the hall, told me her family had run this place for five generations. 'We can taste a one-degree difference in serving temperature,' she laughed. 'Beer means more to us here than church does.' When I asked about church, she just shrugged: 'Church is for making wishes. The beer hall is for telling the truth.'

Past nine, I walked back to Old Town Square. The clock glowed gold against the night sky, the trumpeter leaned out once more to play his hourly fanfare, and this time the square was packed with phones held aloft. Standing at the back of the crowd, I thought about Hanuš and his blinded eyes, about the window two governors were thrown from, about 77,297 names on a synagogue wall, and about the candle in Pavel's hand. Prague never pretends it's only beautiful. It leaves every scar exactly where it happened, and lets you walk right up to look.
