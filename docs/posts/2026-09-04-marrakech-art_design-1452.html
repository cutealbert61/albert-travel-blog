中文

清晨五點四十分,穆安津的喚拜聲比我的鬧鐘更早響起,從麥地那四面八方的宣禮塔傳來,層層疊疊,像一場沒有指揮的合唱。我躺在里亞德(Riad)厚重的石牆房間裡,天花板上是手工雕刻的石膏浮雕,幾何星形圖案一圈圈往外擴散。我瞪著那片天花板想,這趟旅行我最想搞懂的,不是這座城市有多少座清真寺,而是為什麼摩洛哥人可以把「不能畫人」這件事,活成一整套令人屏息的美學系統。於是我決定不睡了,六點鐘就摸黑走進還沒醒來的麥地那。

破曉:班尤瑟夫學院的幾何密碼

六點的麥地那安靜得不真實。石板路上還留著前一夜灑水降溫的濕氣,貓咪縮在打烊的鐵捲門前,烤箱師傅正把成排的圓麵餅送進公共烤爐,麵粉香混著木炭味飄滿整條巷子。我沿著 Rue Assouel 一路往北走,大約二十分鐘,穿過還沒擺攤的香料市集空架子,終於在九點抵達班尤瑟夫伊斯蘭學院(Ben Youssef Madrasa)——這座十六世紀的古蘭經學院,是我心目中全馬拉喀什最誠實的建築,因為它把摩爾式美學的邏輯攤開給你看,毫無保留。

門票七十迪拉姆(約新台幣二百三十元),開門前已經有零星幾個攝影愛好者在排隊。一踏進中庭,水池的倒影把整片牆的雪花石膏雕花複製成兩份,牆面從下到上是三層語彙:底部是彩色瓷磚拼貼的「澤利吉」(zellige)幾何紋,中段是石膏雕刻的可蘭經文與藤蔓紋,頂部則是雪松木雕的幾何格柵。光線從中庭天井直直落下,九點半的陽光角度恰好切過水池,把整個中庭染成一種介於金色與灰藍之間的顏色,我站在那裡整整發呆了十分鐘,直到後面遊客的快門聲把我拉回現實。

他們不畫人,是因為真主的完美無法被複製;於是他們畫下無限——用數學,讓眼睛看見永恆。

離開學院時,我在巷口一間不到十坪的小工坊裡認識了哈山(Hassan),他是第三代澤利吉拼貼師傅,手上永遠沾著白色石灰粉。他蹲在地上,用一把小鑿子把彩色瓷磚敲成三角形、星形、菱形的小碎片,再一片片嵌進圖案模板裡,完全不用尺規畫線稿,「圖案在我腦子裡,」他用法語混著阿拉伯語跟我說,「我爺爺教我的時候,他也沒有畫紙。」我問他為什麼摩洛哥的伊斯蘭藝術幾乎看不到人像或動物,他停下手上的鑿子,認真地說了那句我後來抄在筆記本上、也放進這篇文章開頭的話——關於真主的完美無法被複製,於是他們用幾何學畫下無限。那一刻我才明白,這些滿牆滿地的星形拼貼,不是裝飾,是一種信仰的視覺翻譯。

上午:馬約爾花園與聖羅蘭美術館的鈷藍魔法

十一點,我搭上一輛橘色小計程車,議價後約三十迪拉姆,十五分鐘車程從舊城麥地那穿越到新城古利茲區(Guéliz)。馬約爾花園(Jardin Majorelle)和隔壁的伊夫·聖羅蘭美術館(Musée Yves Saint Laurent),是我在馬拉喀什遇到人潮最密集的地方——建議提前在官網訂票,現場排隊時間可能超過一小時。

走進花園大門的瞬間,那種被稱為「馬約爾藍」(Majorelle Blue)的鈷藍色建築牆面猛然撞進視線,搭配鮮黃色的花盆邊框與滿園仙人掌、竹林的深綠,色彩對比強烈到有點不真實,像有人把調色盤直接潑在建築上。這座花園原本是法國畫家賈克·馬約爾在一九二〇年代的私人花園,後來由聖羅蘭與他的伴侶皮耶·貝傑買下並修復,園裡至今保留超過三百種植物。我在竹林步道裡走了快四十分鐘,聽著噴泉水聲與鳥鳴,空氣裡有一種濕潤的植物氣味,跟外頭麥地那乾燥的塵土味完全是兩個世界。

隔壁的聖羅蘭美術館用清水模與陶土色磚牆構成,外觀線條乾淨俐落,刻意呼應柏柏爾編織紋理的節奏感——建築師 Studio KO 把摩洛哥傳統工藝的邏輯,翻譯成極簡的當代語彙。館內常設展陳列聖羅蘭從一九六二到二〇〇二年間受摩洛哥啟發設計的服裝手稿與成衣,其中一件融合柏柏爾頭飾意象的晚禮服讓我站著看了很久。走出美術館,我在紀念品店買了一本關於柏柏爾博物館的畫冊,四百五十迪拉姆,肉痛但值得。

午餐:在「Le Jardin」庭院裡的一碗塔吉

下午一點,肚子開始抗議,我沒有選擇遊客愛拍照的網美餐廳,而是走進麥地那深處一家叫「Le Jardin」的庭院式餐廳,離攝影博物館(Maison de la Photographie)只有兩分鐘腳程,招牌小到幾乎會錯過,得從一條堆滿地毯攤販的窄巷側門進去。

庭院裡種著芭蕉葉與檸檬樹,烏龜在磁磚地上慢慢爬,頭頂是一層薄薄的遮陽網,陽光透過網眼灑成一格一格的光斑,落在鋪著刺繡桌巾的木桌上。我點了一份杏桃燉羊肉塔吉(tagine d'agneau aux abricots),還有一壺薄荷茶,價格分別是九十迪拉姆與二十迪拉姆,合計約新台幣三百六十元。塔吉端上桌時還冒著煙,錐形陶蓋一掀開,肉桂與番紅花的香氣整個撲出來,羊肉燉得幾乎脫骨,叉子輕輕一撥就散開,杏桃乾吸飽了肉汁,甜中帶著一點燉煮出來的焦香,配著吸滿醬汁的庫斯米麵包一起吃,鹹甜平衡得剛剛好。薄荷茶倒的時候侍者刻意拉高茶壺、讓茶水從高處注入玻璃杯,發出清脆的水聲,這是摩洛哥待客的儀式感,茶要冒泡才算倒得漂亮。我坐在那個庭院裡,聽著隔壁桌的法國老夫妻低聲聊天,鳥叫聲從芭蕉葉間傳出來,那一頓飯吃了快九十分鐘,是整趟旅程裡最放鬆的一段時光。

午後實測路線:從染坊迷宮走到 Sidi Ghanem 設計街區

下午三點,我決定做一件比較冒險的事:徒步從麥地那的染坊區(Souk Sebbaghine)一路摸索到工業區裡新興的設計街區 Sidi Ghanem。這條路線網路上資訊很少,我是問了三個當地人、繞了兩次錯路才拼湊出來的,在這裡完整記錄給有興趣的人。

從傑馬夫納廣場出發,先沿著染料市集的巷子往東北走,大約十分鐘會看到頭頂晾滿一綹一綹彩色羊毛線的窄巷,這裡就是染坊區。染工們赤腳踩在染缸邊,靛藍色、番紅花黃、石榴紅的染料在石缸裡冒著泡,空氣中有一股濃烈的化學染劑混著羊毛油脂的味道,不算好聞,但視覺上非常震撼——垂掛的羊毛線在午後陽光下像一面面彩色瀑布。這段路我走得很慢,大約花了四十分鐘拍照與跟染工聊天,給了一個主動幫我指路的染工二十迪拉姆小費,他倒也沒有強迫推銷,算是友善的互動。

接著我原本打算步行到 Sidi Ghanem,但問了路邊雜貨店老闆後才知道距離超過四公里,加上完全沒有遮蔭,他直接勸我攔計程車。從染坊區到 Sidi Ghanem,議價後車資約五十迪拉姆,車程十五分鐘。這個工業區原本是倉庫與工廠聚落,近十年被一群摩洛哥與歐洲設計師進駐改造,像是以手工陶器聞名的 LRNCE、走現代柏柏爾風格的家飾品牌 Norya Ait Wahmane,還有做手工皮鞋與包款的 Max & Jan。我在 LRNCE 的工作室待了將近一小時,牆上掛滿手繪陶盤與陶偶,老闆娘用不完美卻極具生命力的線條畫出眼睛、月亮與波浪紋,跟老城裡那種嚴謹對稱的伊斯蘭幾何形成有趣的對話——一邊是延續六百年的秩序美學,一邊是解構傳統的當代實驗。整個下午,連交通、拍照、購物,我花了將近四小時,買了一個陶製小碗,一百八十迪拉姆,現在就擺在我台北家裡的書架上。

老城的瓷磚教我什麼是秩序,新區的陶器教我什麼是自由——馬拉喀什的設計語言,一直在這兩者之間呼吸。

黃昏之後:傑馬夫納廣場的說書人與齋月燈火

傍晚六點半,天光開始轉橘,我從 Sidi Ghanem 搭計程車回到傑馬夫納廣場(Jemaa el-Fnaa),廣場正在經歷它每天最戲劇性的變身——白天賣果汁與紀念品的攤販開始收攤,取而代之的是一排排冒著白煙的美食攤車,按照傳統編號排列,老饕都知道要找幾號攤位。

我在14號攤坐下,點了一碗蝸牛湯(babbouche),十迪拉姆一碗,湯頭用茴香、丁香與一長串香料熬煮,喝起來帶點藥膳般的甘苦,老闆遞給我一根牙籤挑蝸牛肉,肉質有點韌卻很入味,是我在摩洛哥吃過最特別的一道小吃。隔壁攤買了一杯現榨柳橙汁,四迪拉姆,酸甜濃郁到懷疑裡面加了糖,老闆笑著跟我說完全沒有,是柳橙本身就這麼甜。

我到訪的那幾天正值齋戒月尾聲,廣場的氣氛比平常更濃烈。日落前十分鐘,原本喧鬧的說書人與Gnawa樂手都安靜下來,所有人的眼睛盯著遠方宣禮塔,等待喚拜聲響起的那一刻——那是可以開齋進食的訊號。我身旁一位帶著全家老小坐在塑膠椅上的大叔看我一臉困惑,主動遞給我一顆椰棗,「日落先吃這個,」他說,「先知是這樣教的。」喚拜聲一響,整個廣場瞬間爆出笑聲與碗筷碰撞聲,鼓聲與嗩吶聲又重新響起,穿著藍色長袍的Gnawa樂手甩著頭上綴滿貝殼的流蘇帽子,鐵製響板敲出重複又催眠的節奏,圍觀的孩子跟著手舞足蹈。說書人在人群中央用阿拉伯語講著我聽不懂卻能感受到高低起伏的古老故事,聽眾席地而坐,笑聲此起彼落。

那天晚上我在廣場邊緣一家屋頂餐廳的露台上坐到快十一點,底下的廣場燈火與人聲隨著夜色加深,一波波律動著,像整座城市的心跳。我終於明白,馬拉喀什的美學從來不只活在博物館與瓷磚牆上,它也活在這碗十迪拉姆的蝸牛湯裡、活在陌生人遞來的一顆椰棗裡、活在鼓聲與人群共同呼吸的節奏裡。這座紅色城市教會我的最後一課是:真正的設計美學,是一種被所有人共同活出來的生活方式,而不是被裝裱起來、只能遠觀的作品。

At five-forty in the morning, the call to prayer woke me before my alarm did, rolling in from every minaret in the medina in layers, an unrehearsed choir with no conductor. I lay in my riad room under a hand-carved plaster ceiling, its star-shaped geometry radiating outward in perfect rings, and thought about what I actually wanted from this trip. Not a tally of mosques visited, but an answer to a question that had nagged me for years: how does a culture that forbids figurative imagery build an entire visual language this hypnotic? I gave up on sleep and walked out into the still-dark medina at six.

Dawn: The Geometric Code of the Ben Youssef Madrasa

The medina at six in the morning feels almost unreal in its silence. The stone alleys were still damp from the previous night's water-cooling ritual, cats curled in front of shuttered metal doors, and a baker was sliding rounds of dough into a communal oven, the smell of flour and charcoal smoke filling the narrow street. I walked north along Rue Assouel for about twenty minutes, past the still-empty spice market stalls, and arrived at the Ben Youssef Madrasa at nine — a sixteenth-century Quranic school that I consider the most honest building in all of Marrakech, because it lays its entire architectural logic bare for anyone willing to look.

Admission was seventy dirhams, and a handful of photographers were already waiting before opening. The moment I stepped into the courtyard, the reflecting pool doubled the carved plasterwork on the far wall. The walls speak in three registers: at the base, zellige tilework in tight geometric mosaics; above that, carved plaster inscribed with Quranic calligraphy and vine motifs; and at the top, cedar wood lattice screens cut into interlocking stars. Light fell straight down through the courtyard's open roof, and at nine-thirty the angle happened to slice across the pool, staining the whole space a colour somewhere between gold and slate blue. I stood there for a full ten minutes doing nothing but staring, until the click of someone else's camera shutter snapped me back.

They do not paint faces, because the perfection of God cannot be replicated; so instead they paint infinity — through mathematics, they make eternity visible.

On my way out, I ducked into a workshop barely the size of a parking space and met Hassan, a third-generation zellige craftsman with lime dust permanently ground into his knuckles. He crouched on the floor, chipping glazed tiles into triangles, stars, and diamonds with a small chisel, working entirely without a drawn template. 'The pattern lives in my head,' he told me in a mix of French and Arabic. 'My grandfather never used paper either.' When I asked why Islamic art in Morocco so rarely depicts people or animals, he paused mid-strike and gave me the answer I later wrote at the top of my notebook, the one that opens this very article — that the perfection of God cannot be copied, so artisans paint infinity instead, through geometry. That was the moment I understood these star mosaics weren't decoration. They were a visual translation of faith.

Late Morning: The Cobalt Magic of Jardin Majorelle and the YSL Museum

At eleven, I flagged down a small orange petit taxi and, after some friendly haggling, paid thirty dirhams for the fifteen-minute ride from the medina to the new town district of Guéliz. Jardin Majorelle and the neighbouring Musée Yves Saint Laurent turned out to be the most crowded spots of my entire trip — book online in advance, because same-day queues can run well over an hour.

The moment I passed through the gate, that saturated cobalt known as Majorelle Blue hit me full force, framed by mustard-yellow planters and dense green cactus gardens, a colour combination so intense it barely looked real. The garden began in the 1920s as the private retreat of French painter Jacques Majorelle, later restored by Yves Saint Laurent and his partner Pierre Bergé, and today holds over three hundred plant species. I wandered the bamboo path for nearly forty minutes, listening to running water and birdsong, the humid green scent a complete inversion of the dry dust smell of the medina outside.

Next door, the YSL museum's exterior — raw concrete paired with terracotta-toned brick — is deliberately clean-lined, its rhythm quietly echoing Berber weaving patterns. Architects Studio KO translated the logic of traditional Moroccan craft into a minimalist, contemporary vocabulary. Inside, the permanent collection traces Saint Laurent's designs from 1962 to 2002 inspired by Morocco, including an evening gown referencing Berber headdress silhouettes that I stood in front of for far longer than I meant to. I left with an art book on Berber material culture from the gift shop, four hundred fifty dirhams, an indulgence I don't regret.

Lunch: A Bowl of Tagine in the Courtyard of Le Jardin

By one in the afternoon my stomach was staging a protest, so instead of chasing an Instagram-famous rooftop, I ducked into a courtyard restaurant called Le Jardin, tucked deep in the medina two minutes from the Maison de la Photographie. Its sign is so modest I nearly walked past it, and the entrance is a side door hidden behind a carpet vendor's stall.

The courtyard was planted with banana leaves and lemon trees, a tortoise ambling slowly across the tiled floor, and overhead a thin shade net scattered the sunlight into a grid of small bright squares across the embroidered tablecloths. I ordered a lamb tagine with apricots and a pot of mint tea — ninety and twenty dirhams respectively, about three hundred sixty New Taiwan dollars combined. The tagine arrived still steaming, and when the conical lid was lifted, cinnamon and saffron rushed out in a wave. The lamb was so tender a fork alone was enough to shred it, and the dried apricots had soaked up the pan juices, sweet with a faint caramelised edge, perfectly balanced against warm, sauce-soaked khobz bread. When the waiter poured the mint tea, he lifted the pot high above the glass on purpose, letting it fall in a thin stream that hissed and foamed — pouring tea with a proper froth is a small ritual of hospitality here. I sat in that courtyard for nearly ninety minutes, half-listening to an elderly French couple murmuring at the next table, birds calling from the banana leaves overhead. It was the most unhurried meal of the entire trip.

An Afternoon on Foot: From the Dyers' Souk to the Sidi Ghanem Design District

At three in the afternoon I attempted something a little riskier: walking from the medina's dyers' souk, Souk Sebbaghine, all the way to Sidi Ghanem, an industrial zone turned design district. There is almost no information about this route online — I pieced it together by asking three different locals and doubling back twice, so here it is, recorded in full for anyone curious enough to try.

Starting from Jemaa el-Fnaa, I headed northeast through the dye market's alleys, and within about ten minutes found myself under skeins of wool dyed in every colour, strung up overhead — this is the dyers' quarter. Barefoot dyers stood at the edge of stone vats, indigo, saffron yellow, and pomegranate red bubbling below. The air carried a sharp mix of chemical dye and raw wool grease, not exactly pleasant, but visually staggering — the hanging wool skeins caught the afternoon light like cascades of colour. I moved slowly through this stretch, spending about forty minutes photographing and chatting with the dyers, and tipped one who pointed me in the right direction twenty dirhams; he didn't push for more, just a friendly exchange.

I had planned to walk the rest of the way, but a shopkeeper along the route informed me Sidi Ghanem was over four kilometres off with zero shade, and firmly suggested I take a taxi instead. The fare, after negotiation, was fifty dirhams for a fifteen-minute ride. This industrial zone, once a cluster of warehouses and factories, has over the past decade been colonised by Moroccan and European designers — LRNCE, known for its hand-painted ceramics, Norya Ait Wahmane's modern Berber-inflected homeware, and Max & Jan's handmade leather shoes and bags. I spent nearly an hour in the LRNCE studio, walls covered in hand-painted plates and clay figures scrawled with imperfect, lively lines — eyes, moons, waves — in fascinating dialogue with the rigid symmetry of the old city's geometric tilework: one an order refined over six centuries, the other a contemporary experiment in deconstructing tradition. Between transport, browsing, and photos, the whole afternoon took nearly four hours. I bought a small ceramic bowl for one hundred eighty dirhams; it now sits on a bookshelf in my apartment in Taipei.

The old city's tiles taught me what order looks like. The new district's ceramics taught me what freedom looks like. Marrakech's design language has always breathed between the two.

After Dusk: Storytellers and Ramadan Lanterns in Jemaa el-Fnaa

At half past six, the light turned orange, and I took a taxi back from Sidi Ghanem to Jemaa el-Fnaa just as the square underwent its daily transformation — the daytime vendors of juice and trinkets packing up, replaced by rows of steaming food carts arranged by traditional numbered stalls, each with its own regulars who know exactly which number to find.

I sat down at stall number 14 and ordered babbouche, a snail soup, for ten dirhams a bowl. The broth was simmered with fennel, cloves, and a long list of spices, tasting faintly medicinal and bitter-sweet, and the vendor handed me a toothpick to pick the snails from their shells — chewy, deeply seasoned, unlike anything else I ate in Morocco. Next door I bought a fresh orange juice for four dirhams, so sweet and concentrated I suspected added sugar, but the vendor laughed and insisted it was simply how sweet the oranges here naturally are.

My visit happened to fall near the end of Ramadan, and the square's atmosphere that evening was even more charged than usual. Ten minutes before sunset, the storytellers and Gnawa musicians who normally fill the square with noise all fell silent, every eye turned toward the distant minaret, waiting for the call to prayer that would signal it was time to break the fast. A man sitting nearby with his whole family on plastic stools noticed my confusion and handed me a date. 'Eat this first, at sunset,' he said. 'That's how the Prophet taught us.' The moment the call rang out, the entire square erupted — laughter, the clatter of bowls and spoons, drums and oboes starting up again all at once. Gnawa musicians in indigo robes shook shell-fringed caps, iron castanets clanging out a hypnotic, repetitive rhythm, children dancing along at the edges. A storyteller held court at the centre of a crowd, speaking Arabic I couldn't understand but whose rhythm and cadence I could feel, listeners seated on the ground bursting into laughter at intervals.

I ended up on a rooftop terrace overlooking the square until nearly eleven that night, watching the lights and noise below pulse in waves as the darkness deepened, like the heartbeat of the entire city. That's when it finally clicked: the aesthetics of Marrakech never lived only in museums and tiled walls. It lives in a ten-dirham bowl of snail soup, in a date handed over by a stranger, in the shared rhythm of drums and a crowd breathing together. The final lesson this red city taught me is that real design is a way of living that everyone participates in — not a framed artefact meant only to be admired from a distance.
