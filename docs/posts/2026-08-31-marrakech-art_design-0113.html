中文

清晨五點四十分,喚拜聲把我從麥地那一間老宅改建的里雅得(riad)客房裡叫醒。聲音先從遠處的庫圖比亞清真寺傳來,像一條線,然後周邊七、八座小清真寺陸續加入,整座舊城的空氣都被這聲音染成了金黃色。我躺在鋪著柏柏爾織毯的床上,盯著天花板上手工雕刻的雪松木藻井,忽然意識到一件事——這趟旅程我根本不需要特地去找「藝術」,因為整座馬拉喀什,從牆面到地磚到光線,本身就是一件持續被使用、被磨損、被生活著的作品。

破曉:巴伊亞宮與巴迪王宮的光影幾何

七點半,天色剛剛轉亮,我沿著還沒甦醒的窄巷走向巴伊亞宮(Bahia Palace)。這條路我走了將近二十分鐘,穿過賣香料的小攤(還沒開門,布簾都還蓋著)、幾隻縮在門口打盹的貓,以及一個正在用水管沖洗石板路的老人。巴伊亞宮九點才開放,我是第三個排隊的人,票價七十迪拉姆(約新台幣230元),現金付款,不能刷卡。

一進門,陽光正好斜斜切過中庭的柑橘樹,落在滿地的彩色瓷磚上。這種被稱為「zellige」的鑲嵌工藝,是把燒好的方形瓷磚手工敲成各種幾何小片,再一片片拼回星形、八角形、菱形的圖案裡,誤差要控制在一兩毫米之內。我蹲下來用手指摸過那些接縫,冰涼、粗糙,帶著一種手工才有的不規則感。導覽員告訴我,伊斯蘭藝術傳統上避免描繪人像或動物,於是工匠把所有的信仰與情感都灌注進幾何圖形裡——一個八角星可能代表宇宙秩序,重複到無限的圖案則暗示真主的無邊無際。

他們把不能畫的臉,都畫成了星星。

從巴伊亞宮走十分鐘就到了巴迪王宮(Badii Palace),票價七十迪拉姆,和巴伊亞宮完全是兩種調性。十六世紀建成、後來被拆得只剩夯土牆骨架的宮殿,如今變成了鸛鳥的家。我抬頭就看到十幾隻白鸛站在斷牆頂端整理羽毛,喙碰喙發出喀喀聲,陽光把牆面的裂縫照得像地圖一樣清晰。同一種美學語彙,在巴伊亞宮是精緻繁複的裝飾語法,在巴迪王宮卻只剩結構本身——這種對比讓我第一次真正理解,摩洛哥的建築美學從來不是表面裝飾,而是關於空間、光線與時間如何互相對話。

上午:馬約雷勒花園與聖羅蘭的柏柏爾博物館

搭小黃計程車從麥地那到蓋利茲新城的馬約雷勒花園(Jardin Majorelle),車程約十五分鐘,議價後四十迪拉姆。九點半抵達時門口已經排了不算短的隊伍,花園門票一百五十迪拉姆,若要加入柏柏爾博物館需另購門票一百迪拉姆,建議提前在官網預約時段票,現場排隊隨隨便便就要等上四十分鐘。

走進去的瞬間,那種被稱為「馬約雷勒藍」的鈷藍色牆面,配上盛開的九重葛和高聳仙人掌林,強烈到有點不真實。這座花園是法國畫家雅克・馬約雷勒(Jacques Majorelle)自一九二三年起花了近四十年打造的私人植物園,1980年被伊夫・聖羅蘭與他的伴侶皮耶・貝爾傑買下並修復。園裡種了將近三百種來自五大洲的植物,竹林間隱約傳來加納瓦(Gnawa)音樂的鼓點,是花園自己播放的背景樂,節奏低沉、帶點催眠感。

隔壁的伊夫聖羅蘭博物館二〇一七年才開幕,由法國建築事務所Studio KO操刀,建築外牆用了赤陶色的編織紋理磚,呼應柏柏爾織品的經緯結構。館內常設展廳裡,近五十套高級訂製服禮服在低照度的展櫃中排開,不少剪裁明顯借用了柏柏爾長袍的直筒輪廓與頭巾的層疊技法。站在那裡我心裡其實五味雜陳——一方面驚嘆設計轉譯的精妙,一方面也忍不住想,這些啟發聖羅蘭的柏柏爾婦女,她們的名字從來沒有被印在任何一件禮服的標籤上。

午後:顛簸一趟MACAAL非洲當代美術館

下午一點,我決定去一趟大部分遊客不會特地去的地方——MACAAL(Museum of African Contemporary Art Al Maaden),位在城外阿邁登高爾夫度假區旁邊。這個館需要提前在官網預約入場時段,現場不接受無預約訪客,門票一百迪拉姆。我在德吉瑪廣場邊招了一輛小計程車,司機的計程表「剛好壞了」,我們議價議到六十迪拉姆單程,車程大概二十五分鐘。

路上塞了快十分鐘,經過一段還在施工的紅土道路,兩旁是半成品的別墅社區,和麥地那的擁擠完全是兩個世界。下車時周圍幾乎沒有任何指標,我甚至以為司機把我載錯地方,直到看到一片橄欖樹林裡立著幾件生鏽鋼板雕塑,才確定自己到了。

館內最讓我駐足最久的,是哈山・哈賈吉(Hassan Hajjaj)的攝影作品——他用可口可樂罐、茶壺、傳統頭巾拼貼出色彩飽和到近乎刺眼的肖像,把街頭日常物件變成普普藝術的邊框,人物眼神卻自信直視鏡頭。館外的雕塑花園沿著橄欖樹林散步大約要四十分鐘,正午的陽光毫無遮蔽,我後悔沒多帶一瓶水。逛完整整兩個小時,出來卻叫不到車——附近完全沒有排班計程車,我靠館方櫃檯人員幫忙用WhatsApp聯絡了一位叫拉希德(Rachid)的司機,等了將近二十分鐘他才到。這段插曲讓我學到一課:去MACAAL務必先問清楚回程方式,或直接請司機在外面等,加碼給他二十迪拉姆等待費就好。

藝術館建在高爾夫球場旁邊的橄欖林裡,像是有人故意把兩種摩洛哥的未來擺在一起讓人選。

傍晚前:蘇克深處的地毯課

下午四點半,我鑽進蘇克塞馬林(Souk Semmarine)迷宮般的窄巷,原本只想找路去染坊區,卻在一條沒有名字的岔路裡迷了路。空氣裡混著雪松木屑、皮革染料和薄荷的氣味,頭頂上遮陽的蘆葦棚在陽光下篩出一格一格的光斑,落在腳邊堆疊的地毯上,像是天然的補光燈。

一位地毯商人西・哈桑(Si Hassan)看我拿著相機東張西望,笑著招手要我進他店裡「只是喝杯茶,不買也沒關係」。他端出三個小玻璃杯,倒了滾燙的薄荷茶,重複斟了三次——他說這是圖阿雷格人的古老說法:第一杯苦如生命,第二杯濃如愛情,第三杯甜如死亡。他一邊說一邊把地毯一張張攤開在地上,教我認柏柏爾織品裡的符號:菱形代表生育與豐饒,鋸齒紋是山脈或水流,一個簡化的眼睛圖案則是用來擋走邪視。他說阿特拉斯山區的婦女織地毯從不看圖紙,圖案全靠記憶,母親傳給女兒,一張地毯可能織上好幾個月。

我們聊起星期五,他說那是全城最安靜的中午——男人們會提早收攤去清真寺做主麻聚禮,回來後全家一起吃庫斯庫斯,這幾乎是不成文的規矩,連蘇克裡最愛講價的商人到了星期五中午都會忽然變得慢條斯理。他也提醒我,拍攝當地婦女之前一定要先開口徵求同意,這在麥地那深處尤其被看重。最後我看中一塊小抱枕套,他開價四百五十迪拉姆,我們你來我往講了快十分鐘,最終以兩百五十迪拉姆成交——他笑著說殺價本身就是一種社交禮儀,不殺價他反而會覺得少了點什麼。

入夜:德吉瑪廣場的煙、光與滋味

傍晚六點半,我走進德吉瑪廣場(Jemaa el-Fnaa),白天賣果汁與弄蛇人的攤位正在收攤,取而代之的是一整排編了號的美食攤,店家陸續點起瓦斯燈,炊煙被夕陽染成橘紅色,整個廣場像是被點燃了一樣。鼓聲從某個角落傳來,一圈人正圍著看阿卡巴的傳統歌舞。

我在31號攤坐下,點了一份坦吉亞(tanjia)——這是馬拉喀什特有的燉肉,把羊肉、油、大蒜、鹽漬檸檬和一堆香料塞進陶罐,埋進公共哈曼(蒸氣浴)爐灶的餘燼裡悶煮六到八小時。上桌時肉已經軟到用麵包一撥就脫骨,油脂混著孜然鹽沾料,鹹香濃郁,一份加麵包大約六十迪拉姆。隔壁攤又點了一碗哈里拉湯(harira),番茄、扁豆、鷹嘴豆熬得濃稠,配著一顆椰棗,十迪拉姆一碗,是廣場上最溫暖的十迪拉姆。

飯後我走到法蘭西咖啡館(Café de France)旁的鮮榨柳橙汁攤,一杯只要五迪拉姆,甜得像加了糖,老闆說完全沒加糖,就是橙子本身的甜度。最後我爬上一間屋頂茶座,點了一壺薄荷茶,坐在露台邊緣俯瞰整個廣場——庫圖比亞清真寺的宣禮塔被打上暖黃色的燈光,底下人聲、鼓聲、烤肉煙霧全部揉在一起,像一齣還沒謝幕的舞台劇。

隔天上午我特地繞去蓋利茲新城的康普托礦業藝廊(Comptoir des Mines Galerie),一棟一九三〇年代的礦業公司舊樓被改造成當代藝術空間,牆上還留著原本的鑄鐵樓梯與拼花地磚,和樓內展出的摩洛哥當代畫作形成一種奇異的時間疊層。走出藝廊,巷子裡的壁畫一路延伸到自由大道(Rue de la Liberté),色彩鮮豔的塗鴉和百年前的裝飾磚並存在同一面牆上。我站在那裡想,馬拉喀什從來不需要把藝術關進玻璃展櫃裡,它就攤在陽光下,攤在每一片瓷磚、每一杯薄荷茶、每一張手織地毯裡,任由每個路過的人伸手觸摸。

ENGLISH

At 5:40 in the morning, the call to prayer pulled me out of sleep in a converted riad room in the medina. It started from the Koutoubia Mosque in the distance, a single thread of sound, then seven or eight neighborhood mosques joined in until the entire old city seemed dipped in gold. Lying under a hand-loomed Berber blanket, staring up at a carved cedar ceiling, I realized I didn't need to go hunting for 'art' on this trip. In Marrakech, from the walls to the floor tiles to the quality of the light itself, the whole city is a living, working piece of design that never stops being used.

Dawn: The Geometry of Bahia Palace and the Ruins of Badii

By 7:30, I was walking through still-sleeping alleys toward Bahia Palace, a twenty-minute walk past shuttered spice stalls, dozing cats, and an old man hosing down the cobblestones. The palace opens at nine; I was third in line. Entry cost seventy dirhams, cash only.

Inside, morning light cut sideways across the citrus trees in the courtyard, landing on floors covered in zellige — hand-cut geometric tiles assembled piece by piece into eight-pointed stars and diamonds, with tolerances of a millimeter or two. I crouched down and ran my fingers along the seams: cool, slightly rough, unmistakably handmade. Islamic artistic tradition generally avoids depicting living beings, so craftsmen poured their devotion into geometry instead — an eight-pointed star suggesting cosmic order, patterns repeating toward infinity as a nod to the boundlessness of the divine.

They painted the faces they weren't allowed to paint as stars instead.

Ten minutes further on stood Badii Palace, also seventy dirhams, a completely different register of the same visual language. Built in the sixteenth century and later stripped down to its rammed-earth bones, it's now home to a colony of storks. A dozen of them stood atop the broken walls, preening, clacking their beaks, while sunlight traced every crack in the walls like a map. Where Bahia is ornament in full bloom, Badii is structure stripped bare — and standing between the two, I understood for the first time that Moroccan architecture was never about surface decoration alone, but about how space, light, and time keep talking to each other across centuries.

Late Morning: Majorelle Garden and Saint Laurent's Berber Museum

A petit taxi from the medina to Gueliz took about fifteen minutes and, after some negotiation, forty dirhams. By 9:30 the line at Jardin Majorelle already snaked around the corner. Entry is 150 dirhams, with the adjoining Berber Museum an extra hundred; booking a timed ticket online in advance saves a forty-minute wait at the gate.

Stepping inside, the cobalt shade now known as 'Majorelle Blue' hit me almost physically, set off against blooming bougainvillea and towering cacti. French painter Jacques Majorelle spent nearly forty years building this garden starting in 1923; Yves Saint Laurent and his partner Pierre Bergé bought and restored it in 1980. Nearly three hundred plant species from five continents grow here, and somewhere in the bamboo groves, Gnawa music plays softly on a loop — low, hypnotic, percussive.

Next door, the Yves Saint Laurent Museum, opened in 2017 and designed by the French firm Studio KO, wears an exterior of terracotta bricks woven in a pattern that echoes Berber textile structure. Inside, nearly fifty haute couture gowns stand in dim, climate-controlled cases, many clearly borrowing the straight silhouettes of Berber robes and the layered draping of traditional headscarves. Standing there, I felt oddly conflicted — admiring the sophistication of the translation while also thinking about the Berber women whose craft inspired it all, none of whose names ever made it onto a single label.

Afternoon: A Bumpy Ride to MACAAL

At one o'clock I set off for a place most tourists skip entirely: MACAAL, the Museum of African Contemporary Art Al Maaden, out by the Al Maaden golf resort on the city's edge. Entry requires an advance online booking for a timed slot — walk-ins aren't accepted — and costs a hundred dirhams. I flagged a petit taxi near Jemaa el-Fnaa; the meter was 'conveniently broken,' so we settled on sixty dirhams for the roughly twenty-five-minute ride.

We crawled through ten minutes of traffic, then hit a half-finished red-dirt road lined with villa developments still under construction — a different Morocco entirely from the crowded medina. When we arrived, there wasn't a single sign in sight; I genuinely thought the driver had taken me to the wrong place, until I spotted rusted steel sculptures standing among an olive grove.

Inside, I lingered longest over photographs by Hassan Hajjaj, who frames his subjects with Coca-Cola cans, teapots, and traditional headscarves, turning everyday street objects into pop-art borders around portraits whose subjects stare back with total confidence. The sculpture garden winds through the olive grove for about forty minutes on foot, with almost no shade under the midday sun — I regretted not bringing more water. Two hours later, I emerged to find no taxis waiting anywhere nearby; the front desk had to WhatsApp a driver named Rachid, who took another twenty minutes to arrive. Lesson learned: figure out your return trip before you go, or just pay a driver an extra twenty dirhams to wait.

They built the contemporary art museum in an olive grove beside a golf course, as if daring visitors to choose between two versions of Morocco's future.

Late Afternoon: A Lesson in Rugs, Deep in the Souk

By 4:30 I'd wandered into the maze of Souk Semmarine, meaning only to find my way to the dye district, and promptly got lost down an unnamed alley. The air carried cedar sawdust, leather dye, and mint all at once, while woven reed canopies overhead filtered the light into little checkered patches that fell across stacks of carpets like natural spotlights.

A rug merchant named Si Hassan spotted me looking lost with my camera and waved me into his shop — 'just tea, no obligation to buy.' He poured mint tea into three small glasses, filling each one three times, reciting an old Tuareg saying as he did: the first glass is bitter like life, the second strong like love, the third sweet like death. As he unrolled carpet after carpet across the floor, he taught me to read the symbols woven into Berber textiles — a diamond for fertility, a zigzag for mountains or flowing water, a simplified eye to ward off the evil gaze. Women in the Atlas Mountains, he said, weave entirely from memory, no patterns on paper, passing designs from mother to daughter across a single carpet that might take months to finish.

We talked about Fridays — the quietest midday hour in the whole city, when men close up shop early for communal prayers and families gather afterward for couscous, an unwritten rule so strong that even the souk's most relentless hagglers slow down by Friday noon. He also reminded me to always ask permission before photographing local women, a courtesy taken especially seriously deep in the medina. In the end I bought a small cushion cover; he opened at 450 dirhams, and after ten minutes of good-natured back and forth we landed on 250. Haggling, he said with a grin, is its own form of hospitality — he'd be a little offended if I hadn't tried.

After Dark: Smoke, Light, and Flavor in Jemaa el-Fnaa

By 6:30 in the evening, Jemaa el-Fnaa was transforming. The daytime juice stalls and snake charmers were packing up, replaced by a long row of numbered food stalls lighting their gas lamps one by one, smoke rising gold against the sunset until the whole square seemed to catch fire. Drums rose from somewhere in the crowd, a circle forming around a traditional Aïta dance troupe.

I sat down at Stall 31 and ordered tanjia, Marrakech's own slow-cooked specialty — lamb, oil, garlic, preserved lemon, and a mix of spices packed into a clay urn and buried in the embers of a public hammam furnace for six to eight hours. It arrived so tender that a torn piece of bread was enough to pull the meat off the bone, the fat mingling with a cumin-salt dip on the side; the whole plate with bread cost about sixty dirhams. Next door I had a bowl of harira — tomato, lentils, and chickpeas simmered into something thick and warm, served with a single date, ten dirhams for what might be the best ten dirhams in the whole square.

Afterward I walked over to the fresh orange juice carts near Café de France — five dirhams a glass, sweet enough that I assumed sugar had been added, though the vendor swore it was all the oranges. I finished the night on a rooftop terrace with a pot of mint tea, looking down over the square: the Koutoubia minaret lit gold in the distance, drums and grilling smoke and a thousand overlapping voices rising together like a play that refused to end.

The next morning, before heading to the airport, I made one last stop in Gueliz at Comptoir des Mines Galerie — a 1930s mining company building converted into a contemporary art space, its original cast-iron staircases and patterned floor tiles still intact beneath the modern Moroccan paintings on display. Walking out, I found street murals spilling down the alley toward Rue de la Liberté, bright contemporary graffiti sharing the same walls as century-old decorative brickwork. Standing there, I understood that Marrakech never needed to lock its art behind glass. It's laid out in the open, in every tile, every glass of mint tea, every hand-knotted carpet, waiting for anyone who passes by to reach out and touch it.
