中文

清晨六點半,我踩著微涼的石板路走出老城區(Medina)一間傳統民宿(Riad),身後厚重的木門「喀」一聲闔上,巷子裡只有一隻瘦骨嶙峋的貓跟著我的腳步聲往前走。空氣裡有隱約的橙花香,混著隔夜炭火與皮革的氣味,這是麥地那特有的晨間配方。天還沒亮透,土紅色的城牆在灰藍色天光下泛著陶土般的暖意——當地人叫這種顏色「馬拉喀什紅」,是黏土混合石灰與稻草燒製出的赭紅色,幾百年來成了整座城市不成文的建築法規:任何新建築外牆,都必須維持介於磚紅與陶土之間的色調。我還沒吃早餐,腦子卻已經被這座城市的顏色學問占滿了。

這趟旅行我給自己一個任務:不從歷史或宗教切入馬拉喀什,而是從顏色、線條與工藝去讀懂它。結果證明,這座城市根本就是為了這個角度而存在的。

鈷藍色的早晨:馬約爾花園與伊夫聖羅蘭美術館

八點整,我在新城區(Gueliz)的馬約爾花園(Jardin Majorelle)門口排隊。從老城走過來大約二十五分鐘,沿著穆罕默德五世大道(Avenue Mohammed V)一路是法式梧桐與摩洛哥式拱門交錯的街景,也可以搭小黃計程車(petit taxi),車資約十五到二十迪拉姆(MAD),但司機常不跳表,上車前先講好價錢比較保險。我特意選開門時間到,就是為了避開九點半後湧入的旅遊團——這招果然奏效,前二十分鐘園裡幾乎只有我和幾隻遊蕩的貓。

花園本身不大,大概四十分鐘就能走完一圈,但我待了快兩個半小時,因為那個鈷藍色實在太攝人。畫家賈克・馬約爾(Jacques Majorelle)在一九三〇年代調出的這種藍,後來被稱為「馬約爾藍」,飽和到近乎螢光,卻和竹林的墨綠、陶盆的橙黃搭配得毫不違和。園區深處是伊夫聖羅蘭美術館(Musée YSL),由法國建築事務所Studio KO操刀,外牆用磚紅色陶磚拼出類似編織紋理的鏤空圖案,白天陽光穿過磚縫,在室內地面投出一整排會隨時間移動的光柵。館內常設展用極簡的黑色展櫃陳列聖羅蘭的高訂禮服草圖,並排展示他當年在摩洛哥採集的紡織紋樣——你會突然明白,原來那些西方時裝史課本裡的「柏柏爾風」剪裁,根源就在這座花園旁邊的工坊。門票兩館合售,約一百五十迪拉姆,現金與卡皆可,建議提前在官網訂票,現場買常常要多等半小時。

馬拉喀什教會我,顏色不只是裝飾,而是一種語言——赭紅色是身分,鈷藍色是叛逆,靛藍色是護身符。

幾何迷宮:班尤瑟夫伊斯蘭學院與老城的建築語言

中午時分,我從新城走回麥地那,鑽進香料市集(Souk Attarine)旁的巷子,銅匠街(Souk Haddadine)傳來叮叮噹噹的敲擊聲,一路循著這個節奏找到班尤瑟夫伊斯蘭學院(Medersa Ben Youssef)。這是十四世紀馬林王朝(Marinid)興建的古蘭經學院,門票約五十迪拉姆,參觀時間大概需要一小時。走進中庭那一刻,我下意識停下腳步——四面牆從地面到天花板,是三種工藝疊加而成的視覺震撼:底部是幾何馬賽克磁磚(zellige),中段是雕滿古蘭經文的灰泥浮雕(stucco),頂部則是雪松木雕的鐘乳石狀裝飾(muqarnas)。中庭水池把整片牆面倒映在水面上,對稱到讓人分不清哪邊是真實、哪邊是倒影。

伊斯蘭藝術傳統上避免描繪人像,於是工匠把所有創造力都灌注進幾何與紋樣裡:八角星無限延伸、藤蔓紋樣循環不斷,象徵的是真主的無限性。我站在天井正中央抬頭看,陽光是唯一會「動」的元素,隨著時間推移在雕花木格窗(mashrabiya)後面投下不斷變化的光影,原本靜止的建築因此有了呼吸感。比起隔壁更華麗的巴伊亞宮(Bahia Palace),班尤瑟夫學院的線條更收斂,卻也因此更純粹——這裡沒有任何一吋牆面是「留白」的,滿與空之間的節奏,本身就是一種音樂性的設計語言。

染坊巷與地毯商人:顏色裡的風俗哲學

下午兩點,我拐進染坊巷(Souk des Teinturiers),還沒看到店面就先聞到刺鼻的靛藍與石榴皮氣味。一位叫優素福(Youssef)的染匠正把一大捆羊毛線浸進石灰岩砌成的染缸裡,手臂到手肘整片靛青色,他抬頭看我盯著染缸出神,笑著用法語混英語跟我解釋:黃色來自番紅花與石榴皮,象徵太陽與豐饒;靛藍來自木藍植物,柏柏爾人相信這個顏色能驅避邪眼(nazar);而黑色,是核桃殼加鐵鏽水發酵出來的,只用在喪禮相關的織品上。他邀我進他家族開了三代的地毯店喝薄荷茶,茶壺舉得老高倒進玻璃杯,茶沫堆得像啤酒泡沫。

我們聊了將近一小時,他從沒直接開口要我買地毯,而是一張張攤開,講每一種紋樣背後的部落身分——菱形代表女性生育力,鋸齒線代表阿特拉斯山的稜線,顏色深淺甚至能看出出自哪個柏柏爾部落的女性織工之手。等他終於報價,我按照當地默契從對折開始還價,他也不惱,笑著說「這才是應該的對話方式」,前後拉鋸了二十分鐘,最後我用比開價低六成的價格,買下一張手織小地毯。優素福說得直白:「議價不是要占你便宜,也不是你要占我便宜,是我們要花時間確認彼此都覺得值得。」那天傍晚,巷口清真寺傳來喚拜聲,整條染坊巷的商人幾乎同時停下手邊工作,轉身面向牆角低聲祈禱,幾分鐘後又若無其事地繼續討價還價——那種宗教與商業無縫共存的日常,比任何一座博物館都更讓我理解這座城市的內裡。

在這裡,議價不是為了佔便宜,而是一場關於尊重的對話,雙方都要覺得值得,交易才算完成。

露台上的塔吉鍋與巷口的湯攤

從染坊巷走十分鐘,我在Nomad餐廳吃了這趟旅行最難忘的一頓午餐。餐廳位於Derb Aarjane巷內,要先鑽進R'ba Lakoua香料市集裡一段幾乎沒有招牌指引的窄巷才找得到入口,爬三層樓梯到頂樓露台,眼前突然開闊——正下方就是整片香料市集鐵皮屋頂,遠處能看到庫圖比亞清真寺(Koutoubia)的宣禮塔尖。我點了燉梅子小羊肉塔吉鍋,肉燉到用叉子輕輕一撥就化開,梅乾的甜混著肉桂與番紅花的暖香,底下鋪著杏仁碎增加口感層次,配一份藜麥沙拉解膩,兩道加一杯薄荷茶大約兩百二十迪拉姆。坐在露台吹著午後的風,看底下市集人聲鼎沸卻聽不真切,像隔著一層濾網在看這座城市。

傍晚七點,我轉戰傑馬夫納廣場(Jemaa el-Fna)的露天美食攤區。整片廣場天黑後會擺出上百個編號攤位,蒸氣、炭烤煙霧混著孜然味瀰漫整片天空。我在14號攤坐下,點了一碗哈里拉湯(harira)——番茄與扁豆燉的濃湯,配一顆水煮蛋和一塊椰棗,湯裡飄著芫荽香氣,價格約十五迪拉姆一碗,是我吃過最溫暖踏實的一碗湯。旁邊攤子在賣蝸牛湯(babbouche),老闆熱情地遞給我一小碗試喝,湯頭用十幾種香料熬煮,帶點藥膳感的苦甘,蝸牛肉Q彈,一小碗約二十迪拉姆。攤主們扯著嗓子招呼客人,語速快到我聽不懂內容,但那種熱鬧的韻律感,本身就是一種聲音的設計美學。

夜幕下的傑馬夫納廣場:光與聲音的謝幕

晚上九點,廣場的另一端聚集起說書人、彈奏格納瓦(Gnaoua)樂器的樂手和畫指甲花(henna)的婦女。我在一位老婦人攤前坐下,她用糖漿般濃稠的指甲花膏在我手背畫出對稱的藤蔓紋樣,線條走勢竟和白天在班尤瑟夫學院牆上看到的紋樣如出一轍——原來從清真寺磁磚到市集裡的手繪紋身,整座城市共用著同一套幾何語彙,只是換了媒材:磁磚、灰泥、羊毛、皮膚。廣場上方掛著一輪不太圓的月亮,底下是煙霧、鼓聲與此起彼落的招呼聲,我坐在小板凳上喝著第三杯薄荷茶,忽然覺得這趟以「藝術與設計」為名的旅程,答案其實從清晨那面赭紅色城牆就已經寫好了:馬拉喀什從來不需要美術館把藝術框起來,因為整座城市本身,從牆面顏色到市集吆喝的節奏,都是一件仍在被使用、被磨損、被重新上色的活體工藝品。隔天一早,我還去了城郊的非洲當代藝術博物館(MACAAL),看見年輕摩洛哥藝術家如何把這些傳統紋樣解構重組成裝置藝術,才確定這套視覺語言不是博物館裡的標本,而是一條還在往前走的活路。

ENGLISH

At half past six in the morning, I stepped out of a traditional riad in the Medina, the heavy wooden door clicking shut behind me. A skinny cat followed my footsteps down the still-cool cobblestones. The air carried a faint trace of orange blossom mixed with last night's charcoal smoke and the smell of leather. The sky hadn't fully lightened yet, and the ochre-red ramparts glowed with a terracotta warmth against the grey-blue dawn. Locals call this shade 'Marrakech Red' — a mix of clay, lime, and straw that has, for centuries, functioned as an unwritten building code: every new structure in the old city must keep its façade somewhere between brick-red and baked clay. I hadn't even had breakfast yet, and my head was already full of this city's grammar of color.

I gave myself one task for this trip: to read Marrakech not through its history or religion, but through color, line, and craft. It turned out the city was practically built for exactly that kind of reading.

Cobalt Mornings: Jardin Majorelle and the Yves Saint Laurent Museum

At eight o'clock sharp, I was queuing outside Jardin Majorelle in the Gueliz district. The walk from the Medina takes about twenty-five minutes along Avenue Mohammed V, a street where French plane trees and Moroccan archways trade places block by block. A petit taxi costs roughly 15–20 dirhams, though drivers rarely use the meter, so it's worth agreeing on a price before getting in. I timed my arrival for opening hour specifically to beat the tour buses that flood in after 9:30 — and it worked. For the first twenty minutes, the garden was mine and a few wandering cats.

The garden itself is small, walkable in forty minutes, but I lingered for nearly two and a half hours because that particular shade of blue is simply hypnotic. Painter Jacques Majorelle mixed this cobalt in the 1930s, and it now carries his name — saturated almost to fluorescence, yet somehow perfectly at ease against the bamboo's deep green and the terracotta pots' burnt orange. Deep inside the grounds sits the Musée YSL, designed by the French firm Studio KO, its brick-red terracotta façade perforated in a woven-looking lattice. During the day, sunlight filters through the gaps and casts a slow-moving grid of light across the interior floor. Inside, minimalist black display cases hold Yves Saint Laurent's haute couture sketches alongside the Moroccan textile patterns he collected during his years here — and suddenly the 'Berber-inspired' silhouettes from Western fashion textbooks make perfect sense, their origin sitting quietly in a workshop just down the road. A combined ticket for both sites runs about 150 dirhams; booking online in advance saves a good half hour of queuing.

Marrakech taught me that color is never just decoration — it's a language. Ochre is identity, cobalt is rebellion, indigo is protection.

A Geometry of Infinity: Ben Youssef Madrasa and the Language of the Medina

By noon I'd walked back into the Medina, ducking through the alleys beside the Souk Attarine, past the metallic clang of the coppersmiths in Souk Haddadine, following that rhythm until I reached the Ben Youssef Madrasa. Built in the 14th century under the Marinid dynasty, the entrance fee is around 50 dirhams and a visit takes roughly an hour. The moment I stepped into the central courtyard, I stopped in my tracks. Every wall, floor to ceiling, layers three distinct crafts into one visual shock: geometric mosaic tilework (zellige) at the base, carved stucco inscribed with Quranic verses in the middle band, and honeycombed cedar-wood muqarnas crowning the top. A reflecting pool in the courtyard doubles the entire façade in the water, symmetrical to the point of disorientation.

Islamic art traditionally avoids figurative representation, so craftsmen poured all their invention into pattern instead — eight-pointed stars extending infinitely, vine motifs looping without end, gesturing toward the infinite nature of God. Standing in the middle of the courtyard, I realized sunlight was the only moving element in this otherwise static architecture, shifting through the carved wooden mashrabiya screens and redrawing the shadows minute by minute — the building seemed to breathe. Compared to the more ornate Bahia Palace nearby, the Madrasa's lines feel more restrained, and precisely because of that, more pure. Not a single inch of wall is left truly blank, and the rhythm between fullness and emptiness becomes its own kind of musical design language.

The Dyers' Alley: A Lesson in Color and Hospitality

At two in the afternoon I wandered into the Souk des Teinturiers, smelling the sharp tang of indigo and pomegranate rind before I even saw a single storefront. A dyer named Youssef was plunging a thick bundle of wool into a limestone vat, his forearms stained a deep indigo up to the elbow. He caught me staring and, in a mix of French and English, explained the vats to me: yellow comes from saffron and pomegranate skin, symbolizing sun and abundance; indigo comes from the indigo plant, believed by Berbers to ward off the evil eye; black is fermented walnut shell and rusted iron water, reserved only for funerary textiles. He invited me into his family's three-generation carpet shop for mint tea, poured from a great height so the foam piled up like beer froth.

We talked for nearly an hour. He never once pushed a sale — instead he unrolled carpet after carpet, explaining the tribal identity behind each pattern: diamonds for fertility, jagged lines echoing the ridgeline of the Atlas Mountains, even the depth of a color hinting at which Berber tribe's female weavers had made it. When he finally named a price, I opened, as is customary, at half. He didn't flinch, smiling and saying, 'this is exactly how it should go.' Twenty minutes of good-natured back-and-forth later, I walked out with a small hand-woven rug at roughly forty percent of his original ask. Youssef put it plainly: 'Bargaining isn't about you cheating me or me cheating you — it's about both of us spending the time to agree it was worth it.' That evening, the call to prayer sounded from the alley's mosque, and nearly every merchant in the dyers' souk paused mid-transaction, turned toward the wall, and murmured a quiet prayer — then, minutes later, went right back to haggling as if nothing had happened. That seamless coexistence of commerce and faith told me more about this city than any museum could.

Here, bargaining isn't about getting the upper hand — it's a conversation about respect, and the deal only closes once both sides feel it was worth it.

Rooftop Tagines and Alley-Corner Soup: An Edible Afternoon

Ten minutes from the dyers' alley, I had the most memorable lunch of the whole trip at Nomad. The restaurant sits down Derb Aarjane, reachable only by threading through the R'ba Lakoua spice market along an almost unmarked lane, then climbing three flights of stairs to a rooftop terrace that suddenly opens onto a wide view — the tin rooftops of the spice souk spread out below, and in the distance, the minaret of the Koutoubia Mosque. I ordered the lamb tagine with stewed prunes: the meat fell apart at the touch of a fork, the prunes' sweetness mingling with warm notes of cinnamon and saffron, crushed almonds scattered on top for texture, alongside a quinoa salad to cut the richness. Two dishes and a mint tea came to about 220 dirhams. Sitting on that terrace in the afternoon breeze, watching the market's noise rise and blur beneath me, felt like viewing the city through a fine mesh screen.

By seven in the evening I'd moved on to the open-air food stalls of Jemaa el-Fna. After dark, the square fills with a hundred numbered stalls, steam and charcoal smoke thick with the smell of cumin drifting up into the sky. I sat at stall No. 14 and ordered a bowl of harira — a rich tomato-and-lentil soup topped with a boiled egg and a date, fragrant with coriander, for about 15 dirhams, the warmest, most grounding bowl of soup I've had anywhere. Next door, a vendor selling snail soup (babbouche) handed me a small taster cup unprompted — a broth simmered with a dozen-odd spices, faintly bitter like herbal medicine, with tender, chewy snails, about 20 dirhams a bowl. The stallholders shouted for customers faster than I could follow, but that chaotic cadence was its own kind of sonic design.

Nightfall at Jemaa el-Fna: The City's Final Performance

By nine, the far end of the square filled with storytellers, Gnaoua musicians, and henna artists. I sat down in front of an elderly woman who painted a symmetrical vine pattern on the back of my hand with syrup-thick henna paste — and I realized the lines matched almost exactly the patterns I'd seen carved into the walls of the Ben Youssef Madrasa that morning. From mosque tilework to hand-drawn skin art in the market, this entire city shares one geometric vocabulary, only changing its medium: tile, stucco, wool, skin. A slightly imperfect moon hung above the square, smoke and drumbeats rising beneath it, and as I sipped my third mint tea of the day on a small stool, it struck me that this whole 'Art & Design' journey had already answered itself back at that ochre-red rampart at dawn. Marrakech never needed a museum to frame its art, because the entire city — from the color of its walls to the rhythm of its market cries — is a living piece of craft still being used, worn down, and repainted. The next morning I visited MACAAL, the Museum of African Contemporary Art on the city's outskirts, and watched young Moroccan artists deconstruct these same traditional patterns into contemporary installations — proof that this visual language isn't a museum specimen at all, but a road still very much being walked.
