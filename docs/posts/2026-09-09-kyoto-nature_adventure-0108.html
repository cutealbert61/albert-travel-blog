中文

手機鬧鐘在清晨五點十分震動,我在京都車站附近的膠囊旅館裡摸黑爬起來,心裡只想著一件事:如果不趁旭日還沒把嵐山的竹林染成觀光客的顏色之前出門,我大概永遠不會知道京都真正安靜的樣子。這趟旅程我刻意把寺廟名單縮到最短,反倒把地圖上的等高線畫得又粗又密——我要的不是金閣寺倒影裡的京都,而是那個藏在山稜與河谷之間、會讓小腿發痠、讓鞋子沾滿泥土的京都。

破曉嵐山:竹林深處與猴山之巔

六點整,我從JR嵯峨嵐山駅走出來,街上只有送報機車的引擎聲和便利商店自動門開闔的聲響。往野宮神社方向的竹林小徑上,我是唯一一個人。京都市曾把這片竹林的沙沙聲列入「日本音風景百選」,那個清晨我終於懂了為什麼——風一吹,竹稈互相摩擦、竹葉翻動,聲音由遠而近像潮水湧來,腳邊還帶著昨夜露水的濕冷,踩在碎石路上,鞋底傳來細微的顆粒感。沒有人力車鈴聲,沒有自拍棒,只有光線一寸寸從竹節縫隙間篩下來,把整條路染成半透明的青綠色。

七點左右,我沿著渡月橋走到嵐山猴子公園入口,買了五百五十日圓的門票,開始爬一段將近二十分鐘、坡度不小的登山道——沒有階梯扶手,全是踩實的泥土路,雨後會非常滑,我慶幸自己這趟旅程的鞋子選對了。爬到半山腰,喘氣聲混著樹叢間野生獼猴移動的窸窣聲,山頂展望台豁然開朗,整個京都盆地連同東山群峰都攤在眼前,晨霧還沒完全散去,鴨川像一條淡灰色的緞帶穿過城市。幾隻獼猴大方地坐在木造欄杆上梳理毛髮,完全不理會遊客,我買了一包一百日圓的花生從室內餵食窗口遞出去,一隻年輕猴子伸手接過,動作俐落得像在談生意。

京都最誠實的樣子,不在金箔貼成的殿堂裡,而在露水還沒被踩乾的山徑上。

鞍馬貴船越嶺:森林裡的修驗之路

把嵐山甩在腦後,我搭上叡山電鐵,從出町柳駅出發前往鞍馬。這條路線本身就是風景——電車行經市原駅到二之瀨駅之間會刻意減速,兩側楓樹在秋天形成一條紅色隧道,車廂燈光還會配合調暗,像是專門為賞楓設計的一場小型表演。單程票價四百七十日圓,若打算像我一樣連鞍馬貴船一起走完,建議直接買叡電一日券,一千兩百日圓,沿線任意上下車也比較划算。

九點半抵達鞍馬駅,一出站就被那尊巨大的紅臉天狗面具嚇了一跳——這裡是傳說中源義經少年時期跟隨天狗習武的地方。我在車站旁的小店租了一個一百日圓的熊鈴掛在背包上,店員用不太流利的英文加上比手畫腳提醒我:「熊,山裡真的有熊,尤其傍晚時分。」買票進入鞍馬寺(愛山費三百日圓)後沿著仁王門一路往上爬,石階陡到我必須放慢呼吸節奏,途經僧正之谷,傳說就是義經與天狗修行的山谷,巨大杉樹的樹根盤結在步道兩側,踩上去要特別小心,雨後濕滑,我看到好幾處立牌用日文和簡單英文提醒「路面濕滑、小心跌倒」。

翻過鞍馬寺本殿後方的稜線,再走大約四十分鐘的森林小徑才能抵達貴船神社的奧宮入口。整段從鞍馬駅到貴船神社,我實際走了兩小時二十分鐘,中途在本殿前的展望台休息拍照、又在僧正之谷駐足看了將近十分鐘的巨杉,才拉長了時間。沿途完全沒有便利商店或自動販賣機,我出發前在鞍馬駅買的一瓶水到後段已見底,強烈建議至少帶六百毫升以上的水和一條毛巾,因為坡度落差超過四百公尺,即使是十一月中旬,爬到一半我依然滿頭大汗。整條山徑沒有手機訊號,靠的是沿途清楚的木牌指標,只要跟著「貴船神社」方向走基本不會迷路,但天黑前一定要下山,鞍馬寺入山門在傍晚四點半準時關閉,錯過就得原路折返,這點務必牢記。

貴船川床:溪水上的流水麵

下午一點半,我終於走下最後一段石階,水聲越來越大——貴船川的川床料理季節從五月延續到九月,雖然我造訪時已接近尾聲,老字號「ひろ文」仍在營業。木造平台直接搭建在溪流正上方,腳下踏板隨著水流的力道傳來細微震動,水氣裹著森林的濕冷味道撲面而來,比山下涼了至少五度。

我點了招牌流水麵,一份一千三百日圓。店員把整段剖開的青竹接成長長的滑道,冰涼的貴船山泉水從竹管上方注入,白色細麵一段段順流而下,我得眼明手快地用筷子在麵條滑過眼前的瞬間夾起,沾進小碗裡混著柴魚高湯、蔥花和山葵的沾醬——動作慢一拍麵就滑進竹管末端的收集網裡。麵條入口滑順帶著微微甘甜,冰涼的溫度和沾醬的鹹鮮形成反差,配著潺潺水聲和偶爾飛濺到手背上的水花,那種專注在「夾麵條」這件小事上的單純快樂,比任何米其林餐廳都更讓我印象深刻。加點的鹽烤香魚(鮎の塩焼き,約一千八百日圓)整條連骨帶皮烤得酥脆,魚肚裡帶著微苦的內臟味,是我在京都吃過最有「山野氣息」的一餐。

伏見稻荷凌晨攻頂:狐狸、鳥居與信仰的重量

隔天,我把鬧鐘設在四點五十分,搭首班JR奈良線從京都駅到稻荷駅,只要五分鐘車程。清晨五點半的伏見稻荷大社幾乎沒有觀光客,只有零星幾位早起晨跑的當地居民和準備開店的攤販。穿過樓門後,千本鳥居的入口在路燈映照下泛著暗紅色,朱紅色木柱一根接一根往山裡延伸,踩在石板路上安靜得能聽見自己的呼吸聲。

爬到四辻展望台大約花了四十分鐘,這裡視野開闊,可以俯瞰整個京都市區,但我沒有停留太久,決定繼續往一之峰山頂前進——這一段路的鳥居明顯稀疏、老舊,遊客幾乎絕跡,取而代之的是無數「御塚」石塚,每一座都是信眾或企業自費奉納,一根中型鳥居的捐獻金額,根據我後來查到的資料,大約從十七萬五千日圓到超過一百三十萬日圓不等,尺寸越大越貴。走到接近山頂時,我在一處小小的祠堂前遇見一位穿著藏青色圍裙的老婦人,她正蹲著點燃蠟燭、擺上一顆蘋果和一小瓶清酒。她注意到我好奇的眼神,用簡單的英文夾雜日文告訴我,她每個月都會上山參拜一次,感謝生意平安;而每年二月「初午」那天——也就是稻荷神最初降臨此山的紀念日——整個伏見會擠滿來還願、祈求商賣繁盛的商家老闆,連神社入口的攤販都會賣一種叫做「畑菜」的油菜炒物,是初午祭典特有的節令小菜。她特別叮囑我,參拜狐狸像時千萬別伸手去摸牠們嘴裡叼著的稻穗、寶珠或鑰匙,那是狐狸作為稻荷神使者的象徵物,觸碰被認為不敬。她說完便對著祠堂深深一鞠躬,轉身沿著我剛剛爬上來的階梯,腳步輕快地下山,像是完成了每天例行的一件小事。

信仰在這座山上不是壯觀的儀式,而是一位老婦人每個月固定爬上來點一根蠟燭的日常。

六點四十分,我站在標高兩百三十三公尺的一之峰山頂,山下的京都被染成一片淡金色,山風把汗濕的後背吹得發涼。整段從山腳到山頂加上拍照停留,我總共走了將近一個半小時,中途完全沒有補給站,回程改走西側較少人知道的「熊鷹社」路線下山,沿途幾間清晨六點就開始賣烤丸子和甘酒的小茶屋陸續亮起燈,老闆娘一邊生火一邊跟我點頭打招呼,那一刻我確實感覺自己不是遊客,而是這座山早晨日常的一部分。

保津川泛舟:嵐山峽谷的湍流洗禮

結束伏見稻荷的攻頂,下午我搭火車到龜岡,參加保津川遊船會社的泛舟行程,費用六千三百日圓,由三位船夫合力操控一艘傳統木船,順著保津川往嵐山方向漂流十六公里、耗時將近兩小時。江戶時期這條河是運送木材與物資到京都的重要水路,船夫至今仍保留著吆喝口令與撐篙的古法技術。船行至幾處被稱為「小地獄」的急流時,浪花直接打上船舷潑濕了我的整條褲管,船夫一邊用力撐篙一邊用京都腔的日語跟乘客開玩笑說「這才是免費的溫泉水」。兩岸楓紅與常綠杉木層層疊疊倒映在碧綠的水面上,偶爾能看到岩壁上停著白鷺鷥,完全不怕船隻經過。船程尾聲水流趨緩,夕陽正好斜斜切過渡月橋的橋墩,把整條河染成橘紅色,那一刻我才真正確定,這趟放棄大半座金碧輝煌的京都、把時間都留給山林與河水的決定,是對的。

回到旅館放下溼透的背包,我看著手機裡這兩天記錄的步數——三萬八千步,還有滿滿一身衣物混著泥土與河水的味道。京都當然有它精緻典雅的一面,但如果只走傳統的觀光路線,大概永遠不會知道這座城市其實被三面山巒和無數溪谷緊緊環抱。真正認識京都的方法,或許就是把自己交給那些沒有電車直達、需要流汗才能抵達的角落。

ENGLISH

My phone alarm buzzed at 5:10 a.m. and I hauled myself out of a capsule bed near Kyoto Station in the dark, thinking only one thing: if I didn't get out before sunrise turned Arashiyama's bamboo grove into a tourist-brochure cliché, I'd never know what a quiet Kyoto morning actually felt like. For this trip I deliberately kept my temple list short and instead traced the thickest contour lines I could find on the map. I wasn't chasing the Kyoto reflected in Kinkaku-ji's golden pond — I wanted the Kyoto hidden between ridgelines and river gorges, the one that leaves your calves sore and your shoes caked in mud.

Dawn in Arashiyama: Into the Bamboo, Up to the Monkeys

At six sharp I stepped out of JR Saga-Arashiyama Station onto streets with nothing but a newspaper delivery scooter and the hiss of a convenience store's automatic doors. On the bamboo path toward Nonomiya Shrine, I was completely alone. Kyoto City once listed the rustle of this grove among Japan's 'One Hundred Soundscapes,' and that morning I finally understood why — wind moved through the stalks and the whole grove creaked and swayed like an incoming tide, while last night's dew still clung cold to the gravel underfoot, each pebble giving a faint grit beneath my soles. No rickshaw bells, no selfie sticks, just light filtering down in slivers through the jointed stalks, washing the entire path in translucent jade.

By around seven I'd walked to Togetsukyo Bridge and on to the entrance of Iwatayama Monkey Park, paid the 550-yen admission, and started up a steep, unrailed dirt trail that took nearly twenty minutes to climb — the kind of packed earth that turns treacherous after rain, and I was grateful I'd packed proper hiking shoes. Halfway up, my own labored breathing mixed with the rustle of wild macaques moving through the brush, and then the summit opened into a wide observation deck: the entire Kyoto basin and the Higashiyama mountains spread out below, morning mist still lingering, the Kamo River a pale ribbon threading through the city. A few macaques sat unbothered on the wooden railing grooming each other, ignoring the handful of visitors entirely. I bought a 100-yen bag of peanuts and passed it through the feeding window inside the lodge; a young monkey snatched it with the brisk efficiency of someone closing a business deal.

Kyoto's most honest face isn't found in gold-leafed halls — it's on a mountain path before the dew has been trampled dry.

The Kurama–Kibune Traverse: A Forest Trail of Ascetic Legend

Leaving Arashiyama behind, I boarded the Eizan Railway from Demachiyanagi Station toward Kurama. The train ride itself is a spectacle — between Ichihara and Ninose stations the driver deliberately slows down as maple branches form a red tunnel overhead in autumn, and the carriage lights even dim to enhance the effect, like a small performance staged just for leaf-peepers. A one-way ticket costs 470 yen, but since I planned to hike the full Kurama-to-Kibune traverse, I bought the 1,200-yen Eizan Railway day pass, which pays off if you hop on and off along the line.

I arrived at Kurama Station at 9:30 and was immediately startled by the enormous red-faced tengu mask looming at the entrance — this is, according to legend, where the young warrior Minamoto no Yoshitsune trained under mountain goblins. I rented a 100-yen bear bell from a shop by the station; the clerk, in broken English backed up by emphatic gestures, warned me, 'Bears, real bears in the mountains, especially in the evening.' After paying the 300-yen mountain entry fee at Kurama-dera and passing through the Niomon Gate, I climbed a steep stone staircase that forced me to slow my breathing, eventually reaching Sojogatani Valley, the legendary training ground of Yoshitsune and the tengu. Massive cedar roots knotted across the path on both sides, and I had to watch my footing carefully — several signs, in Japanese and simple English, warned of slippery surfaces after rain.

Crossing the ridge behind Kurama-dera's main hall, another forty-minute forest trail led down to the inner shrine entrance of Kifune Shrine. The full Kurama-to-Kifune hike took me two hours and twenty minutes, stretched out by a rest at the overlook near the main hall and nearly ten minutes lingering beneath the giant cedars of Sojogatani. There's no convenience store or vending machine anywhere along the route; the single bottle of water I bought at Kurama Station ran dry well before the end, so I'd strongly recommend carrying at least 600ml of water and a towel — the elevation change exceeds 400 meters, and even in mid-November I was drenched in sweat by the halfway mark. There's no phone signal on the trail either, but the wooden trail markers are clear enough that getting lost is unlikely as long as you keep following signs toward Kifune Shrine. What matters more is timing: Kurama-dera's mountain gate closes promptly at 4:30 p.m., and missing it means backtracking the entire way you came — something worth remembering before you start.

Kawadoko Dining on the Kibune River: Noodles Riding the Current

At 1:30 p.m. I finally descended the last stretch of stone steps, the sound of rushing water growing louder — Kibune's riverside dining platforms, or kawadoko, run from May through September, and though I'd arrived near the season's end, the century-old restaurant Hirobun was still open. Its wooden platforms are built directly over the stream; the floorboards trembled faintly with the current beneath my feet, and cold, forest-scented air rose up, dropping the temperature at least five degrees compared to the valley floor.

I ordered the restaurant's signature nagashi somen for 1,300 yen. The staff had rigged a long split-bamboo flume, and icy spring water from Mount Kibune poured down it in a steady rush, carrying short bundles of white noodles past diners seated along its length. I had to catch them mid-slide with my chopsticks — miss the timing and they'd shoot straight into the collection net at the bottom — then dunk them into a small bowl of dashi mixed with scallions and wasabi. The noodles were slick and faintly sweet, and the contrast between their icy chill and the salty-savory dip was sharper than I expected; between the rushing water below and the occasional splash on the back of my hand, there was something almost meditative about focusing on nothing but catching noodles. It struck me as more memorable than any Michelin tasting menu I'd had. I also ordered a whole salt-grilled ayu (sweetfish, roughly 1,800 yen), crisped skin and bones intact, its faintly bitter organs still inside — the most 'mountain-and-river' dish I ate anywhere in Kyoto.

Pre-Dawn Ascent of Fushimi Inari: Foxes, Torii, and the Weight of Devotion

The next day I set my alarm for 4:50 a.m. and caught the first JR Nara Line train from Kyoto Station to Inari Station — a five-minute ride. At 5:30 in the morning, Fushimi Inari Taisha was almost entirely free of tourists, just a handful of local early-morning joggers and vendors setting up their stalls. Beyond the Romon Gate, the entrance to the Senbon Torii glowed a dull crimson under the streetlights, vermillion pillars marching one after another into the mountain, and my footsteps on the stone path were quiet enough that I could hear my own breathing.

It took about forty minutes to reach the Yotsutsuji junction, where the view opens wide over the city, but I didn't linger long — I pressed on toward the summit at Ichinomine. Beyond Yotsutsuji, the torii thin out and grow noticeably older, tourists nearly vanish, and in their place stand countless otsuka — stone mounds donated by individual worshippers or companies. According to what I later looked up, a mid-sized torii donation runs anywhere from 175,000 yen to over 1,300,000 yen depending on its size. Near the summit, I came across an elderly woman in a navy apron crouched before a small shrine, lighting a candle and setting out an apple and a small bottle of sake. Noticing my curiosity, she explained in a mix of simple English and Japanese that she climbs the mountain once a month to give thanks for her business's stability, and that every February, on the day of Hatsuuma — the anniversary of Inari's descent to this mountain — all of Fushimi fills with shop owners coming to fulfill vows and pray for commercial prosperity; even the stalls near the shrine entrance sell a seasonal dish of stir-fried hatakena greens unique to that festival. She specifically warned me never to touch the objects held in the mouths of the fox statues — rice sheaves, jewels, or keys — since these are sacred symbols of the fox as Inari's messenger, and touching them is considered disrespectful. After she finished speaking, she bowed deeply toward the shrine and turned to descend the very steps I'd just climbed, her pace light, as though completing one small, ordinary task in her day.

On this mountain, devotion isn't a grand ceremony — it's an old woman climbing up every month just to light a single candle.

At 6:40 a.m. I stood at the summit of Ichinomine, 233 meters above sea level, watching Kyoto below turn pale gold as mountain wind chilled the sweat on my back. The full climb, including photo stops, took just under an hour and a half, with no rest stations anywhere along the way. I descended via the quieter western route past Kumataka-sha, less known to visitors, where a few small teahouses already had their lights on by six, selling grilled dango and amazake. One shopkeeper nodded at me while stoking her fire, and in that moment I genuinely felt less like a tourist and more like a small part of this mountain's morning routine.

Hozugawa River Rafting: A Baptism of Whitewater Through the Arashiyama Gorge

After the Fushimi Inari climb, I took an afternoon train to Kameoka to join a Hozugawa River rafting trip, priced at 6,300 yen, aboard a traditional wooden boat guided by three boatmen down sixteen kilometers of river toward Arashiyama, a journey of nearly two hours. During the Edo period this river served as a vital route for transporting timber and goods into Kyoto, and the boatmen still use the old calls and pole-handling techniques passed down through generations. At several rapids nicknamed 'little hells,' spray crashed directly over the gunwale and soaked my pant legs through, while the boatmen, poling hard, joked in thick Kyoto dialect that this was 'a free hot spring, courtesy of the river.' Maple reds and evergreen cedars layered along both banks reflected in the jade-green water, and occasionally a white heron perched motionless on the rock face, entirely unbothered by our passing boat. As the current slowed near the end of the run, the setting sun cut diagonally across the pillars of Togetsukyo Bridge, staining the whole river orange, and in that moment I was certain: choosing to give most of my Kyoto itinerary to mountains and rivers rather than gilded halls had been the right call.

Back at the hotel, dropping my soaked backpack on the floor, I checked my phone and found thirty-eight thousand steps logged over two days, along with clothes that smelled thoroughly of mud and river water. Kyoto certainly has its refined, elegant side, but if you only follow the conventional sightseeing route, you might never realize this city is tightly embraced on three sides by mountains and countless ravines. Perhaps the truest way to know Kyoto is to surrender yourself to the corners no train reaches directly — the ones you can only earn by sweating for them.
