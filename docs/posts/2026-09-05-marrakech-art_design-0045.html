中文

清晨六點,我推開Riad El Fenn那扇釘滿銅釘的木門,巷子裡還沒有人。空氣裡有一種特殊的味道——陶土牆被夜露打濕後的土腥味,混著遠處麵包坊剛出爐的麥香,還有一絲若有似無的橙花水氣味,不知道是從哪戶人家的早茶飄出來的。喚拜聲從庫圖比亞清真寺的宣禮塔傳來,拉得很長,迴盪在紅褐色的牆面之間,整座麥地那像是被這聲音喚醒的。我站在原地聽完整段喚拜,才意識到自己來馬拉喀什,不是為了看沙漠或市集,而是為了看這座城市怎麼用泥土、灰泥和顏色說話。

馬拉喀什的紅,其實有官方名字,叫「馬拉喀什赭紅」(Marrakech Ochre)。1913年法國殖民時期就立法規定,麥地那內所有建築外牆必須維持這種赭紅色調,理由是要與周邊的紅土地和阿特拉斯山脈的岩石顏色呼應。走在窄巷裡,你會發現連水泥電線杆都被漆成同樣的顏色,連麥當勞的招牌都得低調地融入這片紅褐色調——這種對城市色彩的集體堅持,本身就是一種設計美學的宣言。我沿著Riad El Fenn外的小巷往北走,兩側牆面斑駁,露出裡層不同年代補上去的灰泥層,像是城市自己的年輪。

灰泥與星辰:本尤素夫學院與巴伊亞宮的幾何詩篇

早上八點半,我到本尤素夫學院(Ben Youssef Madrasa)門口時,排隊的人已經不少。這座建於十四世紀、十六世紀重建的古蘭經學院,是我認為整個北非保存最完整的伊斯蘭幾何裝飾範本。門票約50迪爾汗,換算台幣不到兩百塊,但裡面的工藝density高到讓人想留一整個上午。中庭是一方淺水池,水面平靜無波,倒映著四周牆面雕花——那不是隨便的花紋,是用雪松木雕、大理石鑲嵌、彩色瓷磚(zellige)三種工法疊出來的庫法體古蘭經文字,環繞整座建築轉了一整圈。導覽員告訴我,這些幾何圖案背後有嚴謹的數學比例,八角星、六角星層層嵌套,象徵伊斯蘭教義裡「真主無限」的概念——因為圖案沒有起點也沒有終點,可以無限延伸下去。

這裡沒有一面牆是空的,但也沒有一面牆是喧鬧的——摩爾式裝飾的祕密,在於用密度創造出一種奇異的靜謐。

離開學院,我步行約十五分鐘到巴伊亞宮(Bahia Palace)。這座十九世紀晚期由攝政大臣Si Moussa下令興建、後來由其子Ba Ahmed擴建的宮殿,格局完全是另一種邏輯——它不追求對稱,而是刻意用不規則的庭院串連,呈現一種「移步換景」的節奏感。我在種滿橘子樹與茉莉的中庭裡站了很久,樹蔭把光線切成碎片灑在彩磚地面上,天花板則是滿滿的雪松木雕彩繪,顏色以靛藍、赭紅、金黃為主,據說當年是用蛋清和礦物顏料調製,歷經一百多年依然鮮豔。宮殿裡有一整排小房間,是當年四位正室與眾多妾室各自的居所,每間房的瓷磚花紋都略有不同,導覽員笑說這是設計上的「差異化待遇」,誰的房間裝飾越華麗,誰在後宮的地位就越高——原來室內設計在這裡曾經是一種權力的語言。

藍色庭院與紙上時尚:馬若雷勒花園與YSL美術館全記錄

這一站我特別想記錄實際走法,因為排隊狀況比我預期複雜很多。馬若雷勒花園(Jardin Majorelle)位於新城區古利茲(Gueliz),距離麥地那開車約十五到二十分鐘,我搭的是路邊招手攔的小黃計程車,車資談定30迪爾汗(建議上車前先講好價,司機幾乎不跳表)。官網上寫開門時間是早上八點,我七點四十五分抵達時,售票口前已經排了將近百人的隊伍,大多是歐洲團客。這裡強烈建議提前在官網訂票,現場排隊平均要等四十分鐘以上,而且旺季(三月到五月、九月到十一月)常常會提前售罄。我當時是現場買票,花園與YSL美術館的聯票是195迪爾汗,約合台幣六百多元,單看花園是150迪爾汗。

進場之後,眼前那片「馬若雷勒藍」比我在照片裡看到的還要濃烈,是一種帶點紫調的鈷藍,塗在別墅立面、花盆、走道扶手上,和滿園仙人掌、竹林、九重葛的綠形成強烈對比。這座花園是法國畫家Jacques Majorelle在1920到1930年代建的私人花園,他自己調配出這種藍色並申請了專利。1980年代花園一度荒廢,後來被Yves Saint Laurent與伴侶Pierre Bergé買下修復,才有了現在的樣子。走完花園大約需要四十五分鐘,接著我沿著園區內小徑走到YSL美術館——這棟建築本身就是一件作品,由巴黎建築事務所Studio KO操刀,外牆用赤陶磚砌出類似傳統摩洛哥編織紋理的鏤空圖案,白天陽光穿過磚縫,館內牆面會浮現細碎的光斑,像是把麥地那的光影邏輯搬進了現代建築語彙裡。館內常設展陳列聖羅蘭生前手稿與設計草圖,還有一整面牆展示他從摩洛哥色彩與圖騰中汲取靈感的高訂系列,包括他自己說過那句話的手寫版本:「摩洛哥教會我顏色。」整趟行程含排隊、逛園區、看美術館,我大概花了兩個半小時,建議至少留兩小時以上,中午前離開最能避開下午的旅遊團人潮。

西迪加內姆:工業區裡的設計新浪潮

如果馬若雷勒花園代表的是舊時代的浪漫,那西迪加內姆(Sidi Ghanem)工業區就是馬拉喀什設計圈現在進行式的樣貌。這裡原本是倉庫與小型工廠聚集地,大約從十年前開始,一批本地與外國設計師陸續進駐,把水泥廠房改造成工作室與展示空間。我從古利茲搭計程車過去,車程約十五分鐘,車資40迪爾汗。整條街道乍看灰撲撲,毫不起眼,但幾扇被漆成靛藍或土黃色的鐵捲門後面,藏著完全不同的世界。

我第一個走進的是LRNCE陶藝工作室,創辦人是移居摩洛哥多年的法國設計師Laetitia Lecomte。她的陶器保留手工拉坯的不規則邊緣,但釉色用的是極簡的燕麥白、灰藍、赭石色,和市集裡常見的鮮豔手繪陶器完全是兩種語彙——傳統與當代在這裡不是對立,而是並置。店裡一只中型湯碗標價約450迪爾汗,不便宜,但摸起來的重量與粗礪的邊緣觸感,讓人理解為什麼這個牌子能被巴黎、東京的選物店搶著代理。往前走幾步是MCC概念店,裡面擺滿用摩洛哥傳統染皮工藝製作的圓形皮革坐墊(pouf),顏色從芥末黃到深磚紅一應俱全,老闆告訴我這些皮革是用石榴皮與番紅花天然染色,一個坐墊要價800到1200迪爾汗。走在這條街上,兩側倉庫外牆偶爾會冒出當地年輕藝術家的塗鴉——柏柏爾文字符號與街頭噴漆字體混搭,和麥地那裡千年不變的幾何瓷磚形成一種有趣的時空對話,讓我覺得馬拉喀什的設計基因從來沒有斷過,只是換了一種材質繼續生長。

傳統與當代在這裡不是對立,而是並置——就像這座城市本身,永遠同時活在好幾個世紀裡。

廣場邊的滋味:哈里拉湯與薄荷茶的日常儀式

傍晚五點半,我回到德吉瑪廣場(Jemaa el-Fnaa),這時候攤販正在搭起編號的餐車,煙從烤肉架冒起來,混著孜然、番紅花、烤肉焦香瀰漫整個廣場。我在標著「14號」的攤位坐下,老闆Hassan用阿拉伯語跟隔壁攤位喊了幾句,轉頭用還算流利的法語問我要什麼。我點了一碗哈里拉湯(harira),15迪爾汗,湯裡有番茄、扁豆、鷹嘴豆和細麵,質地濃稠得像羹,喝下去先是番茄的酸,接著是孜然與薑黃的暖香在喉頭散開,最後撒上的香菜碎讓整碗湯有種清新的尾韻——這是齋戒月期間摩洛哥人開齋的第一口食物,現在則是廣場上最庶民的日常宵夜。旁邊攤位賣的蝸牛湯(babbouche)我也鼓起勇氣試了,一碗5迪爾汗,湯頭用了十幾種香料熬煮,喝起來帶著八角與甘草的氣味,蝸牛肉本身沒什麼味道,吃的其實是那口湯的香氣層次。

吃完湯,我走去廣場邊巷子裡的Chez Chegrouni,這是我在網路上被推薦最多次的平價塔吉店。我點了燉羊肉配蜜棗與杏仁的塔吉(tagine),60迪爾汗,約合台幣兩百元出頭。陶鍋上桌時蓋子一掀,蒸氣夾著肉桂與蜂蜜的甜香撲面而來,羊肉燉到用叉子一碰就化開,蜜棗的甜膩恰好平衡了肉的油脂感,鍋底還鋪著吸飽了醬汁的洋蔥絲,配著現烤的圓餅(khobz)一起吃,餅皮外層微焦、內裡鬆軟,拿來蘸醬汁剛剛好。店裡沒有冷氣,風扇吱嘎轉著,隔壁桌的本地計程車司機一邊看足球轉播一邊跟老闆抬槓,那種吵鬧和油煙味,反而讓這頓飯吃得特別踏實。飯後照例來一杯薄荷茶(atay),服務生倒茶時特意把壺舉得老高,茶水劃出一道弧線落進玻璃杯,他說這樣才能讓茶葉的香氣和空氣充分接觸,產生泡沫——這杯茶不用另外算錢,是店家的待客心意,甜得有點誇張,但配著剛才那口鹹香的塔吉,竟意外地順口。

哈曼裡的沉默對話與德吉瑪的日落

隔天下午,我去了老城裡一間叫Hammam de la Rose的傳統澡堂,體驗當地人每週固定的清潔儀式。摩洛哥的公共哈曼通常男女分開時段使用,我去的時段是女賓專用,櫃檯收費250迪爾汗,包含黑肥皂去角質全套服務。換上薄棉浴巾走進蒸氣室,裡面沒有什麼交談聲,只有水潑在瓷磚地上的回音和遠處水龍頭的滴答聲。服務阿姨先幫我全身塗滿savon noir——一種用橄欖油與研磨橄欖渣製成的黑肥皂,質地黏稠帶點油潤,靜置十分鐘讓它軟化角質,然後用一條粗糙的絲瓜手套(kessa)用力刮過全身,搓下來的皮屑多到讓我有點驚訝,但皮膚瞬間變得光滑。整個過程她幾乎不說話,只用手勢示意翻身、抬手,那種沉默裡帶著一種熟練的溫柔,讓我想起這其實是幾百年來當地女性每週固定的社交與清潔場合——在沒有自來水浴室的年代,哈曼是女人們交換家族消息、談論婚事的地方,如今雖然家家戶戶都有浴室,很多本地人還是保留每週去一次哈曼的習慣,不是為了洗澡,而是為了那種被好好對待的儀式感。

離開哈曼時全身輕盈得像換了一層皮,我漫步回到德吉瑪廣場,太陽正好斜斜地打在庫圖比亞清真寺的宣禮塔上,塔身的赭紅色被夕陽染成近乎金色。廣場上說書人、耍蛇人和畫亨娜的婦人陸續收拾家當,取而代之的是成排的宵夜攤子亮起串燈,空氣裡蜂蜜堅果的甜香與炭烤羊肉的煙味混在一起。一位在市集擺攤賣皮革製品的老闆Youssef看我拿著相機東張西望,主動用生澀的中文跟我打招呼——他說前幾年學了幾句應付中國遊客,還笑著糾正我拍照的角度,說要蹲低一點才能把宣禮塔和廣場人群一起收進畫面。那一刻我突然明白,這座城市最動人的設計,或許不在瓷磚或建築裡,而在這種毫不設防、隨手就能開啟的人際互動之中——馬拉喀什把美感刻進了牆壁,也刻進了日常生活裡每一次隨口的招呼。臨走前我回頭望了一眼廣場,燈火漸次亮起,像是這座紅色城市每晚都要重新演一次的、屬於自己的舞台劇。

ENGLISH

At six in the morning I pushed open the studded wooden door of Riad El Fenn and stepped into an empty alley. The air carried a particular mix of smells — the mineral tang of ochre walls still damp from the night, the yeasty warmth of bread baking somewhere nearby, and a faint trace of orange blossom water drifting out from someone's early tea. The call to prayer rose from the Koutoubia minaret, long and unhurried, echoing between the reddish walls until the whole medina seemed to wake up because of it. I stood still and listened to the entire call before realizing that I hadn't really come to Marrakech for the desert or the souks. I had come to see how this city speaks through clay, plaster, and color.

The red of Marrakech actually has an official name: Marrakech Ochre. Back in 1913, during the French protectorate, a law was passed requiring every building in the medina to be painted in this reddish-ochre tone, echoing the surrounding red earth and the rock of the Atlas Mountains in the distance. Walking the narrow lanes, I noticed even the concrete utility poles were painted the same shade, and even a McDonald's sign had been quietly toned down to blend into the palette. This collective insistence on color is, in itself, a design statement. I followed the alley north from the riad, past walls flaking in patches that revealed older layers of plaster underneath — like the city's own tree rings.

Plaster and Stars: The Geometric Poetry of Ben Youssef Madrasa and Bahia Palace

By eight thirty, the line outside Ben Youssef Madrasa was already substantial. Built in the fourteenth century and rebuilt in the sixteenth, this former Quranic school is, to my mind, the most complete surviving showcase of Islamic geometric ornament in North Africa. Admission runs around 50 dirhams, less than seven US dollars, but the density of craftsmanship inside easily deserves a whole morning. The central courtyard holds a shallow reflecting pool, its still surface mirroring walls carved in three overlapping techniques — cedar wood carving, marble inlay, and zellige tilework — all wrapping around the building in a continuous band of Kufic script. My guide explained that these interlocking eight- and six-pointed stars follow strict mathematical proportions, meant to evoke the idea that God's nature is infinite: the patterns have no beginning and no end, and could theoretically extend forever.

No wall here is left empty, yet none feels loud — the secret of Moorish ornament is that density itself can produce a strange kind of stillness.

From the madrasa I walked about fifteen minutes to Bahia Palace, built in the late nineteenth century for the regent Si Moussa and later expanded by his son Ba Ahmed. Its logic is entirely different — not symmetry, but a deliberately irregular sequence of courtyards designed to be discovered step by step. I lingered a long while in a courtyard planted with orange and jasmine trees, watching sunlight break into fragments across the tiled floor beneath the branches. The painted cedar ceilings above, in indigo, ochre, and gold, are said to have been mixed with egg white and mineral pigment more than a century ago and still hold their brightness. A row of small rooms once housed the palace's four wives and numerous concubines, each chamber tiled with a slightly different pattern. My guide joked that this was differentiated treatment by design — the more elaborate the tilework, the higher the status of the woman who lived there. Interior decoration, it seems, once functioned here as a language of power.

Cobalt Courtyards and Couture on Paper: A Full Account of Jardin Majorelle and the Musée Yves Saint Laurent

This stop deserves a detailed walk-through, because the logistics turned out trickier than I expected. Jardin Majorelle sits in the new town, Gueliz, about fifteen to twenty minutes by car from the medina. I flagged a small red petit taxi and negotiated a fare of 30 dirhams — always agree on a price before getting in, since meters are rarely used. The website lists an eight o'clock opening, but when I arrived at seven forty-five, nearly a hundred people, mostly European tour groups, were already queued at the ticket booth. I would strongly recommend booking online in advance; the on-site line can easily run forty minutes or more, and during peak seasons — March through May, September through November — same-day tickets sometimes sell out entirely. I ended up buying at the gate: a combined garden-and-museum ticket cost 195 dirhams, roughly twenty US dollars; the garden alone is 150.

Once inside, the famous Majorelle Blue was even more saturated than in photographs — a cobalt shade with a purplish undertone, painted across the villa facade, plant pots, and railings, set against dense stands of cactus, bamboo, and bougainvillea. The garden was built in the 1920s and 30s by French painter Jacques Majorelle, who formulated and patented this exact blue himself. Left to decay through the 1980s, it was later bought and restored by Yves Saint Laurent and his partner Pierre Bergé. Walking the full garden loop took about forty-five minutes. From there I followed a path to the Musée Yves Saint Laurent, a building that is itself a work of art — designed by the Paris firm Studio KO, its facade built from terracotta brick arranged in a pattern that echoes traditional Moroccan weaving. Sunlight filters through the gaps between bricks, scattering fragments of light across the interior walls, translating the medina's own logic of light and shadow into a contemporary architectural vocabulary. Inside, the permanent collection displays Saint Laurent's original sketches alongside an entire wall dedicated to his haute couture collections drawn from Moroccan color and pattern, including his own handwritten line: 'Morocco taught me color.' Between the queue, the garden, and the museum, the whole visit took me about two and a half hours. I'd suggest budgeting at least two hours and leaving before noon, when the afternoon tour buses arrive in force.

Sidi Ghanem: A New Wave of Design in the Industrial Quarter

If Jardin Majorelle represents the romance of an older era, Sidi Ghanem is where Marrakech's design scene lives in the present tense. Once a cluster of warehouses and small factories, the district has, over roughly the past decade, seen local and expatriate designers move in and convert concrete sheds into studios and showrooms. I took a taxi from Gueliz, about fifteen minutes, for 40 dirhams. The street itself looks unremarkable at first glance — dusty, industrial — but behind a handful of steel shutters painted indigo or ochre lies an entirely different world.

The first stop I made was LRNCE, a ceramics studio run by Laetitia Lecomte, a French designer who has lived in Morocco for years. Her pieces keep the irregular, hand-thrown edges of traditional pottery but glaze them in a restrained palette of oat white, slate blue, and ochre — a completely different visual language from the vividly painted ceramics common in the souks, proof that tradition and contemporary design need not oppose each other here; they simply sit side by side. A medium soup bowl runs about 450 dirhams, not cheap, but the weight in your hand and the rough edge under your fingers explain why concept stores in Paris and Tokyo compete to stock the brand. A few doors down, MCC concept store is filled with round leather poufs made using traditional Moroccan tanning techniques, in shades from mustard to deep brick, naturally dyed with pomegranate skin and saffron according to the owner — priced between 800 and 1,200 dirhams each. Along this same street, the warehouse walls occasionally break into graffiti by young local artists, Berber script mixed with spray-paint lettering, creating an odd, compelling dialogue with the thousand-year-old geometric tilework across town. It left me convinced that Marrakech's design instinct has never actually stopped — it has simply kept growing in new materials.

Tradition and the contemporary don't compete here; they sit side by side — much like this city, which seems to live in several centuries at once.

Flavors at the Edge of the Square: The Daily Ritual of Harira and Mint Tea

By half past five in the evening I was back at Jemaa el-Fnaa, watching vendors wheel out their numbered food carts. Smoke rose from the grills, thick with cumin, saffron, and charred meat, filling the whole square. I sat down at stall number 14, where the owner, Hassan, shouted something to his neighbor in Arabic before turning to ask me, in decent French, what I wanted. I ordered a bowl of harira for 15 dirhams — a thick soup of tomato, lentils, chickpeas, and fine noodles. The first taste was tomato-sharp, followed by the warmth of cumin and turmeric unfolding at the back of the throat, finished with a bright hit of chopped coriander. This is traditionally the first bite eaten to break the fast during Ramadan; these days it's simply the most common late-afternoon snack in the square. Next door I worked up the courage to try snail soup, babbouche, for 5 dirhams — the broth simmered with over a dozen spices, carrying notes of star anise and licorice. The snails themselves barely taste of anything; what you're really eating is the layered aroma of that broth.

After the soup, I walked to Chez Chegrouni, a modest tagine spot in a side alley off the square that had come recommended more than any other. I ordered lamb tagine with dates and almonds for 60 dirhams, about seven US dollars. When the clay lid came off, steam carrying cinnamon and honey rolled out across the table. The lamb fell apart at the touch of a fork; the sweetness of the dates balanced the richness of the meat, and beneath it all sat a bed of onions soaked in sauce, perfect for mopping up with a round of fresh khobz bread — crisp outside, soft within. There was no air conditioning, just a ceiling fan clattering overhead, and at the next table a taxi driver argued cheerfully with the owner over a football match on TV. Somehow the noise and the smell of grill smoke made the meal feel more honest. Afterward came mint tea, atay, poured with the pot held theatrically high so the stream arced into the glass — a technique meant to aerate the tea and raise a light foam on top. It came free, a gesture of hospitality, absurdly sweet, and yet it paired unexpectedly well with the salty richness of the tagine still lingering on my tongue.

Silent Conversations in the Hammam, and Sunset Over the Square

The next afternoon I visited Hammam de la Rose, a traditional bathhouse in the old town, to experience the weekly cleansing ritual so many locals still observe. Public hammams in Morocco typically separate men's and women's hours; I went during the women's session, paying 250 dirhams for the full black-soap scrub treatment. Wrapped in a thin cotton towel, I stepped into the steam room, where there was almost no conversation — only the echo of water splashing against tile and the occasional drip from a distant tap. The attendant coated my skin in savon noir, a thick, oily black soap made from olive oil and ground olive pulp, letting it sit for ten minutes to soften the dead skin, then scrubbed my entire body with a coarse woven kessa mitt. The amount of skin that came off surprised me, but afterward my skin felt startlingly smooth. She barely spoke throughout, guiding me with gestures — turn, lift your arm — and there was a practiced tenderness in that silence. It made me think of the hammam's older role: in an era before running water at home, it was where women gathered weekly to trade family news and discuss marriages. Even now that most households have private bathrooms, many locals still keep the habit of a weekly hammam visit, not really for the washing, but for that particular sense of being cared for.

I left the hammam feeling weightless, as if I'd shed a layer of skin along with everything else, and wandered back to Jemaa el-Fnaa just as the sun tilted low against the Koutoubia minaret, turning its ochre stone almost gold. Storytellers, snake charmers, and henna painters were packing up for the day as rows of food stalls lit their string lights, the smell of honeyed nuts mixing with charcoal-grilled lamb. A leather goods seller named Youssef, noticing me fumbling with my camera, greeted me in a few careful words of Mandarin he'd picked up over the years serving Chinese tourists, then laughed and corrected my camera angle, telling me to crouch lower to fit both the minaret and the crowd into one frame. In that small moment, I understood that the city's most compelling design might not live in tilework or architecture at all, but in this kind of unguarded, easily struck-up human exchange. Marrakech has carved its sense of beauty into its walls, and just as much into the ordinary rhythm of a passing greeting. I looked back once more before leaving the square, its lights coming up one by one, like a play this red city stages anew every single night.
