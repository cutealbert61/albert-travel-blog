中文

清晨四點五十分,鬧鐘在七条一間老町家改建的民宿裡震動。榻榻米的藺草味還帶著昨夜的濕氣,窗外一片墨藍,連烏鴉都還沒開始叫。我在京都待的這第三趟旅程,終於下定決心不再跟著網路上「必去景點」的隊伍走——我想知道,當觀光巴士還沒發動引擎的時候,這座城市長什麼樣子。

清晨五點半:伏見稻荷,狐狸與霧氣獨享的時刻

五點二十分搭上第一班從東福寺開往稻荷的普通車,車廂裡只有兩個要去趕早班的上班族,和我。走出稻荷驛,天色仍是灰藍,千本鳥居入口的朱紅色在薄霧裡顯得幾乎褪成暗棕。白天這裡排隊拍照要等上十幾分鐘才能等到一個「沒人入鏡」的空檔,但這個時間,整條鳥居隧道只有我一個人的腳步聲,木頭地板受潮後有點滑,鞋底踩在上面發出悶悶的回音。

我沿著參道往上爬,大約二十分鐘後經過一間叫「眼力社」的小小分社,傳說是保佑眼力與先見之明的神社,社務所前擺著幾隻瞇眼微笑的狐狸石像,身上還留著昨夜露水。再往上到「四つ辻」——半山腰那個視野突然開闊、可以俯瞰京都盆地的休息平台——這裡有兩間清晨還沒開門的茶屋にしむら亭,竹簾都還捲著。一位負責清掃社域的老先生正拿著竹掃把,一下一下掃著石階上的落葉,見我喘著氣爬上來,只是點了點頭,說了句「おはようさん,涼しいうちに登った方がええで」(趁涼快的時候爬上來比較好),然後繼續掃他的地。整座山除了鳥叫、掃帚聲跟我的呼吸聲,什麼都沒有。

山霧還沒散去的時候,狐狸石像比任何觀光客都先醒過來。

貴船・鞍馬:一條連地圖都懶得標示清楚的山徑

七點半我下山回到稻荷驛,搭JR回京都站,再轉京阪本線到出町柳,接著換乘叡山電鐵——這是我覺得京都最容易被忽略的一段小旅程。叡電車廂是深綠色的木質內裝,長椅面對車窗,途中會經過「もみじのトンネル」楓葉隧道路段,列車還會特地減速讓乘客拍照,只是這天是初夏,兩旁全是濃得發亮的新綠。車廂裡坐著幾個要去鞍馬中學上課的學生,書包壓在膝蓋上打瞌睡,票價從出町柳到鞍馬單程420日圓,車程約30分鐘。

九點抵達鞍馬驛,一走出站就能看到那尊巨大的天狗面具。從這裡開始爬鞍馬寺的參道,一路是之字形的石階,兩旁杉木高聳遮天,空氣裡有股濕潤的木頭與苔蘚味。過了由岐神社後,真正的重頭戲才開始——一條翻越鞍馬山、直通貴船的登山道,官方地圖上畫得輕描淡寫,好像散步就能走完,實際上是連續近九十分鐘、高低落差頗大的碎石與樹根路,雨後特別滑。我在半山腰遇到一位拄著登山杖、看起來七十來歲的婆婆,她見我踩得小心翼翼,主動提醒我:「そこの岩、苔で滑るから右側通ったほうがええよ」(那塊石頭有青苔會滑,走右邊比較好),還說她每個禮拜都會來走這條路運動,「比去健身房便宜多了」。翻過山頂奧之院魔王殿後開始下坡,約十一點抵達貴船神社的朱紅色石階前,兩旁一整排燈籠沿階而下,是我在京都看過最上鏡卻幾乎沒有人排隊拍照的畫面。

下山後貴船川沿岸從五月到九月會架起「川床」納涼床,我選了一間叫「ひろ文」的老店,坐在懸空搭在溪水上方的木棧板座位,點了招牌流水麵線(流しそうめん),竹製滑道用溪水引流,一份1300日圓,老闆娘會提前在滑道上端放入麵線,冰涼的麵線順著竹管咻地滑下來,得眼明手快用筷子夾住,配著溪水的水聲、蟬鳴跟腳下透涼的水氣,那種涼快感是在市區任何一間冷氣房都給不出來的。從民宿出發到吃完這頓午餐,前後大約六個小時,交通花費含電車與川床用餐總共約2200日圓——比起在清水寺人擠人排隊買抹茶霜淇淋,這條路線值得多花的每一分鐘。

出町柳,在地廚房與早餐麻糬

下午一點半搭電車回到出町柳,這一帶其實是京都人真正的生活場景。從車站步行五分鐘就是出町桝形商店街,拱廊下豆腐店的蒸氣還沒散,魚販扯開嗓子喊著今天到貨的新鮮鯖魚,隔壁乾貨行老闆蹲在店門口整理昆布,完全沒有為觀光客特別調整的腳步。商店街中段那間總是排著長長人龍的「出町ふたば」,是京都人從小吃到大的名代豆餅——外皮是撒了鹽漬紅豌豆的粉紅麻糬,包著顆粒分明的紅豆餡,一顆200日圓。排隊時間平日大約十五分鐘,週末可能拉長到四十分鐘,建議挑平日上午去。

咬下去的瞬間,麻糬的黏韌先在齒間拉出一條細細的絲,接著豌豆的鹹味跟紅豆餡的甜在舌尖交錯,溫度還帶著剛蒸好的餘熱,不會過甜,吃完第一顆會忍不住想再買第二盒帶走。店裡沒有內用座位,我抱著紙袋走到旁邊鴨川與高野川交會處那片著名的「烏龜跳石」——鴨川デルタ,找了塊石頭坐下,看幾個放學的小學生手牽手跳過烏龜、鴿子形狀的石墩過河,老鷹在頭頂盤旋,河風帶著青草跟水的味道吹過來,那一刻覺得比任何米其林餐廳都更接近京都的日常滋味。

一乘寺深巷:舊書店、苔庭與地下咖啡館

下午三點,我搭叡電到一乘寺驛,這一帶觀光客通常只為了拉麵激戰區而來,卻很少人知道往山邊走十分鐘,就是詩仙堂——一座江戸初期文人石川丈山隱居造的庭園,平日下午幾乎沒有旅行團,只有偶爾滴答作響的添水(ししおどし)敲擊聲劃破寂靜,青苔庭院被午後陽光切出深淺不一的綠。我坐在緣側發呆了快半小時,一位館方老先生輕聲提醒拖鞋放整齊,除此之外沒有任何人打擾。

離開詩仙堂,我拐進巷子裡的恵文社一乘寺店,這間獨立書店在文青圈子裡頗有名氣,木質書架上混雜著攝影集、獨立雜誌與絕版詩集,店內飄著舊紙張特有的乾燥氣味。店員是個戴眼鏡的年輕人,見我猶豫著挑書,主動推薦了一本用京都方言寫的散文集,說「這本很少觀光客會買,但住這邊的人幾乎人手一本」。傍晚六點,我搭車回到河原町三条,鑽進地下的六曜社珈琲店——這間從1950年代開到現在的老店,店內光線昏黃,黑膠爵士樂低低流動,吧檯後方白髮的老闆娘手沖著虹吸壺咖啡,一杯600日圓,配一份現炸的手工甜甜圈,外皮酥脆內裡鬆軟帶著淡淡肉桂香。附近上班族進來點一杯咖啡坐半小時就走,沒有人滑手機拍照打卡,彷彿時間在這個地下室特別走得慢。

傍晚巷弄裡的地藏盆:京都人的夏日約定

晚上七點,我沿著新町通閒晃回旅館,拐進一條窄巷時,發現路口一尊平時毫不起眼的地藏石像前,竟掛滿了紅白相間的燈籠,幾張矮桌拼在一起,鋪著紅色氈布,幾個穿著浴衣的小孩蹲在地上玩撈金魚,幾位婆婆媽媽正忙著把煮好的紅豆年糕分裝到小碗裡。我站在巷口張望,一位婆婆察覺我好奇的眼神,笑著招手要我過去,遞給我一片米菓,說「今天是這個町内的地蔵盆,你要不要也坐一下」。

她一邊招呼我一邊解釋,地蔵盆是京都獨有的社區傳統,每年八月各個「町内会」(街坊自治會)都會在自己巷弄的地藏尊前搭起小型祭壇,感謝這尊守護孩童的地藏菩薩一整年的庇佑,同時也是難得讓整條巷子的鄰居聚在一起的日子。孩子們玩套圈圈、抽獎遊戲,大人則坐在矮凳上喝麥茶聊天。最讓我印象深刻的,是儀式進行到一半,大家圍成一圈,傳遞一串比人還高的巨大念珠——這叫「百萬遍」,念珠一邊繞圈一邊隨著誦經節奏「喀啦喀啦」地敲擊传遞,老老少少的手依序接過那串沉甸甸的木珠,連我這個外來的旅人也被塞了一段珠子傳下去,粗糙木珠摩擦掌心的觸感、和著孩子們的嬉笑聲,讓我第一次真切感覺到自己不只是路過這座城市,而是被短暫收進了它的生活紋理裡。

京都最動人的風景,從來不在鏡頭裡,而在一串傳到你手裡的念珠上。

那天深夜走回民宿,巷子裡的燈籠光還在身後搖晃,我忽然明白,這趟旅程真正的收穫不是集滿了幾個景點,而是那些原本不屬於我、卻被慷慨分享出來的片刻——一位掃地老先生的叮嚀、一串傳遞的念珠、一顆還溫熱的豆餅。京都的秘境,從來不是藏在某條隱密的巷弄裡,而是藏在你願不願意在清晨五點半,先於這座城市醒來的決心裡。

ENGLISH

My alarm buzzed at 4:50am inside a converted machiya guesthouse near Shichijo. The tatami still smelled faintly of last night's humidity, the window showed nothing but ink-blue dark, and even the crows hadn't started their racket yet. This was my third trip to Kyoto, and I'd finally decided to stop following the queues from travel blogs. I wanted to know what this city looked like before the tour buses turned their engines on.

5:30am: Fushimi Inari, When Only the Fox Statues Are Awake

I caught the first local train from Tofukuji to Inari at 5:20am, sharing the carriage with only two commuters and myself. Stepping out of Inari Station, the sky was still grey-blue, and the vermillion of the thousand torii gates looked almost brown in the mist. By daytime this tunnel of gates has a ten-minute wait just to get an empty photo, but at this hour I had the entire corridor to myself, my footsteps echoing dully off the damp, slightly slick wooden planks.

About twenty minutes up the path I passed Ganriki-sha, a small sub-shrine said to bless eyesight and foresight, its fox statues wearing squint-eyed smiles and still beaded with dew. Further up, at the halfway rest point called Yotsutsuji, the view suddenly opens over the whole Kyoto basin. Two teahouses there, including Nishimuraya, hadn't even rolled up their bamboo blinds yet. An elderly man sweeping fallen leaves with a bamboo broom paused when he saw me huffing up the steps, nodded, and said, 'Better to climb while it's cool.' Then he went back to his sweeping. Aside from birdsong, his broom, and my own breathing, the whole mountain was silent.

Before the mountain mist even lifts, the stone foxes wake up before any tourist ever will.

Kurama and Kibune: A Trail the Map Barely Bothers to Draw

By 7:30am I was back down, catching the JR to Kyoto Station, then the Keihan line to Demachiyanagi, then transferring onto the Eizan Railway — a leg of the journey I think gets criminally overlooked. The Eizan cars have dark green wooden interiors and bench seating facing the windows; the train even slows deliberately through the famous 'maple tunnel' stretch, though in early summer it was all deep, glossy green instead of red. A few students bound for Kurama Junior High dozed with their bags on their knees. One-way fare from Demachiyanagi to Kurama runs ¥420, about 30 minutes.

I arrived at Kurama Station at 9am, greeted immediately by the giant tengu mask outside. From there the approach to Kurama-dera climbs in zigzagging stone steps under towering cedars, the air thick with the smell of wet wood and moss. Past Yuki Shrine, the real challenge begins: a mountain trail that crosses over Mount Kurama and down into Kibune, drawn on official maps as if it were a casual stroll, but actually a demanding ninety-minute-plus route over roots and loose gravel, treacherously slick after rain. Halfway up I met a woman in her seventies with a hiking pole who warned me, unprompted, 'That rock's mossy — better to pass on the right side.' She told me she walks this route every week for exercise, 'cheaper than a gym membership.' After cresting the summit near Okunoin Maoden, the descent brought me to the vermillion steps of Kifune Shrine around 11am, lined with lanterns on both sides — one of the most photogenic scenes I've found in Kyoto, with almost nobody there to photograph it.

From May through September, restaurants along the Kibune River build kawadoko platforms suspended right over the water. I chose one called Hirobun, sitting on a wooden deck above the stream and ordering nagashi somen — flowing noodles sent down a bamboo flume by river water, ¥1,300 a set. The staff drop noodles into the top of the chute and you have to catch them with chopsticks as they slide past, cold and slippery, while the sound of the river, cicadas, and the cool mist rising off the water do more for the body than any air-conditioned room in the city ever could. From guesthouse to finishing this lunch took about six hours door to door, with transport and the meal together costing roughly ¥2,200 — worth every minute compared to queuing for a matcha soft-serve outside Kiyomizu-dera.

Demachiyanagi: A Local Kitchen and a Warm Rice Cake for Breakfast

By 1:30pm I was back at Demachiyanagi, a neighborhood that feels like the real, unpolished engine room of Kyoto life. A five-minute walk from the station brings you to Demachi Masugata Shotengai, an arcade where tofu shops are still steaming, a fishmonger shouts about the morning's mackerel catch, and the dried-goods store owner squats in his doorway sorting kombu — none of it staged for visitors. Midway down the arcade sits Demachi Futaba, always with a line out front, famous for mame mochi: pink rice cakes studded with salted peas, wrapped around a dense, coarsely mashed red bean filling, ¥200 each. Weekday queues run about fifteen minutes; weekends can stretch to forty, so I'd recommend a weekday morning visit.

The moment I bit into one, the mochi stretched into a thin, sticky thread between my teeth before the salt of the peas met the sweetness of the bean paste on my tongue, still warm from steaming, never cloying — the kind of thing that makes you want to buy a second box before you've finished the first. There's no seating inside, so I carried my paper bag to the nearby Kamogawa Delta, where the Kamo and Takano rivers meet and stepping stones shaped like turtles and pigeons cross the shallows. I sat on one of the rocks and watched schoolkids hop hand-in-hand across the turtles, a kite circling overhead, the river breeze carrying the smell of green grass and water — a moment that felt closer to the real texture of Kyoto than any Michelin-starred dinner could.

Ichijoji's Back Streets: Old Bookshops, Moss Gardens, and a Basement Café

At 3pm I took the Eizan line to Ichijoji, a neighborhood most visitors only know for its ramen wars, unaware that a ten-minute walk toward the hills brings you to Shisen-do, a garden built by the Edo-period scholar Ishikawa Jozan as his retirement retreat. On a weekday afternoon there were no tour groups at all, just the occasional clack of a shishi-odori water deer-scarer breaking the silence, sunlight cutting the moss garden into deep and pale shades of green. I sat on the veranda doing nothing for nearly half an hour, the only interruption a soft reminder from staff to line up my slippers.

From there I ducked into a nearby alley to Keibunsha Ichijoji, an independent bookstore well known in certain circles, its wooden shelves stacked with photography books, indie zines, and out-of-print poetry collections, the whole shop smelling of dry, aging paper. A bespectacled clerk noticed me hesitating and recommended an essay collection written partly in Kyoto dialect, saying, 'Tourists almost never buy this one, but almost everyone who actually lives around here owns a copy.' By 6pm I was back at Kawaramachi-Sanjo, descending into Rokuyosha Coffee, a basement café running since the 1950s, dim lighting, low jazz on vinyl, a white-haired proprietress working a siphon brewer behind the counter. A cup runs ¥600, paired with a freshly fried house donut — crisp outside, soft inside, with a faint trace of cinnamon. Office workers drifted in, sat for half an hour with their coffee, and left without ever taking out their phones, as if time simply moved slower down there.

Jizo-bon in the Alley: A Kyoto Summer Ritual

By 7pm I was wandering back toward my guesthouse along Shinmachi-dori when I turned into a narrow lane and found a normally unremarkable roadside Jizo statue suddenly strung with red and white lanterns. A few low tables had been pushed together and covered in a red cloth; children in yukata crouched over a goldfish-scooping game while several older women ladled sweet red bean rice cakes into small bowls. I lingered at the mouth of the alley, curious, until one of the women caught my eye, waved me over, and pressed a rice cracker into my hand. 'Today's our neighborhood's Jizo-bon,' she said. 'Sit down for a bit.'

She explained, between handing out snacks, that Jizo-bon is a distinctly Kyoto neighborhood tradition: every August, each small chōnaikai — a self-governing block association — sets up a modest festival at its own street-corner Jizo statue to thank the guardian deity of children for a year of protection, and, just as importantly, to give the whole lane an excuse to gather. Kids play ring-toss and lottery games while adults sit on stools drinking cold barley tea. What struck me most came halfway through the ceremony, when everyone formed a circle to pass around an enormous prayer bead chain, taller than a person — a ritual called hyakumanben. The beads clattered together as they were passed hand to hand in time with a chanted sutra, and even I, a total outsider, was handed a length of the heavy wooden beads to pass along. The rough wood against my palm, mixed with the children's laughter, was the first moment on this whole trip I felt not like someone passing through the city, but briefly woven into its actual fabric.

Kyoto's most moving scenery was never meant for a camera — it's in a string of prayer beads passed into your own hands.

Walking back to the guesthouse late that night, the lantern light still swaying behind me in the alley, I realized the real reward of this trip was never the list of places checked off, but the moments I hadn't earned and was generously handed anyway — a caretaker's quiet warning, a chain of prayer beads passed hand to hand, a rice cake still warm from the steamer. Kyoto's hidden gems were never really hidden in some secret alley. They were hidden in whether or not you were willing to wake up before the city did, at 5:30 in the morning.
