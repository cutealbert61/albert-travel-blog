中文

清晨五點四十分,手機鬧鐘在漆黑中震動,窗外傳來喚拜聲,像一層薄霧滲進房間。我在麥地那(Medina)裡一間叫Riad El Zohar的老宅改建旅館醒來,天花板是手工雕刻的雪松木格柵,牆面貼滿彩色澤里桀(zellige)磁磚。昨晚入睡前我還在想,這座城市會不會只是明信片上的粉紅色樣板?七天後我才明白,馬拉喀什的美從不在濾鏡裡,而是刻在每一面牆、每一口染缸、每一雙沾滿皮革油脂的手掌上。這篇日記,我想用『設計』的眼睛,重新走一次這座城市。

晨光中的班尤瑟夫學院與巴西亞宮

六點半,麥地那的巷弄還沒被觀光客攻佔,我沿著石板路往前走,路面濕濕的——凌晨剛有人灑水降溫。班尤瑟夫古蘭經學院(Medersa Ben Youssef)七點開門,我是第一批入場的人,門票70迪拉姆(約新台幣220元)。踏進中庭那一刻我倒抽一口氣:陽光斜斜切過方形水池,四面牆從地面到天花板疊了三層工藝——下段雪花石膏浮雕、中段彩色馬賽克磁磚、上段雪松木鏤空雕刻,寫滿庫法體古蘭經文。館內工作人員告訴我,這種由硬至軟的堆疊邏輯,象徵物質世界從石頭走向木頭,最後通向文字與信仰。我沒請導遊,只是坐在池邊石階上待了將近四十分鐘,看光線隨太陽升高從東牆爬到水面,水面又把光反彈到雕花天花板上,整個空間像一台安靜運轉的日晷。

離開學院,我步行十二分鐘到巴西亞宮(Bahia Palace),途中經過一間賣傳統氈帽(chéchia)的小舖,老闆正用駱駝毛氈製帽子,抬頭跟我說了聲法語阿拉伯語混雜的早安——這是我在馬拉喀什聽到最多次的招呼。巴西亞宮建於19世紀末,是大維齊爾巴阿邁德為妻妾所建的宅邸,院落一間接一間,每個房間木門的顏色與雕花圖案都不同,這是刻意設計:妻妾等級不同,裝飾等級也不同。走在裡頭我特別留意天花板,幾乎每間都用不同配色的幾何圖騰,館方說光是這座宮殿就聘請了當時全摩洛哥最頂尖的匠人,耗時十四年才完工。門票70迪拉姆,建議九點前抵達,人潮會比中午少很多。

馬拉喀什的建築不是用來「看」的,是用來「讀」的——每一道牆都是一段被雕刻出來的經文。

藍色庭園裡的摩登摩洛哥

上午十點半,我搭小黃前往Gueliz新城區的馬約爾花園(Jardin Majorelle),車費25迪拉姆,上車前先講好價錢是這裡的默契。花園門票150迪拉姆(博物館另計70),排隊約二十分鐘。一進大門,那抹被稱為「馬約爾藍」的鈷藍色建築,配上飽和芥末黃邊框與盆栽,鮮豔到近乎不真實。這座花園是1930年代法國畫家賈克・馬約爾親手設計,後來由設計師Yves Saint Laurent與伴侶Pierre Bergé買下修復。走在竹林與仙人掌步道間,我聽見水流從摩爾式噴泉汩汩流出,空氣裡混著曬熱的仙人掌青草味與遠處茉莉花香。園區深處的柏柏爾博物館收藏了柏柏爾人的珠寶、地毯與服飾,色彩邏輯跟花園本身互相呼應——原來馬約爾藍其實源自摩洛哥傳統建築常用的深藍色調,只是被放大成整棟建築的主色。

隔壁的Yves Saint Laurent美術館2017年開幕,建築本身就是一件作品:清水磚牆疊出類似編織紋理的立面,呼應摩洛哥傳統紡織圖騰。館內常設展陳列聖羅蘭從1962到2002年間受摩洛哥啟發的手稿與服裝,其中一件柏柏爾風格刺繡外套,展櫃燈光刻意調暗,讓布料金屬絲線在幽暗中發亮,像重現他當年在麥地那市集初見手工刺繡時的驚豔感。我在美術館咖啡廳點了一杯薄荷茶(35迪拉姆),坐在露台看著花園另一頭遊客的剪影,忽然覺得馬拉喀什最迷人之處,是傳統工藝從沒被封存進博物館,而是持續被當代設計師重新翻譯、繼續活著。

手作巷弄:染坊與皮革匠人的日常

中午十二點半,我離開新城搭小黃回舊城,司機收我20迪拉姆,說這個時間走小路比較快,果然七分鐘就在Bab Doukkala附近下車。我的目標是麥地那北側的染坊市集(Souk Sebbaghine)與更北邊Bab Debbagh一帶的皮革染坊區。走進巷子越走越窄,兩旁攤位掛滿剛染好還在滴水的羊毛線——洋紅、藏青、番紅花黃,像一整排濕潤的彩虹垂在頭頂。巷口就聞到金屬與植物混合的氣味,後來才知道那是石榴皮、番紅花與靛藍粉混合染料的味道。一位染坊師傅看我拿著相機張望,主動招手要我進去看他攪動染缸,他整條手臂泛著靛藍色,笑著用破英文說「這是我今天第五種顏色」。我沒被要求付費拍照,但離開前他指了指小費箱,我放了10迪拉姆。

從染坊繼續往北走約十五分鐘,巷弄越來越窄、幾乎只容兩人並肩,空氣中的氣味開始轉變——先是輕微的動物皮革腥味,越走越濃,接近皮革染坊時已經濃到嗆鼻,那是石灰、鴿糞與鞣製藥劑混合的氣味。這裡幾乎每個入口都有人主動遞上薄荷葉要我摀鼻,說可以帶我上頂樓陽台拍照,喊價從50到100迪拉姆不等。我選了在工坊工作的年輕人Karim帶路,付他60迪拉姆,爬上三層樓高、沒有扶手的水泥階梯,站上陽台俯瞰整片圓形染缸,像蜂巢一樣一格一格排開,有的浸著白色石灰水泡皮,有的染成焦糖色、深棕色,幾個赤腳站在及膝染缸裡踩踏皮革的工人,身影在正午陽光下幾乎被熱氣蒸出重影。Karim告訴我,這門手藝在他們家已經傳了四代,鞣皮方法幾乎和幾百年前一樣沒變,唯一改變的是現在多了觀光客這門副業。整趟行程大約花了兩個半小時,建議正午前後前往光線最好,但要有心理準備氣味不好聞,最好自備口罩或薄荷精油。

顏色在這裡不是裝飾,是一整個家族世代相傳的體力活與呼吸的代價。

德吉瑪廣場的煙火氣

傍晚六點半,天色從橘紅轉成深藍,我走進德吉瑪廣場(Jemaa el-Fna),白天賣果汁與耍蛇人的攤位已收起,取而代之的是上百輛冒著白煙的推車一字排開,現場瀰漫孜然、炭烤羊油與薄荷茶甜膩香氣混合的味道,喇叭裡放著Gnawa音樂,鼓聲低沉。我選了編號14號、招牌手寫著「Chez Aicha」的攤位坐下,老闆直接遞來一碗蝸牛湯(babbouche),8迪拉姆一碗,湯頭用茴香、丁香與十幾種香料熬成,喝起來帶著淡淡藥草苦甘,蝸牛肉Q彈帶嚼勁,我用附的牙籤挑出肉沾湯汁吃,老闆笑說本地人相信這湯能治胃痛跟感冒。接著我點了一份哈里拉湯(harira,15迪拉姆)——番茄與扁豆熬得濃稠,撒了香菜與一點檸檬汁,是齋戒月裡摩洛哥人開齋必喝的湯,喝下去整個人從胃裡暖起來。

主食我點了炭烤羊肉串(brochettes,25迪拉姆一份約六串),肉塊帶著明顯孜然與紅椒粉醃漬痕跡,外層微焦帶脆,咬下去油脂香氣直接衝出來,配現烤圓餅(khobz)撕開沾湯汁。飯後我走到廣場邊一攤專賣國王煎餅(msemen)的小攤,老闆現場煎千層薄餅,刷上厚厚一層奶油再淋蜂蜜,一份10迪拉姆,外酥內軟、甜而不膩,是我整趟旅程吃過最療癒的甜點。整頓晚餐加起來大概花了70迪拉姆(約新台幣220元),坐在塑膠椅上,身邊全是講阿拉伯語法語的本地家庭與觀光客混雜,耳邊鼓聲、叫賣聲、蒸氣噴發聲交織,我一邊喝薄荷茶,一邊看著廣場中央一群人圍著說書人聽故事,忽然理解為什麼德吉瑪廣場會被列為人類非物質文化遺產——因為這裡的美食從來不只是食物,而是整套感官劇場。

哈曼、週五庫斯庫斯與城市的節奏

第五天是星期五,我特地起了個大早,想觀察這座城市的「星期五節奏」跟平常有什麼不同。我住的Riad女主人Fatima一早就開始準備庫斯庫斯(couscous),她說星期五中午吃庫斯庫斯是摩洛哥幾乎全國一致的家庭傳統,因為星期五是主麻日(Jumu'ah),男人們中午要去清真寺做聚禮,回來後全家圍坐吃一頓豐盛午餐,通常是庫斯庫斯配七種蔬菜與燉羊肉,象徵完整與豐盛。Fatima邀我一起在廚房幫忙,教我用手掌搓細粗粒麥粉讓它均勻蒸熟——這個動作她說自己從八歲就跟母親學,重複了三十年。中午十二點半,清真寺喚拜聲又響起,巷子裡所有男人幾乎同時放下手邊工作,穿著白色或淺灰色傑拉巴長袍(djellaba)往清真寺走去,那畫面安靜又壯觀,一整條巷子在五分鐘內幾乎淨空。

下午三點,我跟著Fatima的女兒去了她們常去的哈曼澡堂(Hammam Mouassine),不是觀光客常去的高檔spa版本,而是社區居民日常使用的公共哈曼,入場費只要10迪拉姆,自己帶黑肥皂(savon noir)跟去角質手套(kessa)。裡頭沒有精油蠟燭,只有蒸氣、大理石地板跟此起彼落的水桶潑水聲,女性們三五成群互相刷背聊天,完全不遮掩地自在相處,那種坦然一開始讓我有點不知所措,後來反而覺得放鬆——原來哈曼從來不是奢華享受,而是每週例行的社區社交場合。離開前Fatima的女兒半開玩笑提醒我,進清真寺、進哈曼、甚至穿短褲走進舊城某些老街區,都可能引來側目或被拒絕入內,摩洛哥人對身體暴露的界線,跟我原本以為的「北非=開放」印象很不一樣,反而更講究場合分寸。

當晚我沒能親眼碰上婚禮,卻在旅館頂樓聽見遠處傳來一整晚的鼓聲與女聲高音顫音(zaghareet),Fatima笑著解釋,那是附近人家在辦訂婚儀式(khotba),新娘會被朋友們用henna在手上畫上繁複圖騰,儀式常持續到凌晨,鼓聲跟顫音是用來昭告整條街這家人有喜事。躺在天台躺椅上,望著被輕微光害染成暗紫色的夜空,遠方宣禮塔的燈光靜靜亮著,我想,這座城市的美學從來不是設計給觀光客看的展示品,而是活生生長在市井生活裡的日常紋理。

馬拉喀什教會我的最後一件事是:最動人的設計,往往不在博物館裡,而在一鍋星期五的庫斯庫斯裡。

離開的那個早晨,計程車駛過城牆缺口時,我回頭望了一眼那片被晨光染成赭紅色的土牆,忽然明白自己帶不走的東西,遠比行李箱裡的地毯與陶盤還要多——是喚拜聲裡的節奏感,是染坊師傅泛藍的雙手,是Fatima廚房裡搓麥粉的手勢。這些才是馬拉喀什真正的設計語言。

ENGLISH

My alarm buzzed at 5:40am in pitch darkness, and through the window drifted the call to prayer, seeping into the room like a thin mist. I woke inside Riad El Zohar, an old Medina house turned guesthouse, its ceiling a hand-carved cedar lattice, its walls tiled in colorful zellige mosaics. The night before, I'd wondered whether this city was just a postcard cliché of pink walls. Seven days later I understood: Marrakech's beauty isn't filtered, it's carved into every wall, steeped in every dye vat, worked into every leather-stained palm. This is my attempt to walk the city again through the eyes of design.

Morning Light Inside Ben Youssef Madrasa and Bahia Palace

At 6:30am the Medina's alleys hadn't yet been claimed by tour groups. I walked over damp cobblestones, still cool from an early water-down. The Ben Youssef Madrasa opens at 7am and I was among the first through the door, paying 70 dirhams (roughly USD 7). Stepping into the courtyard, I actually gasped: sunlight sliced diagonally across the rectangular reflecting pool, and the walls rose in three distinct layers of craft — carved alabaster at the base, colored mosaic tilework in the middle, and openwork cedar carving above, inscribed with Kufic Quranic script. A staff member told me this hierarchy, from stone to wood, symbolizes the material world ascending toward text and faith. I skipped the guided tour and simply sat on the pool's stone steps for nearly forty minutes, watching light climb the eastern wall as the sun rose, bounce off the water, and scatter across the carved ceiling — the whole courtyard functioning like a slow, silent sundial.

Leaving the madrasa, I walked twelve minutes to Bahia Palace, passing a tiny shop where a man was felting traditional chéchia caps from camel wool; he greeted me with a French-Arabic 'bonjour' — the most common greeting I heard the entire trip. Built in the late 19th century for the vizier Bou Ahmed's wives and concubines, the palace unfolds courtyard after courtyard, each door painted and carved differently by design: rank determined decoration. Wandering through, I kept looking up — nearly every ceiling used a different palette of geometric motifs. Staff told me the palace employed Morocco's finest craftsmen of the era and took fourteen years to complete. Entry is 70 dirhams; arriving before 9am means avoiding the midday crowds almost entirely.

Marrakech's architecture isn't meant to be looked at — it's meant to be read. Every wall is a verse carved into permanence.

Modern Morocco Inside a Blue Garden

At 10:30am I took a petit taxi to Jardin Majorelle in the newer Gueliz district, agreeing on 25 dirhams before getting in, since meters are rarely used here. Garden entry was 150 dirhams, with the adjoining museum an extra 70; the queue took about twenty minutes. Walking through the gate, the cobalt structure known as Majorelle Blue, trimmed in saturated mustard yellow and framed by potted plants, looked almost too vivid to be real. French painter Jacques Majorelle designed the garden himself in the 1930s; decades later, designer Yves Saint Laurent and his partner Pierre Bergé bought and restored it. Walking through bamboo groves and cactus-lined paths, I heard water trickling from Moorish fountains, the air thick with sun-warmed cactus and distant jasmine. The Berbère Museum tucked inside the garden displays Berber jewelry, carpets, and costume, its color logic echoing the garden itself — I learned Majorelle Blue actually derives from a deep indigo long used in traditional Moroccan architecture, simply scaled up to cover an entire building.

Next door, the Yves Saint Laurent Museum, opened in 2017, is a piece of design in its own right: raw brick façades woven into a texture that recalls traditional Moroccan textiles. The permanent collection traces Saint Laurent's Morocco-inspired sketches and garments from 1962 to 2002, including a Berber-style embroidered jacket lit so dimly that its metallic threads seem to glow in the dark — as if recreating the designer's own first encounter with hand embroidery in the Medina souks. I ordered mint tea (35 dirhams) at the museum café and sat on the terrace watching visitors' silhouettes move through the garden beyond. It struck me then that what makes Marrakech so compelling is that its traditional crafts were never sealed away in museums — they keep being translated, reinterpreted, kept alive by contemporary designers.

Back Alleys of Craft: A Day With the Dyers and Tanners

At 12:30pm I taxied back into the old city for 20 dirhams; the driver said cutting through side streets would beat the traffic, and sure enough we arrived near Bab Doukkala in seven minutes. My destination was the dyers' souk (Souk Sebbaghine) on the Medina's north side, and further on, the tannery district near Bab Debbagh. The alleys narrowed with every step, hung overhead with freshly dyed wool still dripping — magenta, indigo, saffron yellow — like a row of wet rainbows suspended above my head. Even at the entrance I caught a metallic, herbal scent, later explained as a mix of pomegranate rind, saffron, and indigo powder. A dyer noticed me hovering with my camera and waved me over to watch him stir a vat, his entire forearm stained blue. 'This is my fifth color today,' he laughed in broken English. No one asked for payment to photograph, but on my way out he nodded toward a small tip box, and I dropped in 10 dirhams.

Walking another fifteen minutes north, the alleys grew so narrow that two people could barely pass side by side, and the smell shifted — a faint animal-hide odor at first, thickening the closer I got to the tanneries, until it became genuinely pungent: lime, pigeon droppings, and tanning chemicals. Nearly every doorway had someone offering a sprig of mint to hold under my nose, promising a rooftop view for anywhere between 50 and 100 dirhams. I went with a young tannery worker named Karim, paid him 60 dirhams, and climbed a three-story concrete staircase with no handrail to a terrace overlooking dozens of round dye pits arranged like a honeycomb — some filled with white lime water for soaking hides, others dyed caramel or deep brown, with barefoot workers standing knee-deep, treading leather, their figures almost shimmering in the midday heat. Karim told me his family had worked this trade for four generations, and the tanning method has barely changed in centuries — the only new addition is tourists like me as a side income. The whole excursion took about two and a half hours; midday offers the best light for photos from the terraces, but come prepared for the smell — a face mask or a dab of mint oil helps enormously.

Color here isn't decoration. It's a family's inherited labor, paid for in held breath, generation after generation.

The Roaring Theater of Jemaa el-Fna

By 6:30pm the sky had shifted from orange to deep blue, and I walked into Jemaa el-Fna. The daytime juice stalls and snake charmers had packed up, replaced by hundreds of steaming food carts lined up in rows, the air thick with cumin, charred lamb fat, and sweet mint tea, Gnawa music thumping low from unseen speakers. I sat at stall No. 14, hand-painted 'Chez Aicha,' where the owner immediately set down a bowl of snail soup (babbouche) for 8 dirhams — a broth simmered with fennel, cloves, and a dozen other spices, faintly bitter and herbal, the snails chewy and satisfying. I used the provided toothpick to fish them out and dip them back into the broth; the owner insisted locals swear this soup cures stomachaches and colds. Next came a bowl of harira (15 dirhams) — a thick tomato-lentil soup finished with cilantro and a squeeze of lemon, the same soup Moroccans traditionally break their Ramadan fast with, warming me from the inside out.

For the main course I ordered lamb brochettes (25 dirhams for about six skewers), the meat clearly marinated in cumin and paprika, charred at the edges, releasing a rush of fat and spice with the first bite, eaten with torn pieces of fresh khobz bread mopping up the juices. Afterward I wandered to a stall specializing in msemen, layered flatbread fried on a flat griddle, brushed thick with butter and drizzled in honey — 10 dirhams a piece, crisp outside, soft inside, sweet without being cloying, easily the most comforting dessert of the entire trip. The whole dinner cost about 70 dirhams (roughly USD 7), eaten on a plastic stool surrounded by a mix of French- and Arabic-speaking local families and tourists, drums and vendor calls and hissing steam braided together in the air. Sipping mint tea, I watched a crowd gather around a storyteller in the square's center and finally understood why Jemaa el-Fna earned UNESCO recognition as intangible cultural heritage — because the food here was never just food. It's an entire sensory theater.

Hammam, Friday Couscous, and the City's Real Rhythm

Day five was a Friday, and I woke early on purpose to observe how the city's rhythm shifts. My riad host Fatima was already preparing couscous at dawn. She explained that eating couscous every Friday at noon is an almost universal Moroccan family ritual, since Friday is Jumu'ah, the day men attend congregational prayers at midday; afterward the whole family gathers for a generous lunch, typically couscous with seven vegetables and stewed lamb, symbolizing abundance and completeness. Fatima invited me into her kitchen, teaching me to roll semolina between my palms so it steams evenly — a motion she said she's repeated since she was eight years old, learned from her mother. At 12:30pm the call to prayer rang out again, and almost in unison every man in the alley set down whatever he was doing and walked toward the mosque in white or pale grey djellabas. The scene was quiet and striking — the entire street emptied within five minutes.

At 3pm I followed Fatima's daughter to Hammam Mouassine, not the polished spa version tourists usually book, but the neighborhood's everyday public bathhouse. Entry was just 10 dirhams, and you bring your own black soap (savon noir) and exfoliating glove (kessa). There were no scented candles, just steam, marble floors, and the constant splash of buckets being poured, women in small groups scrubbing each other's backs and chatting, entirely unselfconscious about their bodies. That openness unsettled me at first, then relaxed me — the hammam was never meant to be luxury, but a weekly community ritual. On the way out, Fatima's daughter half-jokingly warned me that entering a mosque, a hammam, or even walking through certain old streets in shorts could draw disapproving looks or outright refusal — Morocco's boundaries around the body were far more specific than my vague assumption that 'North Africa equals relaxed.'

That night I never witnessed a wedding directly, but from the rooftop I heard drumming and the high, trilling zaghareet of women's voices carrying on for hours. Fatima laughed and explained a neighboring family was holding an engagement ceremony (khotba), the bride's hands hennaed with elaborate patterns by her friends, the celebration often lasting until dawn — the drums and trills a way of announcing to the whole street that this family had something to celebrate. Lying on a rooftop lounge chair, watching the sky dim to a faint purple under the city's light pollution, distant minaret lights glowing steadily, I realized this city's aesthetics were never designed for tourists to admire. They're woven, still living, into the ordinary texture of daily life.

The last thing Marrakech taught me: the most moving design is rarely found in a museum. Sometimes it's simply in a Friday pot of couscous.

On the morning I left, as the taxi passed through a gap in the old ramparts, I turned back for one last look at the ochre walls glowing under early light, and realized what I was bringing home had little to do with the carpets and ceramic plates in my suitcase. It was the rhythm of the call to prayer, the indigo-stained hands of a dyer, the motion of Fatima's palms rolling semolina in her kitchen. Those, I think, are Marrakech's true design language.
