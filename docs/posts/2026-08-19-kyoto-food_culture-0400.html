中文

清晨七點半的京都,四条通還沒被觀光客的行李箱輪子聲喚醒,我卻已經聞到高湯的味道從一條不起眼的拱廊裡飄出來。那是錦市場,京都人喊了四百年的「京の台所」——京都的灶咖。木屐踩在石板路上的聲音、店家掀開暖簾時布料摩擦的聲響、鐵板上煎蛋捲滋滋作響的油聲,交織成這座古都最誠實的晨間交響曲。觀光客總以為京都是金閣寺與嵐山竹林,但真正住在這裡的人告訴我,想認識京都,得先認識京都人怎麼吃飯。

錦市場:一條巷子裡的四百年

錦市場其實只是一條長約390公尺的拱廊街,從高倉通一路延伸到新京極通,盡頭是錦天満宮。但這條窄巷裡擠了一百三十多間店舖,每一間都在賣京都人餐桌上真正會出現的東西:麩、湯葉、漬物、乾貨、京野菜。我建議上午九點左右抵達,這時候觀光團還沒湧入,店家剛把貨架擺好,老闆娘會願意跟你多聊兩句。從高倉通口進去,第一站是三木鶏卵——他們的出汁巻き玉子(高湯煎蛋捲)一支350日圓左右,現點現煎,蛋捲切面濕潤得像果凍,咬下去先是柴魚高湯的鹹鮮,尾韻卻是淡淡的甜。再往前走,打田漬物的醃漬小黃瓜跟京都特有的酸莖菜(すぐき)可以試吃,店員會遞給你牙籤,一口咬下去脆得像雪碎裂的聲音。逛完全程大約需要一個半到兩小時,若每攤都吃一點,預算抓兩千到三千日圓剛好。要注意的是,2023年京都市議會通過了「邊走邊吃」的自律公約,大部分店家現在會要求你在攤位旁的小板凳吃完再走,這其實也是尊重巷弄裡其他行人與店家清潔的禮貌。

京都人吃飯,不是為了填飽肚子,而是為了記住季節走到哪裡了。

巷弄餐桌的溫度

離開錦市場往南走十分鐘,先斗町那條窄得只容兩人錯身的石板小巷才是京都人晚上真正去的地方。我第一次在這裡吃到「おばんざい」(家常小菜)是在一間叫「祇園 权兵衛」的小店,吧檯只有八個位子,老闆娘每天早上去市場買菜,菜單完全看當天進了什麼貨。那晚我吃到的是炊いたん(燉煮蔬菜)——高野豆腐吸飽了柴魚與昆布高湯,入口即化,旁邊配一小碟醃茄子跟一碗味噌湯,整套下來大約一千八百日圓,卻比任何一間米其林餐廳都讓我印象深刻。京都的湯葉(豆漿表面凝結的豆皮)也值得專程去嵐山吃一次,「西山艸堂」的湯豆腐御膳,一鍋咕嘟咕嘟冒著小泡的豆腐鍋端上桌時,蒸氣裡帶著淡淡黃豆香,沾一點特製的柚子醬油,豆腐綿密得幾乎沒有阻力地滑進喉嚨,套餐約三千五百日圓起,秋天坐在窗邊還能看見渡月橋染紅的楓葉。如果想吃點庶民味,回到市區的「麺屋猪一」京都拉麵不能錯過,湯頭是罕見的醬油與雞白湯雙拼,清爽卻回甘,一碗約一千日圓,深夜裡配一杯啤酒,是京都人下班後最放鬆的儀式。

一汁三菜與不時不食:京都人的餐桌哲學

要理解這些食物背後的邏輯,得先知道京都人常掛在嘴邊的兩句話:「一汁三菜」與「不時不食」。前者是江戶時代流傳下來的家常飲食結構——一碗湯、三道配菜、一碗白飯,簡單卻講究均衡;後者出自論語,意思是不吃不合時令的東西。京都四面環山,自古交通不便,只能就地取材,發展出對「旬」(當季)近乎信仰般的堅持——春天吃筍與鯛魚,夏天吃鱧魚(hamo)與冬瓜,秋天吃松茸,冬天則是整座城市飄著柚子與蕪菁燉煮的香氣。這種對季節的敬意也延伸到節慶裡:每年七月的祇園祭,四条通會封街擺滿屋台(路邊攤),賣著烤玉米、章魚燒與冰鎮桃子,京都人會特地穿上浴衣,一邊逛山鉾一邊吃著攤販食物,這是一年裡少數允許「邊走邊吃」被默許的日子。而二月的節分祭,人們會在八坂神社撒豆驅邪,回家後全家人依照自己的年齡數吃下等量的炒豆,祈求一年平安——這些儀式看似與美食無關,卻都圍繞著「吃」這個核心動作展開,彷彿京都人相信,只要好好吃飯,就是對這座城市、對這片土地最大的敬意。

離開京都前的最後一晚,我又繞回錦市場,這時店家大多已拉下鐵門,只剩下錦天満宮前幾盞燈籠還亮著。巷子裡飄著淡淡的柴魚與醬油氣味,像是這座城市留給夜晚的最後一口餘韻。我忽然明白,京都從來不是靠古蹟活著的城市,而是靠每天早上那鍋認真熬煮的高湯,一代一代傳下來的。

ENGLISH

It's 7:30 in the morning and Shijo Street hasn't yet woken to the rumble of tourist suitcases, but I can already smell dashi drifting out from an unassuming arcade nearby. This is Nishiki Market, what locals have called 'Kyoto's Kitchen' for four hundred years. The clack of wooden geta on stone paving, the rustle of noren curtains being lifted, the sizzle of tamagoyaki hitting a hot griddle — together they form the most honest morning symphony this old capital has to offer. Visitors imagine Kyoto as Kinkakuji and the bamboo groves of Arashiyama, but anyone who actually lives here will tell you: to understand this city, you first need to understand how its people eat.

Nishiki Market: Four Centuries in One Alley

Nishiki Market is really just a covered arcade, barely 390 meters long, running from Takakura-dori to Shinkyogoku-dori and ending at Nishiki Tenmangu Shrine. Yet more than 130 shops are packed into this narrow corridor, each selling something that genuinely ends up on a Kyoto dinner table — fu (wheat gluten), yuba, tsukemono pickles, dried goods, Kyoto vegetables. I'd suggest arriving around 9am, before the tour groups descend, when shopkeepers have just finished setting out their goods and are still willing to chat. Starting from the Takakura-dori entrance, my first stop is always Miki Keiran, where a skewer of dashi-infused tamagoyaki costs around 350 yen, grilled to order — the cross-section glistens like soft jelly, and the first bite delivers a savory bonito hit followed by a whisper of sweetness. Further along, Uchida Tsukemono lets you sample pickled cucumbers and Kyoto's signature sugukina (pickled turnip greens); bite down and you get a crunch like breaking thin ice. Budget about ninety minutes to two hours to walk the full length, and if you graze at every stall, two to three thousand yen is about right. One thing to note: since 2023, Kyoto's local council has asked shops to discourage 'eating while walking,' so most stalls now have a small bench where you're expected to finish before moving on — a small courtesy to the other pedestrians and to the shopkeepers who have to sweep the alley clean each night.

In Kyoto, eating was never just about hunger — it's how people keep track of where the season has gotten to.

The Warmth of a Back-Alley Table

Ten minutes south of Nishiki Market, Pontocho — an alley so narrow two people can barely pass each other — is where Kyoto actually goes to eat dinner. My first taste of obanzai, Kyoto's home-style small dishes, was at a tiny place called Gion Gonbei, eight seats at the counter, no printed menu, because the owner shops the market each morning and cooks whatever looked good that day. That night I had taitan, simmered vegetables with koya-dofu (freeze-dried tofu) that had soaked up so much bonito and kombu stock it dissolved on my tongue, served alongside a small dish of pickled eggplant and a bowl of miso soup — the whole set ran about 1,800 yen and left a deeper impression than any Michelin-starred meal I've had. Yuba, the delicate skin that forms on simmering soy milk, deserves a dedicated trip to Arashiyama — at Saga Sodo, the yudofu set arrives as a small pot gently bubbling at the table, steam carrying a faint soybean sweetness; dip the tofu in the house yuzu-soy sauce and it slides down the throat with almost no resistance. Sets start around 3,500 yen, and in autumn you can watch the maple trees blaze red over Togetsukyo Bridge from the window seat. For something more everyday, Menya Inoichi's Kyoto-style ramen — a rare double broth of soy sauce and chicken paitan — is clean but deeply savory, about 1,000 yen a bowl, best eaten late with a cold beer, which is exactly how off-duty Kyoto locals unwind.

One Soup, Three Dishes, and Nothing Out of Season

To understand the logic behind these meals, you need two phrases Kyoto people repeat almost like scripture: ichiju-sansai (one soup, three dishes) and fujifushoku (don't eat what isn't in season). The first, a structure passed down from the Edo period, is the backbone of everyday home cooking — a bowl of soup, three side dishes, a bowl of rice, simple but deliberately balanced. The second comes from Confucian teaching and reflects Kyoto's geography: ringed by mountains and historically cut off from easy trade routes, the city had to rely on what was locally and seasonally available, cultivating an almost religious devotion to shun, the peak moment of a food's season — bamboo shoots and sea bream in spring, hamo pike conger and winter melon in summer, matsutake mushrooms in autumn, and in winter the whole city seems to smell faintly of yuzu and simmering turnips. This reverence for the seasons spills into festivals too: every July during Gion Matsuri, Shijo Street closes to traffic and fills with yatai food stalls selling grilled corn, takoyaki, and chilled peaches, while locals put on yukata and stroll among the towering floats, eating as they walk — one of the few days such behavior is quietly permitted. Come February's Setsubun festival, people throw roasted soybeans at Yasaka Shrine to drive away bad luck, then go home and eat one bean for every year of their age, a small ritual of gratitude for another year of health. None of these customs are really about the food itself — they're built around the simple act of eating, as if Kyoto believes that eating well, mindfully, is the deepest respect you can pay to the land that feeds you.

On my last night in Kyoto, I circled back to Nishiki Market. Most shutters were down by then, only a few lanterns still glowing outside Nishiki Tenmangu. The alley still carried a faint trace of bonito and soy sauce, like the city's last exhale before sleep. It occurred to me that Kyoto has never really survived on its temples and shrines alone — it survives on the pot of stock someone starts simmering every single morning, handed down, generation after generation.
