中文

飛機降落馬拉喀什那晚,機場外的空氣裡飄著柴火與孜然的味道,我就知道自己來對地方了。這座「紅色之城」的城牆、房舍、甚至地上的塵土都是同一種赭紅,彷彿整座城市被同一支畫筆塗過。但住下來幾天後我才發現,馬拉喀什的顏色學遠遠不只紅色——它是瓷磚拼出的幾何藍、染缸煮出的藏紅花黃、夜色裡襯出的燈籠橘。接下來六天,我把行程表換成一張「顏色與線條的地圖」,決定用工匠與畫家的眼睛,重新認識這座城市。

破曉測量:本尤瑟夫神學院的幾何晨光

清晨七點五十分,我走出麥地那裡那間只有六間房的傳統民居旅館,趁著遊覽車還沒把街道塞滿之前,先去本尤瑟夫神學院(Ben Youssef Madrasa)報到。門票約五十迪拉姆,售票窗口的老先生連頭都沒抬,顯然這對他來說是最平常不過的早晨。可是一走進中庭,我整個人愣住了——四面牆從地板到天花板,層層疊疊都是八角星形的馬賽克磁磚(zellige),沒有一片磁磚的裁切角度是機器印出來的規整感,每一塊都帶著手工鑿刻留下的細微不對稱。中庭正中央的水池映著清晨才有的那種淡金色天光,水面幾乎不動,只有偶爾掠過的鴿子翅膀擦出一圈漣漪。我伸手摸了摸牆面的雪松木雕花窗櫺(mashrabiya),木頭被幾百年來的手掌磨得溫潤,指尖劃過那些幾何鏤空,像是在讀一本盲文寫成的建築史。導覽員後來跟我說,十四世紀的工匠相信完美只屬於真主,所以每一組重複的圖案裡,都會故意留下一處不對稱的瑕疵——那一刻我站在原地看了很久,忽然明白為什麼這裡的美讓人安靜,而不是讓人驚呼。

“完美只屬於真主,工匠必須留下一處不對稱的瑕疵。”——這句話,我在馬拉喀什的每一面牆上都重新讀了一次。

秘密花園:一趟腳踏實地的設計朝聖

離開神學院,我沒有叫車,而是決定用雙腳走一趟到Le Jardin Secret,想親自感受麥地那巷弄的紋理。地圖上直線距離不到六百公尺,實際走起來卻花了將近四十分鐘——因為Souk Semmarine裡的巷子窄得只容兩人並肩,兩側掛滿皮革包、銅盤、羊毛披肩,每走十步就有店家探頭喊「你好朋友,只是看看,不用買」,我索性把手機收進包包,靠著記路標(一間賣香料的攤位、一座藍色小門)慢慢摸索。找到入口時大概是上午九點半,買票約八十迪拉姆(含塔樓登頂),裡面別有洞天:這是一座十九世紀被修復的里亞德庭園,分成「伊斯蘭幾何庭園」與「異國植物庭園」兩區,水道以精準的坡度讓水流沿著地磚紋路緩緩移動,象徵伊斯蘭花園裡「天堂四河」的意象。我特地挑近中午時分爬上磚造瞭望塔,站在頂端往下看,整片麥地那的赭紅屋頂像被揉皺的紙,遠處還能看到雪頂的亞特拉斯山稜線。塔梯很窄,遇到下樓遊客得側身相讓,建議避開週末上午十一點後的人潮尖峰。我在裡頭待了將近一個半小時,園內附設的小咖啡館還能點一杯薄荷茶坐下來喘口氣,那杯茶的甜度高得驚人,卻意外地跟正午的日曬很搭。

藍色靈魂:馬約雷勒花園與YSL美術館的色彩教育

下午三點,陽光最烈的時候,我搭了一台橘色小計程車前往新城區的馬約雷勒花園(Jardin Majorelle),車資約二十迪拉姆。這裡曾是法國畫家雅克·馬約雷勒(Jacques Majorelle)在一九二〇年代買下的庭園,他調配出一種飽和到近乎螢光的鈷藍色,塗滿所有建築表面,後來這個顏色乾脆被命名為「馬約雷勒藍」。園內種滿仙人掌與竹林,藍色牆面與翠綠植物的對比強烈到有點不真實,像走進一張被過度飽和處理的底片。門票約一百五十迪拉姆(含柏柏爾博物館),排隊時前後都是舉著自拍棒的遊客,我原本有點抗拒這種「網美景點」的喧鬧,直到踏進隔壁的伊夫聖羅蘭美術館(Musée Yves Saint Laurent Marrakech),心情整個安靜下來。這棟由法國建築師事務所Studio KO操刀的建築,外牆用赭紅陶磚砌出類似柏柏爾織品經緯紋理的鏤空圖案,光線透過磚縫灑進室內,在牆上投出細碎的格狀光影,隨著參觀者移動而緩緩游移。館內展出聖羅蘭生前的手稿、面料樣本與高級訂製服草圖,昏暗的展廳燈光刻意壓低,只讓聚光燈打在每一件禮服的刺繡細節上,我站在一件以摩洛哥柏柏爾珠寶為靈感的禮服前站了快十分鐘,終於理解為什麼這位設計師會說馬拉喀什教會他色彩。

馬約雷勒藍不是一種顏色,而是一種被曬透的記憶,在陰影裡也捨不得暗下去。

染坊巷弄:市集裡活著的設計倫理

第三天上午,我特地繞進麥地那深處的染坊市集(Souk Sebbaghine),這裡沒有觀光指標,得靠鼻子——一股濃烈的礦物與植物氣味混著羊毛的騷味,把我引到一條掛滿彩色羊毛線束的巷子。粗麻繩上垂掛著一整片藏紅花黃、石榴紅、靛藍色的毛線,像是曬在半空中的煙火。一位叫哈桑的染匠看我盯著他手邊那桶正在冒煙的靛藍染液,直接招手要我過去看,他用長木棍攪動染缸,解釋這桶靛藍其實是從非斯(Fes)輾轉運來的乾燥藍靛葉泡製,黃色來自番紅花與石榴皮,紅色則靠茜草根。他把一束剛染好還濕答答的羊毛線塞進我手裡,顏色鮮豔到我一時不敢用力握。哈桑順手遞來一杯薄荷茶——在這裡,交易永遠從喝茶開始,先建立關係才能談價錢,直接殺價反而失禮。後來我在旁邊小攤買了一條手工染的羊毛圍巾,店主開價兩百迪拉姆,我試著還到一百二,他笑著搖頭說「這樣我要哭了」,最後在一百五十成交,兩人握手時他還多塞了一小包薰衣草乾花當作禮物。我後來才知道,午後禮拜前市集會安靜下來,許多攤位主人會暫時離開祈禱,遊客若在此時大聲喧嘩會被視為不禮貌——這種節奏感,比任何導覽手冊都更快地教會我尊重當地人的生活步調。

屋頂上的黃昏盛宴,與廣場甦醒的夜

傍晚五點半,我爬上位於Rahba Lahdim廣場旁的Nomad餐廳,沿著三層狹窄樓梯一路往上,最後一段還得側身通過堆滿香料籃的雜貨鋪屋頂。露台位置一位難求,我提早半小時到,才搶到能俯瞰整個香料廣場的角落桌。點了招牌的鵪鶉法式酥皮餡餅(pastilla),外皮撒滿肉桂糖粉,咬下去先是甜,接著鹹香的肉汁與杏仁碎才慢慢釋放,口感層次複雜到我停下來想了好幾秒才確定自己喜歡;配菜是烤時蔬與一份薄荷檸檬氣泡飲,兩人份消費大約落在四百五十迪拉姆。夕陽把整片赭紅屋頂染成焦糖色,清真寺喚拜聲從遠處傳來,混著樓下攤販收攤的吆喝,那一刻的光線美得讓我完全忘記要拍照。晚上七點多,我沿著小巷走下德吉瑪廣場(Jemaa el-Fnaa),白天的空地此刻已經變身成一整片流動廚房與表演場,煙霧、孜然味與烤肉香瀰漫在空氣裡。我在編號較後段、當地人排隊較長的一個攤位喝了一碗蝸牛湯(babbouche),湯頭帶著八角與胡椒的辛香,一碗大約十迪拉姆,老闆用小叉子示範怎麼把蝸牛肉挑出來吃。廣場另一頭,畫著幾何花紋的henna女藝師蹲坐在小板凳上,手法俐落地在遊客手背畫出對稱的藤蔓圖案,旁邊一圈身穿藍袍、手持金屬響板(qraqeb)的Gnaoua樂手正敲出低沉又催眠的節奏,圍觀的人越聚越多,形成一圈天然的表演場,這種街頭即興的視覺與聲音設計,比任何美術館的裝置藝術都更真實地活著。

我在廣場邊緣找了張塑膠矮凳坐下,買了一杯四迪拉姆的現榨柳橙汁,看著整個廣場在夜色裡像一齣不斷重新排演的劇場——說書人被圍成一圈聽眾,耍蛇人的笛聲斷斷續續,烤肉攤的煙一陣陣飄向星空。那一刻我才真正明白,馬拉喀什的設計美學從來不只在博物館的展櫃裡,它同樣活在染缸的顏色配方、瓷磚工匠故意留下的瑕疵、和廣場上此起彼落的鼓點節奏之中。

回旅館的路上,我又刻意繞了遠路穿過已經漸漸安靜下來的巷弄,牆面上的手繪招牌在昏黃路燈下顯得格外溫柔。這趟旅程讓我重新定義了「設計之城」這個詞——它不需要展覽開幕酒會,只需要一整代工匠願意日復一日,把顏色與幾何刻進生活的縫隙裡。

ENGLISH

The night my plane touched down in Marrakech, the air outside the terminal already smelled of woodsmoke and cumin, and I knew instantly I had come to the right place. This so-called Red City—its ramparts, its houses, even the dust on the ground—shares one single ochre-red hue, as if the whole town had been painted with the same brush. But within a few days of settling in, I realized Marrakech's color theory runs far deeper than red alone: it is the geometric blue of hand-cut tile, the saffron yellow simmering in a dye vat, the lantern-orange glow that spills across the medina after dark. Over the six days that followed, I rewrote my itinerary as a map of color and line, determined to see this city through the eyes of its craftsmen and painters rather than its guidebooks.

Dawn Geometry: The Light Inside Ben Youssef Madrasa

At 7:50am I stepped out of the six-room riad where I was staying, hoping to reach the Ben Youssef Madrasa before the tour buses clogged the lanes. The entrance fee ran about 50 dirhams, and the old man at the ticket window barely looked up—clearly this was just another ordinary morning to him. But the moment I entered the central courtyard, I stopped in my tracks. Every wall, floor to ceiling, was layered with eight-pointed star mosaics known as zellige, and not a single tile carried the flat regularity of machine cutting—each one bore the faint asymmetry of a hand chisel. The pool at the courtyard's center mirrored the pale gold light of early morning, its surface almost still, disturbed only by the occasional flutter of a pigeon's wing. I reached out and touched the carved cedar mashrabiya screens, the wood worn smooth and warm by centuries of palms, and running my fingers over the geometric latticework felt like reading a building's history in braille. My guide later told me that fourteenth-century craftsmen believed perfection belonged to God alone, so within every repeating pattern they deliberately left one small asymmetry. I stood there a long while afterward, finally understanding why this kind of beauty makes you go quiet instead of reaching for your camera.

“Perfection belongs to God alone—the craftsman must leave one flaw.” I read that sentence again on every wall I touched in Marrakech.

A Walking Pilgrimage: Finding Le Jardin Secret

Leaving the madrasa, I skipped the taxi and walked toward Le Jardin Secret instead, wanting to feel the texture of the medina's alleys under my own feet. The map showed barely six hundred meters in a straight line, yet it took nearly forty minutes to arrive—Souk Semmarine's lanes narrow to barely shoulder-width, lined with leather bags, hammered brass trays, and wool shawls, and every ten steps someone called out, 'Hello friend, just looking, no need to buy.' I eventually put my phone away and navigated by landmarks instead—a spice stall here, a small blue door there. I reached the entrance around 9:30am; admission ran about 80 dirhams including access to the tower. Inside was another world entirely: a restored nineteenth-century riad garden divided into an Islamic geometric section and an exotic botanical section, its water channels sloped with astonishing precision so that water moves along the tile grooves in a pattern echoing the Islamic image of the four rivers of paradise. I deliberately climbed the brick lookout tower close to noon, and from the top the entire medina spread out like crumpled red paper, with the snow-capped ridge of the Atlas Mountains visible in the distance. The staircase is narrow, so expect to turn sideways when passing descending visitors, and it's worth avoiding the crowd surge after 11am on weekends. I lingered nearly ninety minutes altogether, finishing with a glass of mint tea at the small on-site café—shockingly sweet, yet somehow perfectly matched to the midday heat.

A Blue Education: Jardin Majorelle and the Musée Yves Saint Laurent

By 3pm, under the harshest sun of the day, I took a small orange petit taxi to the new town for around 20 dirhams to reach Jardin Majorelle. This garden once belonged to the French painter Jacques Majorelle, who in the 1920s mixed a cobalt so saturated it now bears his own name—Majorelle Blue. Cactus groves and bamboo stands fill the grounds, and the contrast between that electric blue and deep green foliage feels almost unreal, like an over-saturated photograph brought to life. Admission runs about 150 dirhams including the Berber Museum, and the queue was thick with people wielding selfie sticks—an atmosphere I found mildly grating until I stepped next door into the Musée Yves Saint Laurent Marrakech. Designed by the French firm Studio KO, its exterior is built from terracotta brick perforated in a pattern echoing the weave of Berber textiles, and daylight filtering through those gaps casts a shifting lattice of shadow across the interior walls as visitors move through the space. Inside, the museum displays Saint Laurent's original sketches, fabric swatches, and haute couture drafts under deliberately dim lighting, spotlights trained only on the embroidered details of each gown. I stood in front of a dress inspired by Berber jewelry for a good ten minutes, and it finally clicked why the designer always said Marrakech was the place that taught him color.

Majorelle Blue isn't really a color—it's a sun-bleached memory that refuses to dim even in shadow.

Alleys of Color: The Living Design Ethics of the Souk

On my third morning I wandered deliberately into Souk Sebbaghine, the dyers' quarter, tucked deep in the medina with no tourist signage to guide me—I found it by smell alone, a heavy mix of mineral and plant odors mingled with raw wool, leading me down a lane hung with skeins of colored yarn draped over rope like fireworks frozen mid-explosion: saffron yellow, pomegranate red, indigo blue. A dyer named Hassan noticed me staring at a smoking vat of indigo and waved me over, stirring the dye with a long wooden pole as he explained that this particular indigo arrived dried from Fes, the yellow came from saffron and pomegranate skins, and the red from madder root. He pressed a still-dripping skein of freshly dyed wool into my hands, its color so vivid I hesitated to squeeze it. In this souk, as in most of Marrakech, transactions begin with tea, not price—Hassan handed me a glass of mint tea before we'd even discussed money, because here relationship precedes negotiation, and haggling too aggressively without first accepting the tea is considered rude. I ended up buying a hand-dyed wool scarf from a nearby stall; the vendor opened at 200 dirhams, I countered at 120, and he laughed and said 'you'll make me cry,' before we settled at 150, sealing it with a handshake and a small gift of dried lavender tucked into my bag. I later learned that the souk quiets noticeably before afternoon prayer, as many stall owners step away briefly to pray, and raising your voice during that window is considered disrespectful—a rhythm no guidebook taught me nearly as fast as watching it happen in front of me.

A Rooftop Feast at Dusk, and the Square Waking Into Night

At 5:30pm I climbed to the rooftop of Nomad, a restaurant just off Rahba Lahdim square, up three flights of narrow stairs and one final sideways squeeze past a grocer's crates stacked along the last landing. Rooftop tables go fast, so I arrived thirty minutes early just to claim a corner overlooking the spice square below. I ordered the signature quail pastilla, its pastry dusted in cinnamon sugar—sweet on first bite, then giving way to savory juices and crushed almonds in a layered complexity that made me pause mid-forkful just to process it. Alongside came roasted vegetables and a mint-lemon sparkler; two people eating this way runs roughly 450 dirhams total. The setting sun turned the endless red rooftops the color of caramel, the call to prayer drifting in from a distant mosque mixed with vendors below packing up for the evening, and the light was so good I simply forgot to take a photo. By 7pm I had wound my way down into Jemaa el-Fnaa, which by then had transformed entirely from an empty daytime square into a sprawling open-air kitchen and stage, thick with smoke, cumin, and grilling meat. At a stall further down the row, where the queue skewed mostly local, I had a bowl of snail soup, babbouche, its broth spiced with anise and pepper, about 10 dirhams a bowl, the vendor demonstrating with a tiny fork how to hook the snail out of its shell. Across the square, henna artists crouched on low stools, painting symmetrical vine patterns onto tourists' hands with practiced speed, while a circle of Gnaoua musicians in indigo robes shook metal qraqeb castanets in a low, hypnotic rhythm that drew an ever-thickening crowd around them—an entirely improvised piece of visual and sonic street design more alive than anything I've seen behind museum glass.

I found a plastic stool at the square's edge and bought a glass of fresh orange juice for 4 dirhams, watching the whole square unfold like a play that reinvents itself nightly—storytellers ringed by listeners, a snake charmer's flute rising and falling, smoke from the grills drifting up toward the stars. It was only then that I truly understood: Marrakech's design sensibility was never confined to museum vitrines. It lives just as fully in the dye vat's color formulas, in the deliberate flaw a tile-maker leaves behind, and in the drumbeat rising and falling across a crowded square after dark.

Walking back to the riad, I deliberately took the long way through lanes already settling into quiet, hand-painted shop signs glowing soft under the streetlamps. This trip rewrote what 'a city of design' means to me—it needs no gallery opening, no champagne reception. It only needs generations of craftsmen willing, day after day, to carve color and geometry into the seams of ordinary life.
