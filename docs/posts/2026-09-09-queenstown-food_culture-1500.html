中文

從皇后鎮機場開車進市區的路上,我一路盯著瓦卡蒂普湖(Lake Wakatipu)墨綠色的湖面,和對岸那排像鋸齒一樣、被當地人叫做「顫抖山脈」(The Remarkables)的山頭。所有人告訴我皇后鎮是南半球的極限運動之都,高空彈跳、噴射快艇、滑雪度假村的宣傳照鋪天蓋地。但這一趟,我行李箱裡塞的不是雪板護具,而是一本寫滿餐廳與市集地址的筆記本。五天下來我才明白,這座小鎮真正讓我念念不忘的,不是任何一次跳傘,而是市集裡凍紅的手指、蒸汽船上飄來的煤煙味,還有酒窖裡那杯讓釀酒師眼睛發亮的黑皮諾。

清晨的市集心跳:皇后鎮農夫市集

週六早上八點四十分,我裹著羽絨外套走到雷馬克斯公園(Remarkables Park)旁的空地,皇后鎮湖區農夫市集(Queenstown Lakes Farmers' Market)已經在晨霧裡熱鬧起來。空氣冷得讓呼出的白氣掛在臉前,大約六十個攤位一字排開,烤麵包的焦香和現磨咖啡的酸香混在一起,還沒醒透的我先被一杯Nectar咖啡車的濃縮咖啡拉回人間。賣生乳起司的Wakatipu Cheese攤主是位滿手龜裂的中年女人,她一邊切著熟成的高地起司一邊跟我解釋紐西蘭對生乳製品的嚴格規範,語氣裡帶著一點驕傲又一點無奈。隔壁攤是從史基帕斯峽谷(Skippers Canyon)開了兩小時車下山的牧羊人家庭,賣的美麗諾羊肉香腸滋滋冒油,老先生說今年山上乾旱,羊養得比往年瘦。街角一個彈木吉他的街頭藝人唱著老式鄉村歌,零錢罐裡躺著幾枚硬幣。我buy了一小罐當地養蜂人「Bee Fabulous」的蜂蜜,回旅館拌優格吃,野花香氣濃到有點刺鼻,卻意外好吃。

皇后鎮的清晨市集告訴我,這座小鎮的溫柔,其實藏在它最忙碌的極限運動海報背後。

排隊的儀式:Fergburger與小鎮的午餐哲學

中午十二點十五分,我走到Shotover街上的Fergburger,還沒轉過街角就先看到排到門外的隊伍。這間2001年從一個小小外帶窗口起家的漢堡店,如今已經是皇后鎮的地標級信仰,排隊時間視季節從十五分鐘到一小時不等。我排了大約二十五分鐘,身邊全是穿著滑雪外套、臉頰凍得發紅的年輕背包客。跟我聊起來的是一位在Fergburger夜班打工的英國女生Lily,她笑說每年冬季雪季一開始,鎮上就湧進一批像她這樣「候鳥式」的季節工,白天滑雪、晚上煎肉排,鎮民甚至半開玩笑叫這群人「Fergburger寄生族」。輪到我點餐,我選了招牌的Sweet Bacon Booster(紐幣約17.5元),加點了一份Fergbaker的牛肉派當前菜。漢堡端出來時,芝麻布里歐許麵包還帶著烤爐的溫熱,咬下去先是脆培根的鹹香,接著炙燒牛肉排的肉汁混著焦糖化洋蔥的甜,最後是甜辣醬的尾勁,層次多到讓人邊嚼邊笑出聲。我端著油紙包走到湖邊的Marine Parade長椅坐下,海鷗在頭頂盤旋覬覦我的薯條,遠處雪山映在湖面,那一刻我完全理解為什麼這間店可以讓遊客心甘情願排隊一小時。

蒸汽船與高原牧場:一趟從湖上到餐桌的旅程

隔天下午一點,我提早半小時抵達Beach街上的Steamer Wharf碼頭,前一晚在Real NZ官網訂了蒸汽船巡遊加農場BBQ午餐的套票,紐幣129元,差點因為訂太晚而沒搶到位子——後來得知一到十二月的旺季最好提前一到兩天預訂。登船時工作人員在手腕上綁了條紋腕帶,我跟著人群走上1912年下水、至今仍以燒煤運轉的TSS Earnslaw雙螺旋槳蒸汽船。船艙下層有個玻璃觀景窗,能看到一位叫Steve的機工正把煤鏟進鍋爐,黑煙味混著機油味撲面而來,黃銅儀表盤在昏黃燈光下閃著油亮的光,活塞規律的撞擊聲像是這艘老船的心跳。渡湖航程約四十五分鐘,湖水在船邊翻起白色浪花,山風大到我得把圍巾拉高蓋住半張臉。抵達對岸的Walter Peak高原牧場後,牧羊人帶我們看剃羊毛示範,一隻叫Tui的邊境牧羊犬俐落地把一群美麗諾羊趕進圍欄,動作快得讓遊客席一陣驚呼,接著還能近距離餵食高地牛和羊駝。午餐在殖民風的Colonel's Homestead餐廳吃烤羊排配時蔬沙拉,最後上桌的巴芙洛娃蛋糕(Pavlova)外殼酥脆內裡綿密,是我這趟旅行吃到最正統的紐西蘭甜點。唯一失算的是我忘了戴帽子,湖上風大又反光,回程時鼻頭已經曬得脫皮。整趟行程含往返船程約三個半小時,四點準時返航,船上小酒吧只收信用卡,賣的是瓦納卡當地精釀啤酒。

在瓦卡蒂普湖上,時間被煤煙和引擎聲拉得很慢,慢到剛好夠你想清楚自己為什麼要來這裡。

山谷裡的酒杯:Gibbston Valley與中奧塔哥的黑皮諾故事

第三天傍晚五點,我沿六號公路開車二十分鐘到Gibbston Valley酒莊,這裡是中奧塔哥產區最早的一批酒莊,1985年就在山坡下挖出一座至今仍在使用的酒窖山洞。品酒室裡的酒窖服務生Anna帶我走進洞穴,涼濕的空氣裡飄著橡木桶和潮土混合的氣味,遠處還能聽見水滴落在石頭上的回聲。她說中奧塔哥是全世界最靠南的葡萄酒產區之一,緯度接近南緯45度,白天日照強、夜晚溫差大,加上片岩土壤(schist)帶來的礦物感,種出來的黑皮諾酸度俐落、單寧細膩。我點了一份五杯試飲(紐幣15元,買酒可退費),其中2021年份的莊園黑皮諾入口是櫻桃與乾燥香草的氣味,尾韻帶一點鐵鏽般的礦石味,配上一盤Mt Difficulty起司與當地煙燻鹿肉香腸(紐幣32元),鹹香跟酒的果酸剛好互相拉扯出平衡。從品酒室的落地窗望出去,能看到不遠處卡瓦勞大橋(Kawarau Bridge)上正有人綁著彈力繩往下跳,尖叫聲隨風飄進酒莊,跟眼前這杯需要靜下心品嚐的紅酒形成一種荒謬又迷人的對比——同一個山谷,同時裝著這座小鎮最喧鬧與最安靜的兩種靈魂。

深夜酒吧與冬日祭:當地人教我的皇后鎮生活方式

當晚九點多,我走進Camp街上的The World Bar,天花板上掛滿了退役的雪板和滑雪杖,牆上塗鴉斑駁,酒吧招牌調酒是裝在真正的雪靴造型杯裡的熱紅酒「Winter Boot」,紐幣15元,肉桂和柳橙皮的香氣撲鼻,喝一口暖流從喉頭直竄到胃裡。調酒師Jayden跟我聊起這座小鎮的真實作息:冬天雪季一到,鎮上人口會因為大批來自英國、加拿大、日本的季節工暴增,本地人半開玩笑把這群人叫做「季節性漂流者」,而真正在皇后鎮長大的人反而是少數。他提到每年六月底舉行的皇后鎮冬日祭(Queenstown Winter Festival),為期十天,開幕夜會在湖邊的Village Green放煙火,湖岸邊擺滿餐車,賣鹿肉迷你漢堡和青貝巧達湯,還有一場誰都不怕冷似的瘋狂變裝遊行,連學校都會讓孩子提早半天放學去看熱鬧。他又說起本地人週末看橄欖球全黑隊比賽的傳統,大家會擠進1863年老建築改建的Pig & Whistle酒吧,半場休息時全場一起分食肉派,那種齊聲吶喊的氣氛,跟白天遊客在湖邊排隊拍照的皇后鎮完全是兩個世界。走出酒吧時已近午夜,寒氣刺骨,但胸口那杯熱紅酒的暖意還沒散去,我忽然明白,這座小鎮把「刺激」留給了白天的遊客,卻把最真實的溫度,留在了這樣一個尋常的深夜。

在皇后鎮,一杯裝在雪靴裡的熱紅酒,比任何一次高空彈跳都更誠實地告訴你,這裡的冬天有多長。

回程班機起飛前,我在機場咖啡座又點了一杯Nectar咖啡,望著跑道盡頭那片熟悉的雪山輪廓,想起這幾天吃過的所有東西——市集裡凍手的蜂蜜、油紙包著的漢堡、蒸汽船上煤煙味裡的巴芙洛娃、酒窖裡的黑皮諾,還有那杯雪靴熱紅酒。皇后鎮的美食從來不是精緻擺盤的排場,而是這座小鎮跟山、跟湖、跟一批又一批留下來又離開的人,反覆磨合出來的生活方式。下次如果有人再問我皇后鎮好不好玩,我大概會說,先別急著跳傘,先去農夫市集喝杯咖啡吧。

ENGLISH

On the drive in from Queenstown airport, I kept staring out the window at the deep green surface of Lake Wakatipu and, across it, the jagged ridgeline locals call The Remarkables. Everyone had told me the same thing before I left: Queenstown is the adventure capital of the Southern Hemisphere, a town built on bungy cords, jet boats and ski passes. But this time my suitcase held no snowboard gear, only a battered notebook full of restaurant names and market addresses. Five days later, what I could not stop thinking about was not a single jump or a single ride. It was the way my fingers turned red holding a coffee at a Saturday market, the smell of coal smoke drifting off a century-old steamship, and a glass of Pinot Noir that made a winemaker's eyes light up in an underground cave.

The Pulse of the Morning: Queenstown Lakes Farmers' Market

It was 8.40am on a Saturday when I walked, wrapped in a down jacket, toward the open ground beside Remarkables Park Town Centre. The Queenstown Lakes Farmers' Market was already buzzing under a thin morning fog. The air was cold enough that my breath hung in front of my face, and roughly sixty stalls lined up in neat rows, the smell of toasting bread mixing with the sharp tang of freshly ground coffee. A double-shot flat white from the Nectar coffee cart dragged me back into consciousness before anything else could. At the Wakatipu Cheese stall, a middle-aged woman with cracked, hardworking hands sliced into a wheel of aged raw-milk cheese while explaining, half proud and half exasperated, just how strict New Zealand's raw dairy regulations are. Next to her, a farming family who had driven two hours down from Skippers Canyon were selling merino lamb sausages sizzling on a portable grill; the older man mentioned, almost apologetically, that this year's drought had left the sheep thinner than usual. Around the corner, a busker strummed an old country tune on an acoustic guitar, a handful of coins rattling in the case at his feet. I bought a small jar of wildflower honey from a beekeeper called Bee Fabulous, and back at the lodge the next morning I stirred it into yoghurt — the floral scent was almost too strong, and somehow that made it better.

The Saturday market taught me that Queenstown's gentleness hides just behind its loudest adventure-tourism posters.

The Ritual of Queuing: Fergburger and the Town's Theory of Lunch

At 12.15pm I turned onto Shotover Street and, before I even reached the corner, saw the line already spilling out the door of Fergburger. What began in 2001 as a tiny takeaway window has become something close to a religious pilgrimage site in this town, with wait times ranging anywhere from fifteen minutes to a full hour depending on the season. I waited about twenty-five minutes, surrounded by young backpackers in ski jackets, cheeks flushed red from the cold. A British woman named Lily, standing next to me, worked the night shift at Fergburger and laughed as she explained that every winter, when ski season kicks off, the town fills up with seasonal migrants just like her — skiing all day, flipping patties all night. Locals half-jokingly call this crowd the Fergburger generation. When my turn finally came, I ordered the signature Sweet Bacon Booster, roughly NZD 17.50, plus a steak-and-cheese pie from Fergbaker next door as a starter. The sesame brioche bun arrived still warm from the oven; the first bite delivered crisp, salty bacon, followed by the char-grilled beef patty's juices mingling with caramelised onion, and finishing with a slow burn of sweet chilli sauce. I carried the greasy paper wrap down to a bench along Marine Parade, seagulls circling overhead eyeing my fries, snow-capped peaks reflected upside down on the lake, and in that exact moment I finally understood why tourists are willing to queue an hour for this burger.

Steamship and High Country: A Journey from the Lake to the Table

The following afternoon at 1pm, I arrived thirty minutes early at Steamer Wharf on Beach Street, having booked a combined steamship cruise and farm barbecue lunch through Real NZ's website the night before for NZD 129 — cutting it close, since I later learned peak season, December through February, can sell out one to two days in advance. Staff fastened a striped wristband to my arm and I followed the crowd up the gangway onto the TSS Earnslaw, a twin-screw coal-fired steamship launched in 1912 and still running on coal today. Below deck, a glass viewing window let passengers watch an engineer named Steve shovel coal into the furnace; the smell of coal smoke and engine oil filled the corridor, brass gauges gleamed under dim yellow light, and the steady thump of the pistons felt like the old vessel's heartbeat. The crossing took about forty-five minutes, white spray kicking up along the hull as mountain wind forced me to pull my scarf up over half my face. On arrival at Walter Peak High Country Farm, a shepherd led us through a shearing demonstration, then a border collie named Tui expertly herded a flock of merino sheep into a pen, drawing gasps from the crowd, before we got to hand-feed highland cattle and alpacas. Lunch was served at the colonial-style Colonel's Homestead: grilled lamb chops with seasonal vegetables, followed by a pavlova with a crisp shell and a soft marshmallow centre — the most authentically New Zealand dessert I ate on the whole trip. My one mistake was forgetting a hat; the glare off the lake was brutal, and by the return crossing my nose was already peeling. The whole excursion, boat included, ran about three and a half hours, docking back at 4pm sharp, with the onboard bar taking card only and pouring craft beer from a small brewery in Wanaka.

Out on Lake Wakatipu, time stretches slow under coal smoke and engine noise — slow enough to remember exactly why you came.

Glasses in the Valley: Gibbston Valley and the Story of Central Otago Pinot Noir

By 5pm on my third evening, a twenty-minute drive along State Highway 6 brought me to Gibbston Valley Winery, one of the earliest wineries in Central Otago, whose cave was carved into a hillside back in 1985 and is still in use today. A cellar hand named Anna led me down into the cave, where cool, damp air carried the mixed scent of oak barrels and wet stone, and somewhere in the darkness water dripped steadily against rock. She explained that Central Otago is one of the southernmost wine regions on earth, sitting near the 45th parallel, with strong daytime sun and sharp night-time cooling, plus schist-heavy soil that lends the Pinot Noir a distinct minerality. I ordered a five-wine flight for NZD 15, refundable against a bottle purchase, and the 2021 estate Pinot Noir opened with cherry and dried herb on the nose, finishing with a faint rusty, mineral edge. Paired with a plate of Mt Difficulty cheese and locally smoked venison (NZD 32), the saltiness pulled nicely against the wine's bright acidity. Through the tasting room's floor-to-ceiling windows, I could see the Kawarau Bridge in the distance, where someone was mid-scream on a bungy cord, the sound drifting faintly into the cellar even as I sat quietly turning a glass of wine that demanded exactly the opposite kind of attention. Same valley, same afternoon — somehow it held both the loudest and the quietest soul of this town at once.

Late-Night Bars and a Winter Festival: What Locals Taught Me About Living Here

Just after 9pm that night, I walked into The World Bar on Camp Street, its ceiling hung with retired snowboards and ski poles, walls covered in fading graffiti, and its signature drink — mulled wine served in an actual ski-boot-shaped mug called the Winter Boot, NZD 15 — arrived smelling of cinnamon and orange peel, the warmth spreading down my throat with the first sip. The bartender, Jayden, told me what the town's rhythm really looks like: every winter, an influx of seasonal workers from the UK, Canada and Japan swells the population, and locals half-jokingly call this migrating crowd seasonal drifters, while people who actually grew up in Queenstown are, ironically, the minority. He mentioned the Queenstown Winter Festival, held every June, ten days of events kicking off with fireworks over the lake at Village Green, food trucks along the shoreline selling venison sliders and green-lipped mussel chowder, and a wildly costumed parade that seems to shrug off the cold entirely — schools even let kids out early to watch. He also described how locals pack into the Pig & Whistle, a pub built inside an 1863 courthouse building, on weekends to watch the All Blacks, sharing meat pies at halftime while the whole room roars in unison — a completely different Queenstown from the one tourists photograph by the lake in daylight. By the time I stepped back out near midnight, the cold cut straight through my jacket, but the warmth of that mulled wine hadn't left my chest, and I understood something clearly: this town saves its adrenaline for daytime visitors, but keeps its real warmth for ordinary nights like this one.

In Queenstown, a mulled wine served in a ski boot tells you more honestly how long the winter really is than any bungy jump ever could.

Waiting at the airport for my flight home, I ordered one last Nectar coffee and looked out at the familiar snow-capped skyline beyond the runway, running back through everything I had eaten that week — honey with cold-numbed fingers at the market, a burger wrapped in greasy paper, pavlova eaten with the smell of coal smoke still in my clothes, a glass of Pinot Noir in an underground cave, and that ski-boot mug of mulled wine. Queenstown's food was never about elegant plating or fine dining theatre. It was a way of living, worked out slowly between a mountain, a lake, and generation after generation of people who arrived, stayed a while, and eventually moved on. So if anyone asks me again whether Queenstown is worth the trip, I think I will just tell them: skip the bungy jump for now, and go get a coffee at the farmers' market first.
