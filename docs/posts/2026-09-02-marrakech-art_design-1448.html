中文

飛機降落在馬拉喀什-梅納拉機場的那一刻,舷窗外是一片被夕陽染成焦糖色的城牆,像是有人把整座城市浸在紅茶裡撈起來曬乾。我拖著行李走出機艙,熱風裡混著柴煙與孜然的氣味撲面而來——那是我對這座城市的第一個記憶,還沒進城,鼻子已經替我上了第一堂課。計程車司機哈金說好七十迪拉姆(約台幣兩百二十元)送我到麥地那城門口,一路上他半法語半阿拉伯語地解釋,城牆之所以是這種粉赭色,是因為就地取用的紅土混合了石灰與稻草稈,「這座城市,」他說,「是從地底長出來的。」

黎明前的赭紅之城

清晨六點,我推開里亞德(riad,摩洛哥傳統合院民宿)厚重的雪松木門,天還沒全亮,喚拜聲從庫圖比亞清真寺(Koutoubia)的宣禮塔傳來,拉長又迴盪,像是整座城市共用的一組心跳。巷子窄得只容兩人並肩,牆面是那種吸飽了整夜濕氣的深赭紅,手指劃過還帶著微涼的顆粒觸感。掃街的老人用棕櫚葉綁成的掃把清出一條乾淨的路,麵包店剛開門,烤爐的熱氣混著酵母香竄出來,我買了一個五迪拉姆的哈布茲圓餅(khobz),邊走邊吃,外皮脆得掉屑,裡面卻軟得像雲。這時候的麥地那還沒被遊客佔領,只有貓、麵包師傅和騎著摩托車送貨的少年,光線是斜的、金色的,打在牆面上,讓那些原本平凡的裂縫和補丁都成了畫。

在馬拉喀什,顏色不是裝飾,是這座城市呼吸的方式。

迷宮深處的幾何密碼——本尤素福學院與巴伊亞宮

吃完早餐,我對照Google地圖從里亞德步行前往本尤素福古蘭經學院(Medersa Ben Youssef),路程約十五分鐘,穿過賽馬林市集(Souk Semmarine)最擁擠的一段,得不斷側身避開扛著羊皮的搬運工和騎驢車的小販。學院九點開門,門票五十迪拉姆,我特意提早到,結果還是撲了個空,排隊排了二十分鐘才進去——後來才知道,旅行團通常十點半後才會湧入,想拍空景真的得趁八點半到九點半這個窗口。一踏進中庭我就愣住了:四面牆從地面到穹頂完全被覆蓋,下段是綠白相間的澤利吉馬賽克磚(zellige),幾何圖案精密得像用尺量出來的星芒;中段轉為手工雕刻的灰泥(gebs),藤蔓與可蘭經文纏繞交錯;最上層才是雪松木雕花天花板,深棕色木頭在天光下泛著油潤的光。導覽員告訴我,這些磚都是先燒好單色瓷磚,再一片片手工切割成幾何小塊拼貼而成,一平方公尺的牆面可能要耗掉工匠一週的時間。我在中庭的水池邊坐了將近四十分鐘,水面倒映著雕花拱門,幾乎忘記時間。

離開學院後,我沿著里亞德拉魯斯街(Riad Laarous)走向巴伊亞宮(Bahia Palace),大約二十分鐘腳程,途中會經過猶太區(Mellah)邊界,空氣裡開始飄出皮革與香料混合的氣味。巴伊亞宮門票七十迪拉姆,佔地遼闊到我逛了將近兩小時才走完主要廳室,大臣的正妻與四位側室各自擁有獨立庭院,每座庭院的磁磚配色與雕花細節都不重複,像是十九世紀的摩洛哥設計師在跟自己比賽誰的品味更講究。整趟行程加起來,兩座建築花了我大約三個半小時,水和防曬乳一定要帶,地磚在正午會反光刺眼,鞋子最好選好脫的,因為部分廳堂要求脫鞋進入。

染坊巷弄與一頓花園裡的午餐

正午時分,我鑽進染坊市集(Souk Sebbaghine),還沒看到店面,先聞到刺鼻又帶點鹼味的染料氣味。頭頂拉滿了曬乾的羊毛線,靛藍、番紅花黃、石榴紅像一道道瀑布垂掛下來遮住天光,踩在濕滑的石板路上得格外小心。一位叫哈山的染匠邀我看他把整束羊毛浸入滾燙的靛藍缸裡,線材拉出水面的瞬間是墨綠色,氧化幾秒後才轉成深藍,他笑著說這是「摩洛哥人的魔術,不用相機濾鏡」。我在他的小攤買了一條手工染的羊毛圍巾,殺價從一百五十迪拉姆談到九十迪拉姆,他還故意嘆氣說我「殺價像本地人」。

下午一點半,我走到麥地那深處的Le Jardin餐廳吃午餐,地址在Sidi el Yamani巷內,得先穿過一條完全看不出裡面有餐廳的木門,推開後卻是一座被香蕉樹與九重葛包圍的中庭花園,烏龜在角落慢慢爬,鳥叫聲蓋過巷外市集的喧囂。我點了炭烤茄子沙拉(zaalouk)當開胃菜,茄子燉到近乎融化,帶著孜然與蒜頭的煙燻香氣;主餐是慢燉羊肉塔吉,配著杏桃乾與烤杏仁,肉質軟到用叉子就能撥開,甜鹹交織的味道讓我一口接一口停不下來;最後奉上的薄荷茶倒得又高又急,茶壺舉在半空中畫出一道弧線,茶沫在杯口堆出細密的泡沫。整頓飯連稅約兩百二十迪拉姆(約台幣七百元),坐在樹蔭下吃了將近一個半小時,是我整趟旅程中最放鬆的一餐。

馬若雷勒花園的鈷藍法則

下午三點半,我搭計程車前往新城區古利茲(Guéliz)的馬若雷勒花園,車程約十五分鐘,車資三十迪拉姆。這座由法國畫家雅克・馬若雷勒於一九二〇年代打造、後來由聖羅蘭與皮耶・貝傑買下修復的花園,門票一百五十迪拉姆,旺季建議提前在官網訂票,不然入口排隊常常要等上四十分鐘。園內那種被稱為「馬若雷勒藍」的鈷藍色建築,在正午陽光下幾乎刺眼,和竹林的墨綠、陶盆的鮮黃形成一種近乎野蠻的對比,聖羅蘭曾說這片花園給了他一輩子都用不完的靈感色票。園區裡的柏柏爾博物館展出銀器與地毯,提醒我摩洛哥的設計語彙其實根植於柏柏爾族的幾何符號,而非後來大家熟悉的伊斯蘭花磚。

走出花園,隔著一條馬路就是由法國建築事務所Studio KO設計的聖羅蘭博物館,土紅色的編織紋理外牆據說是向澤利吉磚的幾何邏輯致敬,卻用當代的清水磚工法重新詮釋。館內展出的高訂草圖與馬拉喀什的旅行手記並陳,我站在一幅一九六〇年代的粉彩速寫前,突然理解這座城市為什麼能持續餵養一整個世紀的設計師——它的色彩邏輯根本不需要翻譯。

幾何不是裝飾,是這座城市的信仰語言,寫在每一面牆上。

廣場上的日與夜

傍晚六點半,我回到德吉瑪廣場(Jemaa el-Fna),太陽正好落在庫圖比亞尖塔後方,染坊的顏色彷彿被搬到了天空。廣場上的說書人、耍蛇人、畫亨娜的婦女一個個支起攤位,煙霧從烤肉攤升起,混著現榨柳橙汁的甜味。一位叫哈蒂嘉的亨娜阿姨拉住我的手,不由分說先畫了一小段圖案才報價——這是她們慣用的招攬方式,我後來學乖了,遠遠看到就先禮貌搖手示意。畫完一整隻手掌花了大約三十分鐘,收費一百二十迪拉姆,她邊畫邊跟我聊起這週五她們家要煮庫斯庫斯(couscous),因為「星期五吃庫斯庫斯」幾乎是全摩洛哥人默契的家庭儀式,連清真寺的主麻聚禮結束後,街上都會飄著同樣的香料味。她也提醒我,若正逢齋戒月造訪,白天在公開場合進食或喝水是很不禮貌的行為,即使是遊客也該避免,黃昏開齋的那一刻,整條廣場會突然安靜下來,所有人低頭吃第一口椰棗。

晚上八點,廣場上的說書傳統「哈爾卡」(halqa)已被列入聯合國非物質文化遺產,我聽不懂阿拉伯語,卻能從老說書人手勢的起伏猜到故事正走到高潮,圍觀的孩子和老人一樣聽得入神。我花五迪拉姆買了一碗蝸牛湯(babbouche),湯頭用茴香、丁香煮得又濃又暖,老闆用牙籤挑出蝸牛肉遞給我,那味道介於中藥與海鮮湯之間,奇特卻上癮。廣場的燈一盞盞亮起,油燈的煙、燒烤的炭香、薄荷茶的清涼氣味交織在一起,我站在人潮中央,忽然覺得這座城市所有的設計美學,最終都收束在這個廣場上——建築的幾何、市集的色彩、手作的溫度,全都在夜色裡活成了一場正在進行的表演。

離開馬拉喀什前的最後一晚,我又爬上一間屋頂咖啡館,俯瞰整座麥地那,清真寺尖塔在暮色中泛著淡淡的粉光。我想起哈金那句話——這座城市是從地底長出來的——此刻我終於懂了,那不只是關於建材,更是關於這座城市如何把幾何、色彩與手工,一代一代地種進每一面牆裡。

ENGLISH

The moment my plane touched down at Marrakech-Menara Airport, the cabin window framed a city wall the color of caramelized sugar, as if someone had steeped the entire city in tea and left it to dry in the sun. I stepped out into a wall of heat thick with woodsmoke and cumin — my first memory of this city arrived through my nose before I'd even entered its gates. My taxi driver, Hakim, negotiated seventy dirhams to take me to the medina entrance, and along the way, in a mix of French and Arabic, he explained why the walls glow that particular rose-ochre: local red clay mixed with lime and straw. 'This city,' he said, 'grew up out of the ground.'

The Ochre City Before Dawn

At six in the morning I pushed open the heavy cedar door of my riad. The sky hadn't fully lightened yet when the call to prayer rolled out from the Koutoubia minaret, stretching and echoing like the shared heartbeat of the whole city. The alleys were barely wide enough for two people abreast, the walls a deep ochre still damp from the night, gritty and cool under my fingertips. An old man swept the lane clean with a palm-frond broom; a bakery had just opened, yeasty heat pouring out with the smell of the oven. I bought a five-dirham round of khobz bread, crust shattering into flakes, the inside soft as a cloud. At this hour the medina still belonged to the cats, the bakers, and delivery boys on mopeds — the light fell sideways and gold, turning every crack and patched wall into something painterly.

In Marrakech, color isn't decoration — it's how the city breathes.

Geometry as Scripture: Ben Youssef Madrasa and the Bahia Palace

After breakfast I walked roughly fifteen minutes from my riad to the Ben Youssef Madrasa, cutting through the most congested stretch of Souk Semmarine, dodging porters hauling sheepskins and boys on donkey carts. The madrasa opens at nine and costs fifty dirhams; I arrived early hoping for an empty courtyard, but still waited twenty minutes in line — I later learned tour groups typically arrive after ten-thirty, so eight-thirty to nine-thirty is the real window for quiet photographs. Stepping into the courtyard stopped me cold: every wall, floor to ceiling, was covered in pattern. The lower band was green-and-white zellige mosaic, geometric stars so precise they looked machine-cut; above that, hand-carved plaster (gebs) twisted with vine motifs and Quranic script; and crowning it all, carved cedar wood glowing oil-brown in the light. A guide told me each zellige tile is fired as a single color and then hand-cut into tiny geometric pieces before assembly — a single square meter of wall can take a craftsman a week. I sat by the courtyard pool for nearly forty minutes, watching the carved archways ripple in the water, and nearly forgot I had somewhere else to be.

From there I walked about twenty minutes along Riad Laarous toward the Bahia Palace, passing the edge of the old Mellah, the air shifting to leather and spice. Entry is seventy dirhams, and the grounds are vast enough that I spent nearly two hours wandering — the vizier's senior wife and four secondary wives each had their own courtyard, no two sharing the same tile palette or carving pattern, as if nineteenth-century Moroccan designers were quietly competing with one another for taste. Between both sites I spent about three and a half hours total. Bring water and sunscreen — the tiled floors glare hard at midday — and wear shoes that slip off easily, since several halls require you to remove them.

The Dyers' Alley and a Lunch in a Hidden Garden

By noon I'd wandered into Souk Sebbaghine, the dyers' souk, smelling the sharp, faintly alkaline tang of dye before I saw a single stall. Overhead, skeins of wool hung drying — indigo, saffron yellow, pomegranate red — cascading down like waterfalls that blocked out the sky, the stone underfoot slick and treacherous. A dyer named Hassan showed me a bundle of wool plunged into a boiling indigo vat; pulled from the surface it looked murky green, only oxidizing into deep blue seconds later in the open air. 'Moroccan magic,' he laughed, 'no filter required.' I bought a hand-dyed wool scarf from his stall, haggling from a hundred fifty dirhams down to ninety, and he sighed theatrically that I 'bargained like a local.'

At half past one I found my way to Le Jardin restaurant deep in the medina, tucked behind an unmarked wooden door on a lane off Sidi el Yamani. Inside, unexpectedly, a courtyard garden opened up under banana leaves and bougainvillea, a tortoise ambling in the corner, birdsong drowning out the souk's noise beyond the walls. I started with zaalouk, smoky charred eggplant cooked nearly to a paste with cumin and garlic; then a slow-braised lamb tagine with dried apricots and toasted almonds, the meat falling apart at the touch of a fork, sweet and savory in equal measure — I couldn't stop eating. Mint tea arrived poured from high above the glass in one continuous arc, foam gathering in fine bubbles at the rim. The whole meal, tax included, came to about two hundred twenty dirhams, and I lingered under the shade for nearly ninety minutes — the most unhurried meal of the entire trip.

The Cobalt Logic of Jardin Majorelle

At half past three I took a taxi to the new town, Guéliz, a fifteen-minute ride for thirty dirhams, to reach the Jardin Majorelle. Built in the 1920s by French painter Jacques Majorelle and later restored by Yves Saint Laurent and Pierre Bergé, the garden costs a hundred fifty dirhams to enter — book ahead online in high season, or expect a forty-minute queue. The cobalt blue buildings, in shades now simply called 'Majorelle Blue,' seemed almost violent against the ink-green bamboo and mustard-yellow ceramic pots at midday. Saint Laurent once said this garden handed him a lifetime's supply of color he never fully used up. The garden's Berber Museum, filled with silver jewelry and woven textiles, reminded me that Morocco's design vocabulary actually roots in Berber geometric symbols, predating the Islamic tile traditions most visitors associate with the country.

Just across the street stands the Yves Saint Laurent Museum, designed by the French firm Studio KO, its terracotta woven-textile façade a contemporary homage to the logic of zellige tiling, reinterpreted in exposed brick. Inside, couture sketches are displayed alongside the designer's Marrakech travel notebooks; standing before a pastel sketch from the 1960s, I finally understood why this city has fed an entire century of designers — its color logic simply needs no translation.

Geometry here isn't ornament. It's a language of faith, written into every wall.

Day and Night on the Square

At half past six I returned to Jemaa el-Fna just as the sun dropped behind the Koutoubia minaret, as though the dyers' colors had been transferred wholesale into the sky. Storytellers, snake charmers, and henna women set up their stations one by one, smoke rising from grill stalls mixing with the sweetness of fresh-squeezed orange juice. A henna artist named Khadija grabbed my hand and began sketching a pattern before I'd agreed to a price — a common tactic here, and one I quickly learned to preempt with a polite wave from a distance. A full hand design took about thirty minutes and cost a hundred twenty dirhams; as she worked, she told me that this coming Friday her family would cook couscous, since 'Friday couscous' is something close to a nationwide unspoken family ritual — even the streets outside the mosque carry the same spice smell after Friday prayers. She also mentioned that if I ever visited during Ramadan, eating or drinking in public during daylight would be considered disrespectful, even for tourists, and that at the exact moment of iftar the entire square falls suddenly silent as everyone bows their head over the first date.

By eight, the halqa storytelling tradition — now recognized by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage — was in full swing. I couldn't understand the Arabic, but I could read the story's climax in the old storyteller's rising gestures, and the children listening were just as rapt as the elders. I bought a bowl of babbouche, snail soup, for five dirhams; the broth, thick with fennel and clove, sat somewhere between herbal medicine and seafood stock, strange and instantly addictive. Lamp after lamp flickered on across the square, the smoke of oil lamps mingling with charcoal and the cool green scent of mint tea. Standing in the middle of the crowd, I realized that every strand of this city's design sensibility — its architectural geometry, its market colors, the warmth of its handwork — finally converges here, in the square, living itself out as an ongoing performance each night.

On my last evening in Marrakech, I climbed once more to a rooftop café overlooking the medina, the mosque's minaret glowing faintly pink against the dusk. I thought back to what Hakim had said — that this city grew up out of the ground — and only then did I truly understand: it wasn't just about building materials. It was about how, generation after generation, this city has planted geometry, color, and craftsmanship into every single wall it owns.
