中文

訂機票之前,我已經去過京都兩次,兩次都在清水寺的人潮裡被推著走,在嵐山竹林裡舉著手機找不到一個沒有人頭的角度。第三次出發前,我告訴自己:這次不趕景點,只跟著京都人的生活時間表走。於是我把鬧鐘設在清晨五點,把地圖上那些寫著「觀光客不知道」的小字全部圈起來。五天下來,我沒有再進清水寺,卻在伏見稻荷的後山、西陣的織布聲裡、上七軒的石板路上,找到了我以為早就被商業化吃光的那個京都。

這篇筆記,寫給也厭倦了人擠人卻還是捨不得放棄京都的你。

清晨五點半,伏見稻荷山頂的寂靜

大部分遊客九點多才從JR稻荷站湧出來,擠在千本鳥居前拍那張人人都有的紅色隧道照。我學會的第一課是:伏見稻荷全年無休、24小時開放,而且真正的稻荷山其實有四公里的登山參道,一路通到山頂的「一ノ峰」。清晨五點半,我從近鐵伏見稻荷站走出來,街上只有送報的機車聲和便利商店的自動門音效。神社入口的大鳥居下沒有半個人,朱紅色的樓門在路燈與微光交界處顯得特別安靜。

穿過千本鳥居時,我幾乎是一個人走在裡面,鳥居與鳥居之間的間隙滲進來的光線隨著坡度慢慢變化,木頭的味道混著清晨潮濕的青苔味撲面而來。走到「四ツ辻」大約花了四十分鐘,這裡有片視野開闊的休息平台,可以俯瞰京都市區,清晨的空氣裡飄著炭火味——一間叫「にしむら亭」的茶屋才剛掀開布簾,老闆娘正在生火準備烤麻糬。我點了一份烤団子配熱茶,坐在木長椅上,看著京都塔在薄霧裡若隱若現,那一刻的兩百日圓,是我這趟旅行花得最值得的錢。

繼續往上走,人潮真的消失了。從四ツ辻到山頂還有將近一小時的路程,沿途會經過「眼力社」「御劍社」等小祠,狐狸石像的表情一尊比一尊詭異,苔蘚爬滿了老舊的鳥居基座,偶爾有當地人牽著狗晨跑經過,點頭致意後又消失在下一個轉彎。整趟登頂來回大約三小時,建議穿好走的鞋、帶一瓶水,山路有些路段濕滑,雨後尤其要小心。抵達山頂「一ノ峰」時剛好七點半,晨光斜斜切過鳥居的紅漆,山下傳來的鐘聲若有似無。沒有人潮、沒有導覽旗,只有風吹過鈴鐺的聲音。

京都最好的風景,常常藏在觀光團還沒睡醒的那一小時裡。

西陣,織布機聲裡的老京都

下山後我搭公車到堀川今出川,走進西陣地區。這裡沒有觀光指標,只有一整排屋齡超過百年的「町家」,格子木窗後面偶爾傳出織布機規律的「喀噠喀噠」聲——西陣織是京都最古老的織品產業,至今仍有不少家庭工坊在自家一樓織布、二樓居住。我沿著小巷亂走,在一条戻り橋附近遇到一位七十多歲的婆婆正在自家門口澆花,她見我駐足看織布機的聲音來源,主動用不太標準的英文混著日文跟我解釋:「這台機器,我先生的爺爺那輩就在用了。」她的院子裡種著紫陽花,五月底剛好開得飽滿,藍紫色的花球在灰色瓦片屋簷下顯得特別鮮豔。

再往前走幾步是晴明神社,供奉平安時代的陰陽師安倍晴明,神社不大,遊客也少,五芒星圖騰刻在鳥居、燈籠、井蓋上,細節多到讓人忍不住蹲下來一個個找。神社角落有口「晴明井」,水質清澈,當地人說喝了會帶來好運,我用隨身水壺裝了一點,冰涼帶著淡淡礦物味。從晴明神社走回今出川通,路邊有間毫不起眼的「西陣織會館」,一樓在賣布料,二樓有免費的和服秀,遊客三三兩兩,完全不像金閣寺那樣人聲鼎沸。我在那裡待了將近一小時,看著模特兒穿著不同紋樣的振袖走秀,講解員細細解說每種花紋背後的季節意涵——梅花代表早春、楓葉代表深秋——這是我第一次真正搞懂京都人為什麼那麼在意「應景」這件事。

出町柳的甜點時光:一顆豆餅的重量

中午十二點半,我走到鴨川與高野川交會處的出町柳,排隊排了大約二十分鐘,終於買到「出町ふたば」的名代豆餅。這家店開業超過一百年,店面小小的,玻璃櫃裡永遠只有那幾樣和菓子,豆餅一個220日圓,現做現賣,晚了下午常常就賣完。我拿到手時餅皮還帶著溫度,外層是帶點鹹味的紅豆與麻糬混合的皮,咬下去先是鹹,再回甘,裡面包著顆粒分明的紅豆沙,甜度控制得極克制,完全不膩。我坐在鴨川的堤防上,河水聲混著遠處小孩踩石頭過河的笑鬧聲,一口豆餅一口便利商店買的冷焙茶,那個下午我什麼都沒安排,就坐了快一小時。

吃完甜的,我又走去河原町巷子裡一間叫「喫茶ソワレ」的老派咖啡廳續攤。這家店從1948年開到現在,店內是深藍色的絨布沙發、教堂彩繪玻璃般的燈光,整個空間帶著一種昭和年代的憂鬱美感,老闆說很多京都本地的作家、畫家以前都常來這裡窩一整個下午。我點了一杯招牌的「ゼリーポンチ」果凍蘇打,600日圓左右,琥珀色的蘇打水裡浮著彩色果凍與冰淇淋球,甜得很直接,配著窗外斜射進來的午後陽光,店裡放著爵士樂,幾乎聽不到英文對話,坐在我隔壁的是兩位穿著制服的女高中生,正在小聲討論考試。那一刻我突然覺得,自己不是「遊客」,只是剛好路過這座城市生活的一個下午。

上七軒,比祇園更安靜的花街

傍晚五點,我搭公車到北野天満宮附近的上七軒。這裡是京都現存最古老的花街,比觀光客爆滿的祇園低調得多,石板路兩側是低矮的茶屋建築,紅色燈籠一盞盞亮起,幾乎沒有拿著相機追著藝妓跑的人群。我在巷口一間小茶屋外,遇見一位正要出門工作的舞妓見習生,她叫「まめ菊」(化名,店家不希望公開真名),年紀約十七、八歲,臉上妝容還沒完全上好,腳步很快,顯然趕時間。茶屋老闆娘看我一臉好奇,笑著跟我解釋:「祇園是給觀光客拍照的花街,上七軒是給京都人喝酒談生意的花街。」她說這裡的規矩比祇園更講究「一見さんお断り」——沒有熟客介紹,外人不能隨便進門喝茶,這也是為什麼上七軒始終維持著一種近乎隱形的低調。

每年二月,北野天満宮會舉辦「梅花祭」,上七軒的藝妓、舞妓會親自到神社境內的野點席上為參拜者奉茶,這是京都少數還能近距離看到舞妓工作日常、而不是純粹表演性質的場合。老闆娘說,梅花祭那天整條上七軒的茶屋幾乎全員出動,清晨就開始準備和菓子與抹茶道具,對她們來說與其說是觀光活動,更像是一年一度、感謝神明保佑生意的儀式。我沒有機會遇上梅花祭,但光是傍晚站在巷口看著燈籠一盞盞亮起、聽見木屐踩過石板路的「喀啦喀啦」聲,就已經讓我明白,京都真正迷人的不是那些被拍到爛的地標,而是這種被小心翼翼保護著、幾乎拒絕被消費的日常儀式感。

祇園是給觀光客看的花街,上七軒是京都人自己還在用的花街。

深夜先斗町,巷底那間只有六個座位的小酒館

晚上九點,我鑽進先斗町最窄的那條巷子,兩側町家屋簷幾乎要碰在一起,頭頂只露出一條細細的天空。主街上滿是遊客與招攬客人的看板,但只要拐進第二、第三條垂直的小巷,喧鬧聲立刻被吸走大半。我在一間門口只掛著暖簾、沒有任何英文招牌的小店前猶豫了很久,最後鼓起勇氣掀簾進去,裡面只有六個吧檯座位,老闆是位六十多歲的伯伯,獨自掌廚也獨自招呼客人。我用簡單日文點了他推薦的「おまかせ」,一份包含烤香魚、出汁玉子燒與炙燒鯖魚壽司的套餐,大約2800日圓,配一杯冰鎮的伏見清酒。烤香魚外皮焦脆,內裡魚肉鬆軟帶著河魚特有的微苦回甘,伯伯說魚是他每天早上去錦市場挑的。整間店安靜得只剩炭火劈啪聲與隔壁客人偶爾的低聲交談,窗外先斗町的喧鬧像是被隔在另一個世界。

走出小酒館已經快十一點,鴨川沿岸的納涼床大多已經收攤,只剩零星幾對情侶坐在河堤上。我沿著河岸慢慢走回旅館,夜風帶著河水的濕氣,遠處傳來三味線隱約的聲音,不知道是哪間茶屋還沒打烊。五天下來,我沒有再去金閣寺、沒有再排清水寺的隊,卻覺得自己終於「到過」京都,而不只是「路過」京都。這座城市最珍貴的秘境,從來不是某個藏起來的地址,而是願意早起、願意迷路、願意在巷子裡多站五分鐘的那份耐性。

ENGLISH

Before this trip, I had already been to Kyoto twice, and both times I was swept along in the crowds at Kiyomizu-dera, holding up my phone in the Arashiyama bamboo grove trying and failing to find a frame without another tourist's head in it. Before my third visit, I made myself a promise: this time, no chasing landmarks. I would follow the rhythm of how Kyoto actually lives. I set my alarm for five in the morning and circled every faded little note on my map that hinted at something locals knew and tourists didn't. Five days later, I never set foot in Kiyomizu-dera again, but on the silent summit of Fushimi Inari, in the clatter of looms in Nishijin, and along the stone paths of Kamishichiken, I found the Kyoto I thought had long since been sold out to tour buses.

This is for anyone who's grown tired of the crowds but still can't quite give up on this city.

5:30 AM, the Silence at the Summit of Fushimi Inari

Most visitors don't spill out of JR Inari Station until after nine, and by then they're packed shoulder to shoulder under the vermillion tunnel of the Senbon Torii, taking the exact same photo everyone else has taken. The first thing I learned was that Fushimi Inari is open twenty-four hours, and that the mountain behind it, Inari-yama, has a four-kilometer pilgrimage trail climbing all the way to the summit, Ichi-no-mine. At 5:30 in the morning, I stepped out of Fushimi-Inari Station on the Keihan line, and the street was empty except for a newspaper delivery scooter and the electronic chime of a convenience store door. There wasn't a single soul under the great vermillion gate at the entrance, and the shrine's main hall stood quiet in that thin blue light before dawn.

Walking through the Senbon Torii, I had the entire tunnel to myself. Light filtered through the gaps between the gates, shifting subtly with the slope, and the smell of old wood mixed with the damp scent of morning moss. It took about forty minutes to reach the Yotsutsuji junction, a wide platform with a sweeping view over the city. The air there smelled faintly of charcoal — a small teahouse called Nishimuraya had just lifted its curtain, and the owner was lighting a fire to grill mochi skewers. I sat on a wooden bench with a plate of grilled dango and hot tea, watching Kyoto Tower emerge and disappear in the mist. Those two hundred yen were the best money I spent on the entire trip.

Past Yotsutsuji, the crowds genuinely vanished. It's nearly another hour to the summit, passing smaller shrines like Ganriki-sha and Miken-sha, each fox statue's expression stranger than the last, moss climbing over the bases of weathered torii. Occasionally a local jogger passed with a dog, nodded, and disappeared around the next bend. The full round trip took about three hours; I'd recommend proper walking shoes and a bottle of water, since some sections get slick after rain. I reached Ichi-no-mine at exactly 7:30, morning light cutting sideways across the red lacquer of the gates, a temple bell somewhere below sounding almost imaginary. No crowds, no tour flags, just wind moving through hanging bells.

Kyoto's best views are usually hiding in the one hour before the tour buses wake up.

Nishijin, Where the Looms Still Clatter

After coming down the mountain, I took a bus to Horikawa-Imadegawa and walked into the Nishijin district. There are no tourist signs here, just row after row of century-old machiya townhouses, and from behind their wooden lattice windows I occasionally heard the steady clack-clack of a loom — Nishijin-ori is Kyoto's oldest textile tradition, and quite a few family workshops still weave on the ground floor and live upstairs. Wandering the alleys near the Ichijo Modoribashi bridge, I met a woman in her seventies watering plants outside her door. Noticing me lingering at the sound of her loom, she explained, in a mix of broken English and Japanese, that her husband's grandfather had been using that very machine. Her garden was full of hydrangeas, blooming heavily in late May, their blue-violet clusters vivid against the grey roof tiles.

A few steps further stands Seimei Shrine, dedicated to the Heian-era onmyoji Abe no Seimei. It's small and rarely crowded, and the five-pointed star emblem is carved into everything — the torii, the lanterns, even the manhole covers — enough detail that I found myself crouching down to spot them one by one. In a corner of the shrine grounds is Seimei-i, a well said to bring good fortune to those who drink from it; I filled my bottle and the water was cool, faintly mineral. Walking back toward Imadegawa Street, I stumbled on the unassuming Nishijin Textile Center. The ground floor sells fabric, and upstairs there's a free kimono fashion show with only a handful of visitors, nothing like the crush at Kinkaku-ji. I stayed nearly an hour watching models in furisode with different seasonal patterns — plum blossoms for early spring, maple leaves for deep autumn — while a guide explained the symbolism behind each motif. It was the first time I truly understood why Kyoto people care so deeply about matching everything to the season.

An Afternoon of Sweets in Demachiyanagi

At 12:30 that afternoon, I walked to Demachiyanagi, where the Kamo and Takano rivers meet, and queued about twenty minutes for the famous mame-mochi at Demachi Futaba. This shop has been running for over a hundred years, the storefront tiny, the glass case always holding the same handful of wagashi. Each bean mochi costs 220 yen, made fresh throughout the day, and they often sell out by mid-afternoon. Mine was still warm when I took it — the outer layer a mix of mochi and salted red beans, salty first, then sweet, giving way to a coarsely mashed red bean filling with restrained sweetness that never felt heavy. I sat on the riverbank, the sound of the water mixing with children hopping across stepping stones, alternating bites of mochi with sips of cold roasted tea from a convenience store. I had nothing else planned that afternoon and simply sat there for nearly an hour.

After the sweets, I wandered into a narrow alley off Kawaramachi to an old-school café called Kissaten Soirée. Open since 1948, it's all deep blue velvet sofas and stained-glass lighting reminiscent of a small chapel, the whole room carrying a melancholic Showa-era beauty. The owner told me that local writers and painters used to camp out here for entire afternoons. I ordered the signature jelly punch soda for around 600 yen — amber soda water topped with colorful jelly cubes and a scoop of ice cream, sweet and unapologetic. Afternoon light slanted through the window, jazz played softly, and I barely heard a word of English. Beside me, two high school girls in uniform whispered about an upcoming exam. In that moment, I didn't feel like a tourist at all — just someone who happened to be spending an afternoon inside this city's ordinary life.

Kamishichiken, the Quieter Geisha District

At five in the evening, I took a bus to Kamishichiken, near Kitano Tenmangu Shrine. This is Kyoto's oldest surviving hanamachi, or geisha district, far less crowded than Gion. Stone paths run between low teahouse buildings, red lanterns switching on one by one, with almost no crowds chasing geiko for photos. Outside a small teahouse, I ran into a young maiko-in-training on her way to work — her makeup not quite finished, her pace quick, clearly in a hurry. The teahouse owner, noticing my curiosity, laughed and explained, 'Gion is the hanamachi for tourists to photograph. Kamishichiken is the hanamachi Kyoto people still actually use.' She told me the district follows an even stricter version of the 'ichigen-san okotowari' rule than Gion — no first-time customers without an introduction from a regular — which is exactly why Kamishichiken has managed to stay almost invisible.

Every February, Kitano Tenmangu holds the Baika-sai, or plum blossom festival, during which the geiko and maiko of Kamishichiken personally serve tea to visitors at an outdoor tea ceremony on the shrine grounds. It's one of the rare occasions where you can watch these women at genuine, unstaged work rather than pure performance. The owner said that on Baika-sai day, nearly every teahouse in the district turns out its staff, preparing wagashi and tea utensils from early morning — for them, it's less a tourist event than an annual ritual of gratitude to the gods for another year of business. I never got to witness the festival myself, but standing at the mouth of that alley as the lanterns lit up one by one, listening to the clatter of wooden geta on stone, I understood that Kyoto's real magic isn't in its most-photographed landmarks — it's in these carefully guarded, almost unmarketable daily rituals.

Gion is the hanamachi built for tourists to watch. Kamishichiken is the one Kyoto still keeps for itself.

Midnight in Pontocho, a Six-Seat Bar at the End of an Alley

At nine that night, I slipped into the narrowest alley of Pontocho, its machiya eaves nearly touching overhead, leaving only a thin strip of sky visible above. The main street was full of tourists and touts, but ducking into the second or third perpendicular side-alley instantly muffled most of the noise. I hesitated a long while outside a shop marked by nothing but a plain noren curtain — no sign in English, nothing to indicate it welcomed outsiders — before finally working up the nerve to step in. Inside, there were only six counter seats, and the owner, a man in his sixties, cooked and served entirely alone. In simple Japanese, I ordered his omakase course, which came to around 2,800 yen: grilled ayu sweetfish, dashi-rolled tamagoyaki, and seared mackerel sushi, paired with a chilled glass of Fushimi sake. The ayu's skin was crisp and slightly charred, the flesh underneath soft with the faintly bitter, sweet aftertaste unique to river fish. The owner mentioned he picked the fish himself at Nishiki Market every morning. The room was silent except for the crackle of charcoal and the occasional murmur from the customer beside me, the noise of Pontocho outside sealed off as if in another world.

I stepped out of the bar just before eleven. Most of the riverside dining platforms along the Kamo had already closed for the night, leaving only a few couples scattered along the embankment. I walked slowly back to my hotel along the river, the night air carrying the damp smell of water, the faint sound of a shamisen drifting from some teahouse that hadn't yet closed. Over five days, I never returned to Kinkaku-ji, never queued for Kiyomizu-dera again, and yet I finally felt I had actually been in Kyoto, rather than merely passing through it. This city's most precious hidden places were never a secret address on a map — they were the patience to wake up early, to get a little lost, and to stand still in an alley for five more minutes than you planned to.
