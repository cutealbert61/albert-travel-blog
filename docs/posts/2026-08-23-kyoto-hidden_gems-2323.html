中文

第三次來京都,我終於承認一件事:清水寺的舞台再美,也美不過我在人群裡被推來擠去時心裡的煩躁。這次我把「必去景點」清單整個丟進垃圾桶,改成問民宿老闆娘一句話——「你自己假日都去哪裡?」她想了很久,寫給我一張紙條,上面是幾個我從沒聽過的地名。這篇日誌,就是照著這張紙條走出來的五天。

清晨五點半,往鞍馬的第一班電車

鬧鐘在五點十分響起的時候,窗外的京都還是一片墨藍色。我沒吃早餐,揣著一顆昨晚超商買的飯糰,走到出町柳站。叡山電鐵的月台上只有三個人,一個穿著登山鞋的歐吉桑,一個像是要去上早班的女高中生,還有我。六點十分,兩節車廂的小電車緩緩開動,車窗外的樹影從稀疏變成濃密,大約三十分鐘後,我在終點站「鞍馬」下車,月台上立著一尊巨大的天狗面具,鼻子有我半個手臂長。

鞍馬寺的仁王門六點半就開了,入山費三百日圓投進木箱,沒有售票員,全靠自覺。我沒有搭纜車,選擇整段徒步——這是給自己的一個小測試。石板參道濕滑,昨夜下過雨,空氣裡全是杉木和苔蘚混著雨水的味道,很像小時候外婆家後山的味道,潮濕、微腥,卻讓人一下子清醒過來。爬到由岐神社前,我聽見的不是遊客的交談聲,而是山鳥的叫聲和自己的呼吸聲,那一刻我才明白,這趟路值得五點起床。

京都最珍貴的聲音,不是祇園祭的太鼓,而是一個人走在木の根道上,只聽得見自己鞋底踩過樹根的聲音。

從鞍馬寺本殿再往上,會走進一段叫「木の根道」的山路,粗大的杉樹根盤根錯節地露出地表,像是大地的血管。這段路不好走,我摔了一跤,手掌擦破皮,但沒有一個人看到——這種尷尬又痛快的孤獨感,在京都市區裡幾乎找不到。翻過奧の院魔王殿,大約花了我兩小時二十分,終於下坡看到貴船神社的朱紅色鳥居,那一瞬間陽光正好從樹縫灑下來,把石階染成一片橘。

貴船神社的水占卜みくじ我花了兩百日圓試了一次,籤紙要放進神社前的小溪裡泡水,字才會浮現出來——我抽到「半吉」,泡水後浮出「旅立ち良し」四個字,老闆娘後來跟我說這是很少見的籤。中午時分,貴船川沿岸的川床料理店陸續開始準備,我在「貴船茶屋」坐下,點了流しそうめん(流水麵線),一份一千三百日圓,竹管裡的水從山泉直接引下來,冰得手指發麻,麵線咻一下滑過去,得用眼明手快夾住,吃法本身就是一種遊戲。整趟鞍馬貴船的來回健行加午餐,我大約花了六個小時、不到四千日圓,卻是這趟京都行裡最不後悔的一天。

西陣早餐時光:とようけ茶屋的一碗湯豆腐

隔天早上,我睡到七點半才起床,慢慢晃到上京区的晴明神社附近。西陣是京都老織品職人的聚落,清晨的巷子裡沒有店家招呼客人的吆喝,只有木造老屋捲簾拉開的聲音,和遠處織布機隱約的喀噠聲——這一帶至今仍有人家在家庭工坊裡織西陣織和服腰帶。我在「とようけ茶屋」的門口排了大概十分鐘,店還沒到九點就已經坐滿了附近上班的居民,不是觀光客。

我點了湯豆腐定食,一千兩百日圓。豆腐是店家自己每天早上現做的嵯峨豆腐,端上桌時鍋子還在小火爐上咕嘟咕嘟冒著氣泡,豆腐塊軟得幾乎要散開,用湯勺輕輕舀起,沾一點柚子醬油,入口是溫熱的豆漿香氣混著淡淡海帶昆布出汁的鮮味,完全沒有市售豆腐那種死板的粉感。配菜是一小碟切成細絲的炸豆皮和醃漬京野菜すぐき漬,酸得剛剛好,解了豆腐的油潤感。隔壁桌的阿伯用京都腔跟老闆娘閒聊昨晚的棒球賽,我聽不太懂,但那種市井的生活感,比任何一間米其林餐廳都讓我覺得「這才是京都」。

午後秘境:一乘寺詩仙堂的寂靜庭園

吃飽早餐後,我搭叡山電鐵到一乘寺駅,步行約十五分鐘的上坡路,兩旁全是住宅,幾乎看不到任何觀光指標。詩仙堂建於江戶時代,是文人石川丈山的隱居所,入場費五百日圓,午後兩點我到的時候,整個庭院只有我和一位在畫水彩的老先生。緣廊坐下,眼前是一片白砂與修剪成圓形的杜鵑花叢,竹製的「添水」(ししおどし)每隔一段時間會發出「叩」的一聲敲擊聲,那聲音本來是用來嚇走山豬的裝置,如今卻成了整座庭園最安靜的存在感——你會忍不住屏住呼吸,等待下一次的「叩」聲。

走出詩仙堂,再往上走五分鐘,是更少人知道的圓光寺,秋天楓紅時這裡的「十牛之庭」會美到讓人講不出話,但我去的時候是初夏,滿眼是層層疊疊的新綠苔蘚,踩在木地板上發出輕微的嘎吱聲,配殿裡供奉著德川家康贈與的古活字版木,寺方會員工小聲提醒我拍照不要用閃光燈,語氣溫柔卻堅定——這種近乎家規的規矩,反而讓人更珍惜眼前這片安靜。

先斗町立ち飲み:站著喝酒的深夜京都

晚上八點多,我沒有走進鴨川旁那些燈火通明的納涼床餐廳,而是拐進先斗町中段一條窄得只能容兩人並肩的小巷,推開一間叫「神馬」的立ち飲み屋的暖簾。店裡站著喝酒的客人擠得像沙丁魚罐頭,吧檯後的老闆一邊烤雞肉串一邊跟熟客打招呼,完全沒空理我這個外國人,直到我用生澀的日文點了鹽烤雞皮串和一杯生啤酒,他才抬頭對我笑了一下。

雞皮串一份三百日圓,烤得焦香酥脆,油脂滴在炭火上發出滋滋聲響,咬下去先是脆殼,接著是油潤的口感在嘴裡化開,配一口冰涼生啤酒(六百日圓),整個人瞬間放鬆下來。旁邊一位穿西裝的上班族大叔喝到微醺,主動跟我聊起天,說他每週至少來三次,「這裡的老闆記得我三十年前第一次來的樣子」。站著喝酒這件事,在京都的巷弄文化裡其實是一種平等的儀式——沒有位子高低之分,大家都站著,肩並著肩喝一杯,那種親密感是坐在餐桌前吃懷石料理絕對感受不到的。

觀光客在鴨川邊拍夕陽的時候,真正的京都夜晚,才剛在這些窄巷裡的立ち飲み屋開始。

地蔵盆:巷口的孩子與百年結界

離開的前一晚,我原本只是要抄近路走回民宿,卻在西陣一條小巷口撞見整條街掛滿了紅白相間的燈籠,地上鋪著草蓪,一群孩子圍坐著玩套圈圈,幾位阿嬤正在分裝點心。一位察覺我張望的婆婆用不太流利卻很熱情的態度招手要我過去,她告訴我這是「地蔵盆」——京都特有、其他地方幾乎看不到的地方祭典,每年八月下旬,家家戶戶會在自家巷口供奉的地藏菩薩前搭起小小的祭壇,感謝地藏菩薩一整年守護孩子平安,同時也是社區大人小孩難得聚在一起的日子。

婆婆說,她從小在這條巷子長大,地蔵盆的準備工作是輪流由巷內各戶負責,今年輪到她們家張羅點心和布置。她指著巷口那尊已經風化到看不清五官的石地藏說,「這尊比我阿嬤還老」,語氣裡帶著一種近乎家人般的親暱。孩子們排隊跟地藏尊鞠躬拜拜,才能領到一包糖果,整個過程沒有任何觀光客,連我這個誤闖進來的外國人,都被塞了一包蜜柑軟糖,還被邀請坐下來喝杯麥茶。那一刻我忽然明白,京都真正迷人的不是千年古寺的莊嚴,而是這種巷弄裡代代相傳、外人幾乎無緣得見的日常信仰。走回民宿的路上,燈籠的光暈在濕潤的石板路上拉出長長的影子,我想,這才是我這趟旅程裡最捨不得忘記的畫面。

ENGLISH

This was my third trip to Kyoto, and somewhere between the third shove in the crowd at Kiyomizu-dera and the third selfie stick to the ribs, I made a decision: forget the must-see list. Instead, I asked my guesthouse owner one question — 'Where do you go on your day off?' She thought about it for a long moment, then scribbled down a handful of place names I'd never heard of. What follows is the five days I spent chasing that scrap of paper.

5:30 AM, the First Train to Kurama

My alarm went off at 5:10, and Kyoto outside the window was still ink-blue. I skipped breakfast, grabbed a rice ball from the convenience store the night before, and walked to Demachiyanagi Station. The platform for the Eizan Electric Railway held exactly three people — a man in hiking boots, a schoolgirl heading to an early class, and me. At 6:10, a two-car train pulled out, and the scenery outside thickened from scattered trees into dense forest. Thirty minutes later I got off at the last stop, Kurama, where a massive tengu mask with a nose longer than my forearm greets arrivals on the platform.

The gate to Kurama-dera opens at 6:30. I dropped 300 yen into a wooden collection box — no ticket booth, no attendant, just an honor system. I skipped the cable car and walked the whole mountain, partly as a test for myself. The stone path was slick from last night's rain, and the air smelled of cedar and moss soaked through with water — a smell that instantly took me back to my grandmother's house in the mountains as a kid, damp and faintly green, but sharpening my senses immediately. By the time I reached Yuki Shrine, the only sounds were birdsong and my own breathing — no chatter of tourists, nothing. That was the moment I knew the 5 AM wake-up had been worth it.

The most precious sound in Kyoto isn't the drums of Gion Matsuri — it's the silence of walking alone over the exposed cedar roots of Kinone-michi, hearing only your own footsteps.

Past the main hall of Kurama-dera, the trail turns into a stretch called Kinone-michi, where massive cedar roots snake across the surface of the ground like exposed veins of the earth. It's rough walking — I tripped and scraped my palm, and not a single person saw it happen. That kind of clumsy, private solitude is nearly impossible to find anywhere in central Kyoto anymore. It took me about two hours and twenty minutes to cross Okuno-in Maō-den and descend toward the vermillion torii gates of Kifune Shrine, arriving just as sunlight broke through the trees and painted the stone steps orange.

At Kifune Shrine I tried the water fortune-telling omikuji for 200 yen — you float the blank paper strip on the small stream in front of the shrine and the characters appear as it gets wet. Mine came up 'half-lucky,' with the words 'a good journey ahead' emerging from the water — the shrine attendant told me afterward that this particular fortune is rare. By noon, the kawadoko riverside dining platforms along the Kifune River were setting up for lunch. I sat down at Kifune-chaya and ordered nagashi-somen, flowing noodles, for 1,300 yen — mountain spring water piped through a bamboo channel, cold enough to numb my fingers, with noodles sliding past so fast I had to snatch them with chopsticks. It's a meal that's also a game. The whole round trip — hiking, temple, lunch — took about six hours and cost under 4,000 yen, and it remains the day I regret the least from this entire trip.

Breakfast in Nishijin: A Bowl of Tofu at Toyouke-jaya

The next morning I slept until 7:30 and wandered slowly toward the Seimei Shrine area in Kamigyo-ku. Nishijin is Kyoto's old weaving district, and its early-morning alleys carry no hawking of shopkeepers — only the sound of wooden shutters rolling up and, somewhere behind the walls, the faint clack of a loom. Families here still weave Nishijin-ori obi sashes in home workshops, a craft passed down for generations. I waited about ten minutes outside Toyouke-jaya, a tofu restaurant that was already packed with local office workers before nine — not a tourist in sight.

I ordered the yudofu set for 1,200 yen. The tofu is made fresh every morning from Saga-style soy milk, and it arrived still simmering gently in its own small pot over a tabletop burner. Scooped out with a ladle and dipped lightly in yuzu soy sauce, it was warm, soft enough to nearly fall apart, carrying the mild sweetness of soybean milk mingled with the umami of kelp dashi — nothing like the flat, grainy tofu sold in supermarkets. On the side came a small plate of shredded fried tofu skin and pickled sugukizuke, its sharp sourness cutting perfectly through the richness of the tofu. At the next table, an older man chatted with the owner in thick Kyoto dialect about last night's baseball game — I barely understood a word, but that everyday texture of local life felt more like the 'real Kyoto' than any Michelin-starred kaiseki dinner ever could.

A Quiet Afternoon: The Hidden Gardens of Ichijoji

After breakfast, I took the Eizan line to Ichijoji Station and walked uphill for about fifteen minutes through a purely residential neighborhood with almost no tourist signage in sight. Shisen-do, built during the Edo period as the retirement retreat of the scholar Ishikawa Jozan, charges a 500-yen entrance fee. When I arrived around two in the afternoon, the entire garden held only myself and an elderly man painting watercolors. Sitting on the wooden veranda, I looked out over white raked gravel and neatly trimmed azalea bushes, punctuated every so often by the sharp 'clack' of a shishi-odori bamboo deer-scarer — originally designed to frighten off wild boar, now the loudest presence in an otherwise silent garden. You find yourself holding your breath, waiting for the next clack.

Five minutes further up the hill lies the even less-visited Enko-ji Temple. In autumn, its 'Garden of the Ten Oxen' turns into a blaze of red maple that leaves visitors speechless, but I arrived in early summer, when the garden was instead a dense layering of fresh green moss. My footsteps on the wooden floor made a faint creak, and in a side hall sat an antique wooden movable-type printing block gifted by Tokugawa Ieyasu. A temple staff member quietly but firmly reminded me not to use flash photography — the kind of gentle, almost familial house rule that made me appreciate the silence around me even more.

Standing at the Bar in Pontocho: Kyoto After Dark

By eight in the evening, instead of joining the crowds at the riverside kawadoko restaurants glowing along the Kamo River, I ducked into a narrow alley in the middle of Pontocho, barely wide enough for two people to pass, and pushed through the noren curtain of a standing bar called Shinma. Inside, customers were packed shoulder to shoulder like sardines. The owner grilled skewers behind the counter while greeting regulars, completely ignoring the foreigner in the corner — until I managed, in halting Japanese, to order salted chicken skin skewers and a draft beer, at which point he finally looked up and gave me a small smile.

The chicken skin skewers, 300 yen a plate, were grilled until crisp and charred, fat dripping onto the coals with a sizzle. The first bite cracked, then melted into rich, oily softness, washed down with a cold draft beer (600 yen) that made my whole body relax at once. Beside me, a salaryman in a slightly rumpled suit, a few drinks in, struck up a conversation and told me he'd been coming here at least three times a week for years — 'the owner still remembers what I looked like the first time I walked in, thirty years ago.' Standing to drink, in Kyoto's alleyway culture, is its own kind of equalizing ritual — no seating hierarchy, everyone shoulder to shoulder with a glass in hand, an intimacy you simply can't replicate at a formal kaiseki table.

While tourists photograph the sunset along the Kamo River, the real Kyoto night is just getting started in alleys like this one.

Jizo-bon: The Children at the Alley Shrine

On my last evening, I took a shortcut back to my guesthouse through a small Nishijin alley and stumbled straight into a street strung with red and white lanterns, straw mats laid across the pavement, children crouched in a circle playing ring-toss games, and several grandmothers dividing up sweets. One of them, noticing me staring, waved me over with warm, broken English. She explained this was Jizo-bon — a hyper-local festival almost unique to Kyoto, held every year in late August, when each household along a street builds a small shrine in front of the neighborhood's stone Jizo statue to give thanks for a year of watching over the local children, and the whole community — young and old — gathers together.

She told me she'd grown up on this very street, and that responsibility for organizing Jizo-bon rotates each year among the households — this year, hers was in charge of the snacks and decorations. Pointing to the weathered stone Jizo at the corner, its features worn nearly smooth, she said, 'That one's older than my grandmother,' with a tenderness that sounded almost like she was talking about family. The children lined up to bow before the statue before receiving a small bag of candy, and somehow, even I — a foreigner who had wandered in by accident — was handed a bag of mikan gummies and invited to sit down with a cup of barley tea. It was in that moment I understood: what makes Kyoto extraordinary isn't only the grandeur of its thousand-year-old temples, but this — a quiet, generations-old faith tucked into a backstreet, almost invisible to outsiders. Walking back to the guesthouse, the lantern light stretched long shadows across the damp stone pavement, and I knew this was the image from the whole trip I'd be least willing to forget.
