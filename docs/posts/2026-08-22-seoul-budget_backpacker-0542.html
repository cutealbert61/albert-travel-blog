晚間九點四十分,仁川機場的空橋門一打開,濕潤中帶著淡淡消毒水味的空氣撲面而來,我才真正意識到自己落地首爾了。這趟旅程沒有訂好每一天的行程,只帶了一個45公升登山背包、一顆還沒儲值的T-money卡,和一本寫滿問號的筆記本。五天四夜,預算抓在新台幣一萬二千元左右,我想用最貼近背包客的方式走一遍首爾——不是明洞百貨公司裡的首爾,而是巷弄裡冒著熱氣、飄著炒年糕辣醬味的首爾。

深夜降落:仁川機場到弘大的第一堂交通課

入境審查排了快四十分鐘,韓國海關對背包客特別謹慎,行李箱上貼滿貼紙的女生前面被多問了兩句「旅行目的」。出關後我沒有理會機場大廳那些寫著「最優匯率」的換匯亭——後來問了住宿的韓國室友才知道,那其實比市區明洞的地下換錢所匯率差了將近3%。我先在機場地下一樓的CU便利商店買了一張T-money卡,押金2,500韓元,加值了20,000韓元,店員操作熟練到我還沒反應過來卡片已經遞回手上。

接著是首班交通抉擇:機場快線AREX直達車到首爾站要9,500韓元、43分鐘;普通列車(All-stop)到弘大入口站只要4,150韓元,但要55分鐘,中途停靠十幾站。背著背包又是晚上十點,我選了普通車,理由很單純——省下的5,350韓元夠我隔天吃兩碗海帶湯。車廂裡安靜到只剩空調運轉聲,對面座位一個穿著西裝、明顯剛下班的韓國大叔全程閉眼假寐,直到廣播念到自己站名前三秒才睜眼起身,那種精準讓我印象深刻。

晚上十一點多走出弘大入口站九號出口,街上完全是另一個世界:烤肉店的鐵板煙霧混著街頭小吃攤的甜辣醬味道直衝鼻腔,音樂從地下一樓的酒吧滲出來,便利商店外擺著塑膠椅,幾個大學生模樣的年輕人坐在路邊喝著燒酒配魷魚乾,笑聲很大聲卻一點也不惹人厭。我入住的Kimchee弘大分館是韓國知名的連鎖背包客旅館,六人女生混合宿舍一晚28,000韓元,櫃檯值班的是個台灣女生打工換宿,她給我的第一個提醒是:「晚上十二點半後地鐵全部停駛,凌晨五點半才恢復,計程車那時候會加成20%,你要玩夜生活就先算好回程。」這句話我後來在弘大真的驗證了代價。

首爾的地鐵準時到近乎冷酷,但一旦錯過末班車,這座城市對背包客一點都不溫柔。

晨光中的北村:景福宮守衛交接與韓屋巷弄迷走

隔天清晨六點我就醒了,一部分是時差、一部分是不想跟滿街的韓服租借觀光客人擠人。搭3號線到安國站,2號出口出來,晨霧還沒散,北村韓屋村第一巷幾乎沒有人,只有一位阿珠媽拉著菜籃車經過,木門吱呀一聲打開又關上,那是真正有人居住的痕跡,不是佈景。我沿著北村路11街往上走,在「北村八景」的第三景轉角遇到一塊寫著韓文與英文的告示牌:「此處居民已多次反映噪音與隱私問題,請降低音量、勿拍攝民宅內部」,才明白這些看起來像明信片的巷弄,背後其實是韓屋居民長年與觀光客拉扯的日常。我把相機收起來,單純用眼睛把屋簷曲線和瓦片上的青苔記下來。

從北村走下坡到景福宮光化門大約十五分鐘,腳程加上拍照大概二十分鐘。門票是3,000韓元,如果你像我一樣在旅館附近隨便租一套一日韓服(租金15,000韓元起跳),穿著韓服可以免費入場,而且能理解為什麼滿街的年輕人願意花錢租——那身衣服走在朝鮮王朝五百年正宮的紅牆前,違和感意外地低。上午十點的守門將交代儀式(守門將交代式)準時開演,穿著傳統甲冑的守衛隊伍伴著傳統打擊樂進場,遊客瞬間從三三兩兩擠成三層人牆,我提早二十分鐘卡到光化門正前方的位置,才勉強拍到不被人頭擋住的畫面。整個儀式約二十分鐘,下午兩點還有一場,若錯過上午這場不必扼腕。

入場後我特別繞去景福宮東北角的國立民俗博物館,免費參觀,遊客比正宮少了八成,午後陽光斜斜灑進迴廊,反而是我整個上午最安靜、最能好好呼吸的十五分鐘。這裡順帶一提一個省錢秘技:每個月最後一個星期三是「文化日」,景福宮、昌德宮等古宮免費開放,若行程能配合,一天省下的門票錢夠吃一頓像樣的部隊鍋。

味覺記憶:土俗村蔘雞湯與廣藏市場的庶民滋味

從景福宮西門步行約十五分鐘,穿過紫霞門洞附近的巷弄,我找到了那間開業超過三十年、據說柯林頓訪韓時也吃過的土俗村蔘雞湯本店。中午十二點半到店門口已經排了將近二十分鐘的隊,店員用韓語跟英語交叉喊號碼牌,我點了招牌原味蔘雞湯,24,000韓元,坐在需要盤腿的暖炕座位區,膝蓋撐不到十分鐘就開始發麻,但一端上桌那股濃郁的人蔘與糯米甜香瞬間讓我忘記腳麻——整隻春雞塞滿糯米、紅棗、栗子,湯頭清澈卻厚工,雞肉燉到用湯匙一撥就骨肉分離,店家附上一小杯自釀人蔘酒,酸中帶甘,一口下肚配著雞湯的熱氣,整個人從喉嚨暖到胃裡。

傍晚六點我轉戰廣藏市場,這裡比景福宮周邊接地氣太多,空氣裡full是芝麻油煎餅的焦香和生牛肉區冰塊融化的濕氣味道。我在市場中段一間常年大排長龍、招牌寫著「元祖」的順姬빈대떡攤位坐下,點了一份綠豆煎餅,5,000韓元,現點現煎,外圈煎得金黃酥脆、裡面卻保留綠豆泥的鬆軟濕潤,老闆娘用長筷一翻鍋鏟聲清脆俐落,油煙味撲鼻卻讓人食指大動。隔壁攤是傳說中的「摸魚仔紫菜飯捲」(마약김밥),迷你一口大小,沾黃芥末醬油,一份10捲才3,000韓元,吃起來米飯偏軟帶點微酸,配上醃蘿蔔絲的爽脆,難怪韓國人暱稱它「毒品飯捲」——吃了會上癮。我坐在攤位前的塑膠矮凳上,左手邊是一群下班後直接來吃晚餐的上班族大叔,右手邊是拉著行李箱、明顯剛從機場直衝市場的日本旅客,大家共用一張油膩的塑膠桌,卻毫無違和地各自埋頭吃著眼前那份庶民美味。

在廣藏市場,沒有服務生會遞給你菜單,你得靠鼻子跟排隊的人潮找到真正好吃的那一攤。

汗水與人情:新村弼水汗蒸幕裡的韓國式親密

第三晚我做了一個省錢又想體驗文化的決定:不訂旅館,改去首爾站附近的弼水汗蒸幕(실로암 사우나)過夜。24小時入場費9,000韓元,比我原本訂的青旅還便宜近三分之二。踏進去先是女湯區,韓國汗蒸幕的裸湯文化對第一次體驗的外國人來說有點衝擊——所有人赤裸裸地坐在溫泉池邊聊天、互相搓背,完全沒有一絲不自在,反而是我這個穿著浴巾遮遮掩掩的外國人顯得格格不入。一位年約六十歲的阿珠媽主動示意要幫我搓澡(때밀이),我付了額外25,000韓元躺上塑膠鋪墊,她的力道大到我以為皮膚要被搓掉一層,搓完後皮膚卻真的滑嫩到不可思議,她邊搓邊用簡單的英文加上手勢問我一個人來韓國會不會怕,還說她兒子在美國唸書,語氣裡帶著一種混合驕傲與想念的複雜情緒,那大概就是韓國人常說的「情」(정)——一種說不清楚卻真實存在的黏著感。

換上汗蒸幕發的粉紅色棉質上衣和短褲後,我走進男女共用的休息大廳,地板燒得溫熱,有人平躺打呼、有人窩在角落追劇,牆邊小攤賣著식혜(甜米露)和水煮蛋,一份合起來3,000韓元,是深夜裡最療癒的宵夜組合。躺在木頭枕頭上的那一刻其實有點不習慣,硬邦邦的觸感讓後腦杓隱隱發脹,但看著整個大廳裡橫七豎八睡著的韓國家庭、加班到深夜的上班族、還有像我這樣貪便宜的背包客,忽然覺得這個空間本身就是首爾生活最誠實的縮影。剛好那趟旅程正逢五月初佛誕節前夕,隔天我路過鐘路的曹溪寺,滿街掛滿粉紫黃三色紙燈籠,傍晚僧侶誦經聲從寺內傳出,信眾與觀光客一起排隊免費領取材料親手做蓮花燈,寺方完全不趕人,一位年輕比丘尼還耐心糾正我摺紙的角度,那種不強迫、卻歡迎參與的態度,讓我對韓國佛教文化的印象徹底改觀。

弘大末班車前的狂歡與背包客的省錢生存法則

最後一夜我回到弘大,晚上九點街頭藝人已經把主要廣場擠得水洩不通,一個翻唱團體用吉他和手鼓演繹K-pop,圍觀的人群裡韓國高中生跟著大合唱,外國背包客舉著手機錄影,氣氛比任何演唱會都自然。我跟旅館認識的兩個日本女生跑去唱練歌房(노래방),一小時15,000韓元三人平分剛好5,000韓元一人,包廂裡的糖果色燈光跟老舊麥克風的回音效果意外地讓人上癮,一小時完全不夠唱。走出練歌房已經接近凌晨十二點十分,我們才驚覺末班地鐵是十二點半,狂奔到弘大入口站時月台上擠滿跟我們一樣掐點狂奔的年輕人,那種集體衝刺的荒謬感,現在想起來還是會笑出來。

如果真的錯過末班車,我後來學到的教訓是千萬別在出口隨便攔路邊拉客的「白牌車」,弘大周邊晚上常有司機開價一口價15,000韓元起跳看似便宜,實際上比跳表貴,而且沒有計程車執照缺乏保障;正確做法是打開Kakao T叫車,晚間雖有加成但透明可查,或乾脆在24小時的便利商店外跟朋友喝杯燒酒配辣炒年糕耗到五點半首班車。順帶一提,首爾幾乎每條巷子都有便利商店直接在店外擺桌椅,買杯裝燒酒配包裝好的下酒菜,就是韓國人所謂「一次性聚餐」的日常縮影,比任何精緻酒吧都更貼近真實生活。

最後把五天四夜的花費攤開來算:住宿平均一晚28,000韓元(含一晚汗蒸幕9,000韓元拉低平均)、三餐加點心每天約20,000到25,000韓元、地鐵與公車交通每天約8,000到10,000韓元、門票與體驗類每天約5,000韓元,整體一天生活費落在55,000到65,000韓元之間,換算新台幣大約1,300到1,500元,還沒算進機票。幾個真心的避雷提醒:別在機場換錢、Google地圖在韓國幾乎失能一定要改用Naver Map或KakaoMap、記得抓緊十二點半末班車、離境前把T-money卡餘額在便利商店花光或退款(退款會扣手續費500韓元)。背著背包走過這五天,我沒有去樂天塔看夜景,卻在汗蒸幕的木枕頭上、廣藏市場油煙裡,摸到了首爾真正的體溫。

At 9:40 p.m. the jet bridge door at Incheon opened and a wall of humid air, faintly tinged with disinfectant, hit me — that was the moment Seoul became real. I hadn't booked a single day's itinerary. Just a 45-liter backpack, a T-money card I hadn't even topped up yet, and a notebook full of question marks. Five days, four nights, on a budget of roughly USD 400. I wanted to walk through Seoul the way a backpacker actually would — not the department-store Seoul of Myeongdong, but the Seoul that smells of gochujang steam rising from street carts at midnight.

Landing After Dark: My First Transit Lesson from Incheon to Hongdae

Immigration took nearly forty minutes; Korean customs officers were noticeably thorough, and the woman ahead of me — her suitcase plastered with travel stickers — got extra questions about her 'purpose of visit.' Once through, I ignored the currency exchange kiosks in the arrival hall advertising 'best rates.' A Korean roommate at my hostel later told me those airport rates run about 3% worse than the underground money changers in Myeongdong. Instead I bought a T-money card at the basement CU convenience store — a 2,500 won deposit, topped up with 20,000 won — and the cashier processed it so fast I barely registered the card was back in my hand.

Then came the first real decision: the AREX express train to Seoul Station costs 9,500 won and takes 43 minutes; the all-stop local train to Hongik University Station costs only 4,150 won but takes 55 minutes, stopping at over a dozen stations. Backpack on, ten at night, I took the slow train — the logic being simple: the 5,350 won saved would buy two bowls of seaweed soup the next day. The carriage was silent except for the hum of air conditioning. A man in a suit across from me, clearly just off work, dozed with his eyes shut the entire ride, only snapping awake three seconds before his stop was announced. That precision stuck with me.

Walking out of Exit 9 at Hongik University Station past 11 p.m. felt like stepping into another world entirely — the smoke off barbecue grills mixed with the sweet-spicy tang of street tteokbokki, music seeping up from basement bars, plastic chairs set up right outside convenience stores where university students drank soju with dried squid, laughing loudly without a hint of self-consciousness. I checked into Kimchee Hongdae, a well-known backpacker hostel chain, 28,000 won a night for a six-bed mixed dorm. The front desk was staffed by a Taiwanese girl doing a work exchange, and her first warning stuck with me: 'Subways stop completely after 12:30 a.m. and don't resume until 5:30. Taxis add a 20% night surcharge, so plan your way home before you plan your night.' I would pay for ignoring that advice later.

Seoul's subway runs with an almost merciless precision — but miss the last train, and this city shows backpackers no mercy at all.

Morning Light in Bukchon: The Royal Guard and the Hanok Alleys

I woke at six the next morning, partly from jet lag, partly to beat the crowds of hanbok-clad tourists. Line 3 to Anguk Station, Exit 2, and the morning fog hadn't even lifted yet. The first alley of Bukchon Hanok Village was nearly empty except for an ajumma pulling a shopping cart, a wooden gate creaking open and shut — proof that people actually live here, not a stage set. I walked up Bukchon-ro 11-gil to the third of the famous 'Bukchon Eight Views' and found a sign in Korean and English: residents had repeatedly complained about noise and privacy, please keep quiet and don't photograph inside homes. It reframed the whole postcard-perfect alley — behind the pretty rooflines is a decades-long tension between residents and tourists. I put my camera away and just traced the curve of the eaves and the moss on the tiles with my eyes.

From Bukchon it's a fifteen-minute walk downhill to Gwanghwamun Gate at Gyeongbokgung — closer to twenty with photo stops. Admission is 3,000 won, but wearing a rented hanbok (starting around 15,000 won a day from any of the countless shops nearby) gets you in free, and after seeing hundreds of young people happily paying for the privilege, I understood why — walking those red palace walls in traditional dress feels far less awkward than expected. The 10 a.m. Sumun-jang changing-of-the-guard ceremony started right on schedule, guards in traditional armor marching in to the beat of ceremonial drums, and the scattered crowd instantly compressed into three rows deep. I'd staked out a spot directly in front of Gwanghwamun twenty minutes early and still barely got a clear shot. The whole thing runs about twenty minutes, with a second show at 2 p.m. if you miss the morning one.

Inside, I detoured to the National Folk Museum in the palace's northeast corner — free admission, and eighty percent fewer tourists than the main grounds. Afternoon light slanted through the covered walkway, giving me the quietest fifteen minutes of breathing room all morning. One genuine money-saving tip: the last Wednesday of every month is 'Culture Day,' when Gyeongbokgung, Changdeokgung, and other royal palaces open free of charge. Time it right, and the ticket money saved covers a proper bowl of budae-jjigae for dinner.

Taste Memory: Ginseng Chicken Soup and the Honest Flavors of Gwangjang Market

A fifteen-minute walk west from Gyeongbokgung, through the alleys near Jahamun Tunnel, brought me to Tosokchon Samgyetang — the thirty-year-old institution reportedly visited by Bill Clinton. By 12:30 p.m. the line already stretched twenty minutes deep, staff calling out order numbers in a rapid mix of Korean and English. I ordered the classic ginseng chicken soup, 24,000 won, and sat cross-legged on the heated ondol floor seating — my knees went numb within ten minutes, but the moment the bowl arrived, thick with the sweet aroma of ginseng and glutinous rice, I forgot about my legs entirely. A whole young chicken stuffed with sticky rice, jujubes, and chestnuts sat in a broth so clear yet so rich that the meat fell off the bone with a single push of my spoon. A small shot of house-made ginseng liquor came on the side — tart, faintly sweet — and drinking it down after a mouthful of hot broth sent warmth from my throat straight to my stomach.

By six that evening I'd moved on to Gwangjang Market, which feels far more grounded than the palace district — the air thick with sesame oil frying pancakes and the damp smell of melting ice from the raw beef stalls. I sat down at Sunhee's Bindaetteok, a stall with a permanent queue and a sign proudly declaring 'original,' and ordered a mung bean pancake for 5,000 won. Fried to order, the edges crisped golden while the center stayed soft and mashy, and the sharp clatter of the ajumma's spatula against the pan only sharpened my appetite through the oily smoke. Next door was the legendary mayak gimbap — 'drug' kimbap — tiny bite-sized rolls dipped in mustard-soy sauce, ten pieces for 3,000 won, the rice slightly soft with a hint of tang, the pickled radish adding a satisfying crunch. It's easy to see why Koreans nicknamed it 'addictive.' Sitting on a low plastic stool, I had off-duty office workers on one side and Japanese tourists who'd clearly come straight from the airport, suitcases and all, on the other — everyone crowded around the same greasy plastic table, heads down, equally absorbed in the same humble feast.

At Gwangjang Market, no one hands you a menu — you find the good stuff by following your nose and the length of the queue.

Sweat and Intimacy: A Night at a Sinchon Jjimjilbang

On my third night I made a decision that was both frugal and culturally curious: skip the hostel and spend the night at Siloam Sauna near Seoul Station. Twenty-four-hour entry cost 9,000 won, roughly a third of what I'd originally booked for a hostel bed. Inside, the women's bathing floor came first, and the nudity culture at Korean jjimjilbangs is genuinely a jolt for first-timers — everyone sits naked around the hot pools chatting, scrubbing each other's backs, with zero self-consciousness, while I, wrapped awkwardly in a towel, was clearly the odd one out. An ajumma in her sixties gestured that she'd scrub my back for an extra 25,000 won. I lay down on the plastic mat and her strength was such that I genuinely thought a layer of skin was coming off — but afterward my skin felt unbelievably smooth. While scrubbing, she asked in simple English and gestures if I was scared traveling alone, mentioning her son was studying in America, her voice carrying a tangled mix of pride and longing — probably the closest thing to what Koreans call jeong (정), that hard-to-translate but very real sense of attachment.

Changed into the sauna's pink cotton top and shorts, I wandered into the shared common hall where the floor was warmly heated. Some people lay flat and snored, others huddled in corners streaming dramas, and a small stall along the wall sold sikhye (sweet rice drink) and boiled eggs, 3,000 won for both — the perfect late-night comfort combo. Lying down on the wooden pillow felt strange at first, the hard edge pressing uncomfortably against the back of my skull, but watching entire Korean families sprawled across the floor, office workers who'd worked late into the night, and cheapskate backpackers like me, I realized this room was probably the most honest cross-section of Seoul life I'd see all trip. As it happened, the visit fell right before Buddha's Birthday in early May, and the next day I passed Jogyesa Temple on Jongno, its streets strung wall to wall with pink, purple, and yellow paper lanterns. Chanting drifted out from the temple at dusk, and both worshippers and tourists lined up for free materials to make their own lotus lanterns — no one rushed anyone along, and a young nun patiently corrected the angle of my folded paper. That unforced, welcoming attitude completely reshaped how I saw Korean Buddhist culture.

One Last Night in Hongdae, and a Backpacker's Survival Math

My final night I went back to Hongdae. By 9 p.m. the main plaza was already packed for street performances — a cover band playing K-pop hits on guitar and hand drums while Korean high schoolers sang along and foreign backpackers filmed on their phones, the whole scene more genuine than any concert I've paid for. I ended up in a noraebang with two Japanese girls I'd met at the hostel — 15,000 won an hour, split three ways to 5,000 won each — and the candy-colored lighting plus the tinny echo on the old microphones was somehow addictive; an hour flew by far too fast. We stumbled out around 12:10 a.m. and realized, with genuine panic, that the last train left at 12:30. Sprinting to Hongik University Station, the platform was already jammed with young people doing exactly the same last-minute dash — a collective absurdity I still laugh about now.

If you do miss the last train, the lesson I learned the hard way is: never flag down the unlicensed drivers who linger outside Hongdae exits at night offering flat rates from 15,000 won — they look cheap but often cost more than the metered fare, and without a taxi license there's zero recourse if something goes wrong. The right move is to book through the Kakao T app; night surcharges apply but the pricing is transparent, or simply wait it out with friends over a soju outside a 24-hour convenience store until the first train at 5:30. Nearly every alley in Seoul has convenience stores with tables set up right on the sidewalk — grabbing a bottled soju and packaged side dishes for what Koreans call a 'one-shot gathering' is a slice of daily life far more real than any polished cocktail bar.

Tallying up five days and four nights: lodging averaged 28,000 won a night (one jjimjilbang stay at 9,000 won pulled the average down), meals plus snacks ran 20,000–25,000 won a day, transit 8,000–10,000 won a day, and admissions or experiences around 5,000 won a day — total daily spend landed between 55,000 and 65,000 won, or roughly USD 40–48, not counting flights. A few final warnings worth remembering: never exchange money at the airport; Google Maps is basically useless in Korea, switch to Naver Map or KakaoMap immediately; keep the 12:30 last-train deadline burned into your brain; and spend down or refund your T-money balance before leaving (refunds carry a 500 won fee). I never made it up Lotte Tower for the skyline view on this trip. But lying on a wooden pillow in a jjimjilbang, and standing in the oily smoke of Gwangjang Market, I think I got closer to Seoul's actual body heat than any observation deck could offer.
