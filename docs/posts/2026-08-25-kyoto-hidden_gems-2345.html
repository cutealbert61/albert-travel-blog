中文

這是我第四次來京都,前三次分別交給了清水寺的人潮、伏見稻荷的紅色鳥居隧道,還有嵐山竹林裡此起彼落的自拍快門聲。這一次出發前,我在京都住了六年的朋友只跟我說了一句話:「妳要看的京都,不在觀光巴士的路線上。」於是我把地圖上那些紅色星星全部劃掉,換上一條她手寫在便條紙上的路線——從出町柳搭上叡山電鐵開始,一路往北,往山裡去。

清晨五點四十分:出町柳月台上,只有貓與我

五點四十分,京都的天色還是一種介於藍與灰之間的顏色,鴨川的水聲比白天清晰得多。出町柳車站幾乎沒有人,只有一隻虎斑貓蹲在售票機旁邊,尾巴一甩一甩地看著我研究時刻表。叡山電鐵的第一班往鞍馬方向的列車是六點零五分發車,單程票價420日圓,我另外買了一張「鞍馬・貴船日歸乘車券」共1700日圓,含來回車資與貴船口到貴船神社的巴士。車廂裡幾乎是空的,只有兩位揹著登山杖的婆婆,聊著今天要去撿栗子。列車一過寶ヶ池,窗外就開始出現成片的杉樹林,晨霧還沒散,車廂燈光映在玻璃上,整節車廂安靜得只聽得到鐵軌摩擦的聲音。

京都最動人的瞬間,往往發生在觀光巴士還沒發動引擎的時候。

鞍馬古道健行到貴船:一段沒有觀光巴士的山林修行

六點三十五分抵達鞍馬車站,月台上立著一尊巨大的天狗面具,紅臉長鼻子在晨光裡有點滑稽又有點威嚴。鞍馬寺的仁王門六點就開了,愛山費300日圓投進木箱,沒有人在收票,完全憑良心。我沒搭纜車,選擇走「九十九折參道」,沿途經過由岐神社的巨杉與義經公背比石,大約四十分鐘爬到本殿金堂。這段路石階濕滑,清晨露水未乾,建議穿抓地力好的鞋子,我看到一位當地阿伯直接拄著撿來的樹枝當登山杖。過了本殿之後,真正的重頭戲是「木の根道」——一段樹根裸露盤結在地表、像血管一樣蔓延的原始山徑,傳說是源義經幼年修行躍動輕功的地方。這段路大概二十分鐘,林木蔽日,只有鳥叫聲和我自己的呼吸聲,完全沒有遇到任何一個觀光團。走到西門出口下山,接上貴船川沿岸的石板參道,大概八點半抵達貴船神社本宮,朱紅色的春日燈籠一路排開,因為時間還早,整條參道只有掃地的神職人員和我,水占卜籤詩200日圓,把白紙放進御神水裡才會顯字,我抽到的是「半吉」,籤紙上的字慢慢浮現的瞬間,老實說比抽到大吉還讓我起雞皮疙瘩。整趟鞍馬到貴船的健行,加上參拜時間大約花了兩個半小時,體力消耗中等,但因為完全沒有排隊等候,反而是我在京都少數感覺「時間是自己的」的時刻。

下山後在貴船口車站前的小店買了一份烤麻糬配味噌,坐在木頭長椅上邊吃邊等九點四十分的電車回一乗寺方向,溪水聲混著炭火香,那一刻我突然明白朋友說的「不在觀光巴士路線上的京都」是什麼意思。

一乗寺中谷的葛切:午後的療癒儀式

搭電車回到一乗寺,已經接近正午,肚子開始咕嚕咕嚕叫。一乗寺這一帶其實是京都著名的拉麵激戰區,但我沒有加入排隊的行列,而是拐進巷子裡那間開業超過六十年的和菓子老鋪「一乗寺中谷」,地址就在京都市左京区一乗寺花ノ木町5番地,外觀是低調的木造町家,拉門上掛著暖簾,完全不像會出現在旅遊書封面的樣子。我點了店裡的招牌「特製くずきり」850日圓,店員送上來時是兩層漆器盒,上層裝著透明如水晶的葛切條,下層是冰塊鎮著保持冰涼,旁邊附上一小壺沖繩產黑糖蜜。用筷子夾起葛切條沾一點黑蜜送進嘴裡,口感先是滑溜彈牙,接著葛粉特有的微微顆粒感在舌尖化開,黑糖蜜的焦香跟一絲鹹味中和了甜度,吃完整份也不會覺得膩。店內的老闆娘看我一個人坐在窗邊發呆,主動用京都腔跟我聊了幾句,說這道甜點的葛粉是每天早上現點現做,放超過兩小時口感就會變硬,所以完全不接受外帶。窗外是一乗寺住宅區安靜的巷弄,偶爾有腳踏車經過的鈴聲,那一碗葛切吃下肚,整個人從腳底涼到心裡,比任何冰品都更有效地把京都盆地悶熱的暑氣壓下去。

真正的京都美味,往往藏在沒有招牌翻譯成英文的那扇木格拉門後面。

鷹峯光悦寺與紫野街巷:紙屋川畔的隱士庭園

下午三點,我搭公車轉往京都西北方的鷹峯地區,這裡海拔稍高,是江戶初期藝術家本阿彌光悅獲德川家康賜地隱居之處,遊客稀少到公車上只剩我一個外國臉孔。光悦寺拜觀料400日圓,寺內最著名的是那道以竹子編成、線條低垂如牛背的「光悦垣」,又稱臥牛垣,秋天楓紅時節襯著遠方的鷹峯三山,構圖美得像一幅未乾的水墨畫,但因為地處偏遠,平日下午幾乎沒有旅行團會安排到這裡。我沿著細細的石徑走到寺後,能俯瞰整片紙屋川流域的谷地,風裡帶著杉木與泥土混合的氣味。從鷹峯往南走約二十分鐘下坡路,就進入紫野的住宅巷弄,這一帶是今宮神社的門前町,我在参道兩側找到兩家對門競爭超過三百年的烤麻糬老店「一和」與「かざりや」,各點了一份あぶり餅500日圓,炭火現烤的小塊麻糬串在竹籤上,外層沾滿白味噌甜醬,咬下去外皮微焦帶脆,內裡還保留麻糬的軟糯,老闆邊烤邊跟熟客閒話家常,完全是京都人日常生活的一景,而不是為遊客表演的橋段。

上七軒黃昏:天神市與老茶屋裡的花街日常

傍晚五點多,天色開始轉橙,我走到北野天満宮旁邊的上七軒——京都現存最古老的花街,比祇園的歷史還要早了將近百年。因為我運氣好碰上每月25日的「天神市」緣日,窄窄的石板路兩側擺滿了賣古著和服、舊陶器與烤糰子的攤販,空氣裡混著炭火燒烤的醬油香和線香的味道。街角一家有著格子窗與掛燈籠的茶屋門口,一位年紀約莫五十多歲、和服打扮的女將正在灑水清潔門前的石階,這是京都花街「打ち水」的傳統,象徵驅暑迎客,也帶有淨化門面、迎接晚上客人的儀式意味。我站在旁邊拍照時被她發現,她沒有生氣,反而笑著跟我解釋,晚上七點左右會有還在受訓中的「仕込みさん」出來買晚餐,因為还没有正式出道成為舞妓,不能被拍照,如果看到年輕女孩快步經過、低頭避開鏡頭,請務必收起手機。果然不到十分鐘,一位穿著便服、頭髮還沒盤起的年輕女孩快步從巷子另一頭走過,街上幾位熟門熟路的當地攝影愛好者都自動放下相機,沒有人追拍,那種心照不宣的默契,比任何禁止拍照的告示牌都更有說服力。夜色徹底降臨後,上七軒歌舞練場前的紅燈籠一盞盞亮起,我在一家不到十個座位的老式居酒屋坐下,點了一份加茂茄子田樂味噌燒650日圓和一合伏見產的純米酒700日圓,老闆是土生土長的西陣人,聊起小時候放學會沿著這條巷子跑去天満宮抓蟲子,如今巷子裡的茶屋少了一半,但天神市依然是街坊鄰居每個月固定報到的日子。走出居酒屋時已近晚上九點,回頭望向那條窄巷,紅燈籠的光暈在濕潤的石板路上暈開,那一瞬間我確信,這才是我這趟旅程裡,真正屬於京都的一張明信片。

ENGLISH

This was my fourth trip to Kyoto. The first three had been swallowed whole by the crowds at Kiyomizu-dera, the endless red torii tunnel of Fushimi Inari, and the constant click of selfie shutters in the Arashiyama bamboo grove. This time, before I even landed, a friend who has lived in Kyoto for six years gave me one piece of advice: 'The Kyoto you actually want to see isn't on any bus tour route.' So I crossed out every red star on my map and replaced them with a route she scribbled on a sticky note — starting at Demachiyanagi Station on the Eizan Electric Railway, heading north, straight into the mountains.

5:40 AM: Alone on the Demachiyanagi Platform, Except for a Cat

At 5:40 in the morning, Kyoto's sky sits in that strange color between blue and gray, and the sound of the Kamo River is far clearer than it ever is by day. Demachiyanagi Station was nearly empty — just a tabby cat crouched beside the ticket machine, flicking its tail as it watched me study the timetable. The first Eizan train toward Kurama departs at 6:05 AM; a one-way ticket costs 420 yen, though I bought the 'Kurama-Kifune Day Pass' for 1,700 yen, which covers the round-trip fare plus the local bus from Kifune-guchi to Kifune Shrine. The carriage was nearly empty except for two elderly women with hiking poles, chatting about gathering chestnuts. Past Takaragaike, cedar forests began crowding the windows, morning fog still clinging to the branches, the carriage lights reflecting faintly on the glass. The only sound was the friction of the rails.

Kyoto's most moving moments almost always happen before the tour buses have even started their engines.

Hiking from Kurama to Kifune: A Mountain Pilgrimage With No Tour Buses in Sight

I reached Kurama Station at 6:35, where a massive tengu mask stares down from the platform, its red face and long nose somehow both comical and imposing. Kurama-dera's gate opens at 6:00 AM, and the 300-yen entry fee goes into an unattended wooden box — entirely on the honor system. I skipped the cable car and walked the switchback approach path instead, passing the giant cedar at Yuki Shrine and the stone where Yoshitsune supposedly measured his height as a boy, reaching the main hall in about forty minutes. The stone steps were slick with morning dew, so grippy shoes matter here; I watched one elderly local simply use a fallen branch as a walking stick. Past the main hall comes the real highlight: the Kinone-michi, or 'Root Path,' a stretch where exposed tree roots ripple across the ground like veins, said to be where the young Yoshitsune trained his legendary agility. This section takes about twenty minutes, canopy overhead blocking most of the light, with nothing but birdsong and my own breathing for company — not a single tour group in sight. Descending through the western gate, the trail connects to a stone-paved path along the Kifune River, and by 8:30 AM I reached Kifune Shrine's main hall, its bright vermilion spring lanterns lined up in a row. Because it was still early, the approach was empty except for a shrine attendant sweeping the grounds. I tried the water fortune-telling slips for 200 yen — the paper only reveals its text once dipped in the sacred water — and watching the characters slowly bloom onto the blank page gave me more chills than pulling a top-tier fortune ever could. The full hike from Kurama to Kifune, including time spent at both shrines, took roughly two and a half hours — moderately demanding, but because there was zero waiting in line anywhere, it was one of the rare moments in Kyoto where time genuinely felt like my own.

After descending, I bought grilled mochi with miso glaze from a small shop by Kifune-guchi Station and sat on a wooden bench waiting for the 9:40 train back toward Ichijoji, the sound of the stream mixing with the smell of charcoal smoke. That was the moment I finally understood what my friend meant by 'the Kyoto that isn't on any tour bus route.'

Kuzukiri at Ichijoji Nakatani: An Afternoon Ritual of Cooling Down

By the time I got back to Ichijoji it was nearly noon, my stomach growling. The neighborhood is famous as a ramen battleground, but instead of joining any queue, I ducked into a side alley to find Ichijoji Nakatani, a wagashi shop that's been open for more than sixty years, tucked at Hananoki-cho 5, Ichijoji, Sakyo Ward. Its low wooden facade and hanging noren curtain make it look nothing like something that would end up on a travel magazine cover. I ordered their signature 'Tokusei Kuzukiri' for 850 yen. It arrived in a two-tiered lacquer box: the top layer held translucent, crystal-clear kuzu starch noodles, the bottom packed with ice to keep them chilled, alongside a small pitcher of Okinawan brown sugar syrup. Dipping a strand in the syrup with chopsticks, the texture was slippery and springy at first bite, followed by the faint graininess of pure kudzu starch dissolving on the tongue — the syrup's smoky sweetness balanced by a whisper of salt, so the whole dish never felt cloying. The proprietress noticed me sitting alone by the window and struck up a conversation in a soft Kyoto dialect, explaining that the kuzu starch is mixed fresh every morning and turns hard within two hours, which is why they refuse all takeout orders no matter how much tourists ask. Outside the window, the residential lanes of Ichijoji sat quiet, occasionally punctuated by a passing bicycle bell. By the time I finished the box, my whole body had cooled from the inside out — far more effective than any ice cream at cutting through the thick, humid heat of the Kyoto basin.

The real flavors of Kyoto are usually hiding behind a wooden lattice door with no English sign out front.

Koetsu-ji in Takagamine and the Backstreets of Murasakino: A Hermit's Garden by the Kamiya River

At 3 PM I caught a bus northwest to Takagamine, a hillier district settled by the Edo-era artist Hon'ami Koetsu after Tokugawa Ieyasu granted him the land as a retreat. Visitors were so scarce that I was the only non-Japanese face on the entire bus. Entry to Koetsu-ji costs 400 yen, and the temple's most famous feature is the Koetsu Fence — bamboo woven into a low, sloping line resembling a resting ox's back, also called the 'Reclining Ox Fence.' Against the distant silhouette of the three Takagamine peaks in autumn color, it looks like an ink painting that hasn't quite dried. Because the location is so out of the way, tour groups almost never schedule an afternoon stop here. I walked a narrow stone path behind the main hall to a spot overlooking the whole Kamiya River valley, the air thick with the mingled scent of cedar and damp earth. From Takagamine, a twenty-minute walk downhill leads into the residential lanes of Murasakino, the old approach to Imamiya Shrine. There, facing each other across the same stone path, sit two grilled-mochi shops that have been rivals for over three hundred years: Ichiwa and Kazariya. I ordered a plate of aburi-mochi from each for 500 yen apiece — small skewered mochi grilled over charcoal and coated in sweet white miso sauce, the outside faintly charred and crisp, the inside still soft and chewy. The shopkeepers grilled away while chatting with regulars about neighborhood gossip, a completely unstaged slice of everyday Kyoto life rather than any kind of performance for visitors.

Dusk in Kamishichiken: Tenjin Market and the Daily Rhythms of a Geisha District

By just past 5 PM the sky had turned orange, and I walked to Kamishichiken beside Kitano Tenmangu Shrine — the oldest surviving geisha district in Kyoto, predating even Gion by nearly a century. I happened to arrive on the 25th of the month, the day of the Tenjin Market, so the narrow stone lane was lined with stalls selling vintage kimono, old ceramics, and grilled dango, the air a mix of grilled soy sauce and burning incense. Outside a lattice-windowed teahouse strung with lanterns, a woman in kimono, likely in her fifties, was sprinkling water over the stone steps out front — the traditional 'uchimizu' ritual of the geisha district, meant to cool the summer air and symbolically purify the entrance before the evening's guests arrive. She noticed me photographing her and, rather than objecting, laughed and explained that around 7 PM a young trainee not yet a full geiko — called a shikomi-san — would come out to buy dinner, and because she hasn't formally debuted, photographing her is considered off-limits; if I saw a young woman hurry past with her head down avoiding the camera, I should simply lower my phone. Sure enough, within ten minutes a young woman in plain clothes, her hair not yet styled up, hurried through the alley, and every local photographer nearby lowered their cameras automatically — an unspoken understanding far more persuasive than any 'no photography' sign could ever be. Once night properly fell, the red lanterns outside the Kamishichiken Kaburenjo theater lit up one by one, and I settled into a tiny izakaya with fewer than ten seats, ordering a plate of grilled Kamo eggplant with miso for 650 yen and a small carafe of Fushimi junmai sake for 700 yen. The owner, a lifelong resident of the Nishijin weaving district, told me stories of running through this same alley as a kid to chase insects near the shrine after school — half the teahouses of his childhood are gone now, but the Tenjin Market remains a fixture the whole neighborhood still shows up for, every single month. Walking out just before 9 PM, I turned back to see the red lanterns bleeding soft light onto the damp stone lane, and in that instant I was certain — this, finally, was the one postcard from this trip that truly belonged to Kyoto.
