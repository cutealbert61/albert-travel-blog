中文

行前我跟京都住了十年的朋友美咲說,我這次不想再去清水寺人擠人拍同一張照片了。她笑著回我一句話,我到現在都還記得:「京都人自己也不去清水寺的,我們都是凌晨去,或者根本不去。」這句話成了我這趟旅程的路標。接下來六天,我把地圖上那些寫著滿滿五顆星、永遠在排隊的地方全部劃掉,換上美咲用手機語音訊息一個一個告訴我的地名,有些連Google地圖都標錯位置。

清晨五點半,稻荷山頂只有狐狸與我

第一天,我把鬧鐘設在清晨五點。京都的旅館房間隔音不太好,窗外還能聽見送報機車的引擎聲從巷口遠遠傳來,混著一種說不出的、屬於老城市清晨特有的潮濕木頭味。五點四十分我走出旅館,搭上第一班JR奈良線,從京都站到稻荷站只要五分鐘車程,票價一百五十日圓,車廂裡只有三個人,其中兩個看起來是要去上班的當地人。

走出車站,伏見稻荷大社的第一座鳥居就在眼前,但這時候整條參道空空蕩蕩,連平常擺攤賣稻荷壽司的小販都還沒出來。我踩過石板路往上走,千本鳥居朱紅色的木柱在晨光裡幾乎是半透明的,陽光斜斜切進鳥居縫隙,空氣裡是木頭經年累月被摸過的那種微微油亮的氣味,還有一點點山裡濕苔蘚的味道。往上走大約四十分鐘會到「四つ辻」這個岔路口,這裡才是關鍵——九成遊客走到千本鳥居拍完照就折返了,只有繼續往上爬的人才看得到真正的稻荷山。

從四つ辻繼續往上,路變窄、鳥居變舊、階梯也變得不太規則,偶爾要側身才能通過。大概再走三十分鐘,我在一間叫「仁志むら亭」的小茶屋前停下,老闆娘已經在生炭火,賣的是現烤麻糬和自家釀的甘酒,一份三百日圓,我坐在店前唯一一張長木凳上,喝著溫溫的甘酒,看山下京都盆地被晨霧蓋住一半,遠處還能看到京都塔像根細針立在霧裡。老闆娘用帶著京都腔的日文跟我說,她媽媽在這裡賣了四十年,以前山上根本沒有觀光客,都是附近居民清晨上來拜拜順便運動。整趟稻荷山一周大約需要兩到三小時,全程幾乎不需要花錢,只有這杯甘酒和後來在山頂熊鷹社附近買的一支稻荷壽司串(兩百日圓),但那種安靜,是花多少錢都買不到的。

京都真正的樣子,從來不在中午十二點的清水坂上,而在沒有人想早起的那個時間點裡。

一乘寺深處:詩仙堂與曼殊院之間的無人小徑

同一天下午兩點,我從出町柳站搭上叡山電鐵,這條被京都人暱稱為「叡電」的小火車只有兩節車廂,車窗是老式的木框,晃啊晃五分鐘就到了一乘寺站,票價兩百六十日圓。一出站,完全看不到任何觀光客該有的樣子——路邊是尋常的住宅、腳踏車行、還有掛著暖簾的乾洗店,我照著美咲傳來的手繪路線圖,沿著白川通往東走了大約十五分鐘的緩坡,兩旁開始出現竹林和石牆,空氣忽然安靜下來,只剩自己的腳步聲和遠處若有似無的水聲。

詩仙堂建於江戶時期,是隱士石川丈山晚年隱居之處,入場費七百日圓,入口窄小得容易錯過。脫鞋走進屋內,踩在磨得發亮的木地板上,涼意從腳底竄上來,庭園裡那座鹿威し(添水)每隔一段時間就「咚」一聲敲在石頭上,那個聲音在寂靜裡格外清晰,我坐在緣側整整發呆了二十分鐘,身邊只有兩三個看起來像是附近退休老人的訪客,安靜地坐著喝自己帶來的茶。從詩仙堂走出來,再往北走十分鐘就是曼殊院門跡,門票六百日圓,這裡的枯山水庭園在傍晚的斜光裡,白砂被耙成的紋路投出長長的陰影,整個空間安靜得能聽到自己的呼吸聲。離開前我繞去一旁幾乎沒有指標的鷺森神社,免費參觀,神社被巨大的樹林包圍,連本地人來拜拜都要走過一段沒有鋪石板的泥土小徑,我在那裡遇到一位遛狗的阿伯,他說這一帶的居民都叫這裡「後山的神社」,很多京都人自己都沒進去過。

巷弄裡的真滋味

傍晚六點,肚子開始咕嚕叫,我沿著一乘寺站附近那條被京都大學學生稱為「拉麵激戰區」的白川通小巷走,兩側藏著至少五六間不起眼的拉麵店,招牌小到不注意就會錯過。我選了「ラーメン高安」,木頭拉門一推開,濃厚豚骨醬油香氣整個撲過來,店裡只有吧檯八個位子,老闆一個人顧爐火。我點了招牌的中華そば(七百五十日圓),湯頭濃到表面浮著一層油光,叉燒厚實帶點焦邊,麵條是偏硬的中細直麵,吸飽湯汁後咬下去還有彈性。隔壁坐著兩個明顯是京大學生的年輕人,邊喝湯邊聊考試,對他們來說這只是日常的一碗晚餐,沒有人在意這碗麵在旅遊網站上有沒有星星。

隔天上午我特地繞去出町柳的「出町ふたば」,這間創業超過百年的和菓子店,清晨九點開門前就已經有在地歐巴桑排隊,隊伍裡幾乎聽不到外語。我排了大約二十分鐘,買了招牌的豆餅(まめ餅),一顆兩百三十日圓,外皮是帶著微鹹紅豌豆粒的麻糬,咬下去先是鹹味,接著紅豆餡的甜慢慢在舌尖散開,質地軟糯但不黏牙,老闆包的時候手法俐落到幾乎沒看清楚動作,包好直接遞給我,還提醒我「今天做的,傍晚前吃完最好吃」。我站在店門口的鴨川堤防邊,一邊吃一邊看晨跑的當地人經過,河水聲、腳踏車鈴聲、還有麻糬入口的溫熱口感,構成了我這趟旅程最真實的一段記憶。

町內的地藏盆:京都人不對外人說的夏日儀式

第三天傍晚,我在一乘寺住宅區裡散步時,拐進一條小巷,發現路口一座平常應該不起眼的小地藏菩薩石像,竟被裝飾得五顏六色,前面掛滿了紅白相間的燈籠,幾張矮桌拼在一起,擺著西瓜和零食,幾個小孩在旁邊玩套圈圈。一位坐在小板凳上的阿嬤看我探頭探腦,笑著招手要我過去,遞給我一杯麥茶。她說這是「地蔵盆」,每年八月二十三、二十四日左右,京都各個町內會都會為守護孩子的地藏菩薩重新上色、辦兩天的小型祭典,這不是對外公開的觀光活動,而是街坊鄰居自己張羅的儀式,外地遊客幾乎不會知道,連很多住在京都市區其他區的人也未必參加過自己町以外的地藏盆。阿嬤說她從小就在這裡長大,小時候地藏盆是全巷子的孩子最期待的兩天,現在換她張羅給孫子那一輩玩。她一邊說一邊用毛筆沾紅漆,細心地替地藏石像補上臉部的顏色,說每年一定要重新畫,不然地藏公公會「看不清楚」誰是誰家的孩子。

沒有導覽牌、沒有售票口的地藏盆,才是京都人真正過的夏天。

夜幕下的貴船:溪聲、螢火與最後一碗湯豆腐

最後一晚,我又搭上叡電,這次坐到終點貴船口站,再轉乘接駁公車上山,單程車資兩百七十日圓,傍晚五點半的貴船,溪谷已經比市區低了將近五度,涼意順著衣領鑽進來。六月初的貴船川床料理正當季,兩側店家把座位架在溪水上方,潺潺水聲幾乎蓋過交談聲,我在「貴船ひろ文」點了流水麵(流しそうめん,一份一千三百日圓),涼麵順著竹管流下來,得用筷子眼明手快夾住,吃起來清爽帶著微微柚子醬油香氣,配著溪風,整個人的暑氣瞬間被沖散。飯後沿著貴船神社參道往上走,石燈籠一盞盞亮起,參道兩旁樹林在夜色裡幾乎是黑的,只有腳邊的光,走到本宮參拜完,回程時同行的一位當地大叔用手電筒指給我看溪邊草叢裡忽明忽滅的螢火蟲光點,他說這幾年因為遊客變多、光害變嚴重,螢火蟲已經比他小時候少了一半,叮嚀我千萬別用手機閃光燈拍照驚擾牠們。搭最後一班公車下山時,我望著窗外漸漸遠去的燈籠光點,忽然覺得,這趟旅程真正留下來的,不是任何一張照片,而是那些沒有排隊、沒有拍照打卡的安靜片刻。

回旅館的路上,我傳訊息給美咲,只寫了一句:「我終於懂妳說的京都了。」她回了一個笑臉,加上一句:「歡迎入坑,下次帶你去更裡面的地方。」京都這座城市最迷人的地方,或許從來不是那些寫在旅遊指南封面上的名字,而是願意早起、願意走遠一點、願意在巷口停下腳步跟一位陌生阿嬤喝杯麥茶的人,才看得到的那個版本。

ENGLISH

Before I left, I told Misaki, a friend who has lived in Kyoto for over a decade, that I refused to fight crowds at Kiyomizu-dera for the same photo everyone else takes. She laughed and said something I still remember: 'Kyoto people don't go to Kiyomizu either. We go at dawn, or not at all.' That single sentence became the compass for my entire trip. Over the next six days I crossed off every five-star, permanently-queued spot on my map and replaced them with places Misaki described to me in voice messages, some of which weren't even correctly pinned on Google Maps.

5:30am, Alone with the Foxes on Inari Mountain

On day one I set my alarm for five. My ryokan room wasn't well soundproofed, and through the window I could hear a newspaper delivery scooter buzzing somewhere down the alley, mixed with that particular damp-wood smell old cities have at dawn. By 5:40 I was out the door, catching the first JR Nara Line train, a five-minute ride from Kyoto Station to Inari Station for 150 yen. The carriage held only three people, two of whom looked like locals heading to work.

Stepping off the train, the first torii gate of Fushimi Inari Taisha stood right in front of me, but the approach was completely empty—even the vendors who usually sell inari-zushi hadn't set up yet. I walked up the stone path, the vermillion wood of the Thousand Torii Gates almost translucent in the early light, sun slicing through the gaps between posts. The air smelled faintly of wood polished smooth by decades of hands, and something green and mossy from the mountain itself. About forty minutes up is a junction called Yotsutsuji—this is the crucial fork. Ninety percent of visitors turn back after photographing the Thousand Torii Gates below; only those who keep climbing ever see the real Mount Inari.

Past Yotsutsuji the path narrows, the torii gates grow older and weathered, the stone steps become uneven enough that I had to turn sideways in places. About thirty minutes further, I stopped at a tiny teahouse called Nishimura-tei, where the owner was already stoking charcoal. She sells freshly grilled mochi and homemade amazake for 300 yen a cup. I sat on the only wooden bench outside, sipping the warm, slightly sweet rice drink, watching half of the Kyoto basin still buried in morning mist below, Kyoto Tower poking up through the haze like a needle. The owner, speaking in a distinct Kyoto dialect, told me her mother had run this stall for forty years, back when there were no tourists at all—just neighbors climbing up at dawn to pray and stretch their legs. The full loop around Inari mountain takes two to three hours and costs almost nothing besides that cup of amazake and a 200-yen inari-zushi skewer I bought later near Kumataka Shrine near the summit. But the silence up there is worth more than money can buy.

The real face of Kyoto never shows up on Kiyomizu Slope at noon—it only appears at the hour nobody wants to wake up for.

Deep in Ichijoji: The Empty Path Between Shisen-do and Manshu-in

At two that same afternoon, I boarded the Eizan Electric Railway from Demachiyanagi Station—a two-car train the locals affectionately call 'Eiden,' with old-fashioned wooden window frames that rattled gently for five minutes until I reached Ichijoji Station, 260 yen. Stepping out, there wasn't a hint of tourism anywhere—just ordinary houses, a bicycle repair shop, a dry cleaner with a hanging noren curtain. Following a hand-drawn map Misaki had sent me, I walked about fifteen minutes up a gentle slope along Shirakawa-dori, past stone walls and bamboo groves, until the noise of the street suddenly dropped away, leaving only my footsteps and the faint sound of running water.

Shisen-do was built during the Edo period as the retirement retreat of the hermit-poet Ishikawa Jozan. The entrance fee is 700 yen, and the doorway is so narrow it's easy to walk right past it. Inside, I stepped onto floorboards polished to a soft shine, coolness rising through my socks. In the garden, a shishi-odoshi bamboo water device let out a sharp 'clack' against stone at irregular intervals, the sound cutting cleanly through the silence. I sat on the veranda for a full twenty minutes doing nothing, sharing the space with only two or three visitors who looked like retired locals, sipping tea they'd brought themselves. From Shisen-do, a ten-minute walk north brought me to Manshu-in, entrance 600 yen, where the raked-gravel garden cast long shadows in the late afternoon light, quiet enough to hear my own breathing. Before leaving the area, I wandered to nearby Saginomori Shrine, which has almost no signage and is free to enter, hidden inside a dense grove of trees; even local visitors have to walk an unpaved dirt path to reach it. There I met an elderly man walking his dog who told me neighbors simply call it 'the shrine behind the hill'—many Kyoto residents themselves have never actually gone inside.

Flavors Found Only in the Back Alleys

By six that evening, my stomach was growling, so I walked the stretch of Shirakawa-dori near Ichijoji Station that Kyoto University students call the 'ramen battleground'—a narrow alley hiding five or six unassuming ramen shops with signs so small you could easily miss them. I chose Ramen Takayasu. Sliding open the wooden door released a wave of thick tonkotsu-shoyu aroma. Inside there were only eight counter seats, and the owner worked the broth alone. I ordered the signature chuka soba (750 yen); the broth had a glossy sheen of fat on top, the chashu was thick with slightly charred edges, and the noodles were medium-thin and firm, still springy after soaking up the broth. Next to me sat two young men who were clearly Kyoto University students, slurping soup while discussing an upcoming exam—for them, this was just another weeknight dinner, with no idea their bowl had ever been mentioned on a travel blog.

The next morning I made a special trip to Demachi Futaba near Demachiyanagi, a wagashi shop over a hundred years old. Before its nine o'clock opening, a line of local grandmothers had already formed—barely a word of a foreign language among them. I waited about twenty minutes and bought their famous mame-mochi, 230 yen each: a soft mochi skin studded with lightly salted red beans, the salt hitting first, followed by the slow spread of sweet red bean paste, chewy but never sticky on the teeth. The shopkeeper's wrapping movements were so quick I barely saw them before the mochi was in my hand, along with a reminder: 'Made today—best eaten before evening.' I stood on the Kamo River embankment outside, eating while joggers passed by, the sound of the river and bicycle bells mixing with the warm texture of the mochi in my mouth. It became one of the most honest moments of the entire trip.

The Jizo-bon Festival: A Summer Ritual Kyoto Locals Rarely Mention to Outsiders

On the evening of my third day, wandering through a residential block in Ichijoji, I turned into a narrow lane and noticed a small Jizo statue at the corner—normally unremarkable—now decorated in bright colors, strings of red-and-white lanterns hung above it, low tables pushed together holding watermelon and snacks, children playing a ring-toss game nearby. An elderly woman sitting on a small stool noticed me hovering and waved me over, handing me a cup of cold barley tea. She explained this was Jizo-bon, held every year around August 23rd and 24th, when each neighborhood association repaints and celebrates the guardian Jizo statue that watches over local children for two days. This isn't a public tourist event at all—it's something neighbors organize entirely among themselves. Most visitors never learn it exists, and even many Kyoto residents from other districts have never attended a Jizo-bon outside their own block. She told me she grew up right there, and as a child Jizo-bon was the two most anticipated days of the year for every kid on the street; now she's the one organizing it for her grandchildren's generation. As she spoke, she dipped a brush in red paint and carefully touched up the Jizo statue's face, explaining that it must be repainted every year—otherwise, she said, 'Jizo-san won't be able to tell whose child is whose.'

No admission gate, no information sign—Jizo-bon is the real summer that Kyoto people actually live.

Night Falls on Kifune: River Sound, Fireflies, and One Last Bowl of Tofu

On my last evening, I took the Eiden again, this time riding to the end of the line at Kibuneguchi, then transferring to a connecting bus up the mountain for 270 yen. By 5:30pm the valley at Kifune was already nearly five degrees cooler than the city, the chill creeping in through my collar. Early June is peak season for kawadoko dining platforms built directly over the river, where the sound of rushing water nearly drowns out conversation. At Kifune Hirobun I ordered nagashi-somen, flowing noodles, for 1,300 yen—cold noodles sliding down a bamboo chute that you have to catch quickly with chopsticks, refreshing with a faint yuzu-soy tang, the mountain breeze sweeping away the last of the day's heat. After dinner, I walked up the approach to Kifune Shrine as stone lanterns flickered on one by one, the forest on either side turning almost black, lit only by the path at my feet. After paying respects at the main hall, a local man walking the same route pointed his flashlight toward the riverside grass, where fireflies blinked faintly in the dark. He mentioned that as tourism and light pollution have increased in recent years, the firefly population has dropped by half compared to his childhood, and gently warned me never to use a phone's flash near them. On the last bus down the mountain, watching the lantern lights recede behind me, I realized what I would actually carry home from this trip wasn't any photograph, but those quiet, unphotographed, unqueued moments.

On the walk back to my ryokan, I texted Misaki just one line: 'I finally understand the Kyoto you meant.' She sent back a smiling emoji, then added: 'Welcome to the obsession. Next time I'll take you somewhere even deeper.' Perhaps the true charm of Kyoto was never the names printed on the cover of a guidebook, but the version of the city only visible to those willing to wake up early, walk a little further, and stop at a street corner to share a cup of barley tea with a stranger.
