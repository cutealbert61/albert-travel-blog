中文

飛機降落在El Prat機場時是深夜十一點半,但我心裡想的不是旅館的床,而是三天後要穿上的那雙登山鞋。這趟巴塞隆納之行,我刻意把地圖攤開,把高第的糖霜建築暫時放在一旁,劃出另一條路線:柯利塞羅拉山的稜線、蒙特塞拉特的巨石、加拉夫的懸崖,還有聖胡安之夜的篝火。巴塞隆納這座城市很少被人用「戶外探險」的角度書寫,但它其實被地中海與提比達博山夾在中間,山海之間的距離近得不可思議——這正是我想要親身走一遍的理由。

破曉柯利塞羅拉:城市甦醒前的稜線

清晨五點四十分,我的手機鬧鐘在El Born一間老公寓改建的民宿裡震動起來。窗外還是深藍色,巷口麵包店的鐵捲門連百分之一的縫都沒開。我背著一瓶水、兩片前一晚買的橄欖麵包,走到最近的地鐵站轉FGC鐵路到Peu del Funicular站,再轉乘藍色的Tibidabo登山電車,單程票約4.5歐元,車廂裡只有我跟一位遛狗的老先生,狗在木頭地板上滑來滑去,發出規律的喀喀聲。

電車把我丟在提比達博山腰,天色已經開始從墨藍轉成灰紫。我沒有往遊樂園的方向走,而是繞進柯利塞羅拉自然公園的健行入口——這裡幾乎沒有觀光客的蹤影,只有幾個穿螢光背心的晨跑者和牽著狗的當地人。我沿著標記GR92長距離健行步道的紅白油漆記號往柯利塞羅拉電視塔的方向走,腳下是乾燥的紅土與松針混雜的氣味,松脂味在清晨濕氣裡特別濃。走了大約四十分鐘,坡度陡了起來,我的小腿開始發燙,呼吸聲比想像中大聲。

六點五十分左右,我爬上一處視野開闊的岩石平台,整座巴塞隆納像一塊被揉皺又攤平的毯子鋪在腳下,聖家堂的塔尖在晨霧裡只露出一個模糊的輪廓,遠處地中海的顏色跟天空幾乎融成一片,分不出海平線在哪裡。太陽從海面那端升起的瞬間,城市的橘色磁磚屋頂被染成一片燃燒般的紅銅色,我聽見自己倒吸一口氣的聲音,身邊沒有別人,只有風吹過乾草叢的沙沙聲和遠處教堂鐘聲隱約傳來。

巴塞隆納最誠實的樣子,不在蘭布拉大道的人潮裡,而在山脊上無人看見的那五分鐘日出。

這段來回健行加上等日出,我大概花了三個半小時,回到市區時剛好趕上早餐時段,鞋子裡全是紅土的細沙,但那種疲憊很乾淨,不像旅遊景點排隊那種疲憊。

蒙特塞拉特:巨岩上的攀爬與健行全記錄

如果要選一個巴塞隆納近郊「必須親身走過才懂」的探險路線,我會毫不猶豫指向蒙特塞拉特。這座距離市中心約一小時車程的鋸齒狀山脈,遠看像是被巨人用刀削過的一整排石柱,加泰隆尼亞人稱它「Muntanya Màgica」,魔法山。我從Plaça Espanya車站搭乘FGC的R5線,車票在自動售票機上選擇「Trans Montserrat」套票,含來回火車加齒軌纜車或吊掛式纜車,一張約27歐元,建議提早半小時到,因為週末排隊買套票的人潮會排到月台外面。

火車車程約一小時,窗外景色從郊區公寓慢慢變成葡萄園與橄欖樹田,最後火車停在Monistrol de Montserrat站,轉搭紅色的Cremallera齒軌小火車爬升到修道院,窗外的坡度陡到讓人有點腿軟,岩壁上真的能看到一根根石柱貼著鐵軌擦身而過。到達修道院廣場後,我沒有停留太久看黑面聖母像(雖然排隊的信徒隊伍已經繞了半圈廣場),而是直接走向標示「Sant Jeroni」的健行指標,準備挑戰全區最高峰。

這條路線先搭乘Funicular de Sant Joan登山纜車省下前段陡升的力氣,單程約8.5歐元,纜車把我送到海拔約1000公尺處,接下來是純健行,大約四公里的稜線步道,途中會經過裸露的岩脊,寬度窄到只能容納一人通過,右邊就是垂直落下數十公尺的峭壁。步道上偶爾出現鐵製扶手與鑿出的踏階,算是簡易的攀爬輔助設施,但沒有安全繩,雨後或起霧時我聽當地嚮導說最好取消行程,因為石灰岩表面濕滑起來相當危險。

我在正午前抵達海拔1236公尺的Sant Jeroni頂峰,風大到把外套吹得啪啪作響,視野卻毫無阻礙——一邊是庇里牛斯山隱約的雪線,另一邊是地中海的水色,腳下是無數個像巨人手指般矗立的石柱山頭。來回健行加上纜車與拍照停留,我總共花了將近五個小時,腿部肌肉隔天痠痛了兩天,但那種站在裸露岩脈上被風吹到說不出話的感覺,是任何室內景點都給不了的。

蒙特塞拉特的岩石不是用來欣賞的風景,是用來被雙腿記住的地形。

修道院旁的家常味:Restaurant Abat Cisneros的一餐

下山後我的膝蓋已經在打抖,肚子餓得幾乎聽得見自己的腸胃聲。我沒有選擇修道院廣場上遊客眾多的自助餐廳,而是搭齒軌火車回到山腳的Monistrol de Montserrat小鎮,走進一間叫Restaurant Abat Cisneros的餐館,它附屬在同名的小旅館裡,木頭裝潤的用餐空間安靜到能聽見廚房裡鍋鏟碰撞的聲音。

我點了一份「escalivada amb botifarra」,烤茄子與烤紅椒去皮後冷冷地鋪在盤子上,淋上橄欖油與少許海鹽,旁邊配著一整條烤到表皮微焦、油脂滋滋作響的加泰隆尼亞香腸botifarra,咬下去先是脆殼的爆裂感,接著肉汁混著茄子的煙燻甜味在嘴裡化開,那種煙燻味不是刻意的調味,而是柴火燒烤留下的真實痕跡。主餐之外我加點了一碗「escudella」什錦燉湯作為登山後的暖胃湯品,湯裡有雞肉、豬肉丸與粗管麵,喝下去整個胸口都暖了起來。整套餐點加一杯當地產的Penedès白酒,大約落在22歐元左右,份量大到我幾乎吃不完盤裡最後幾塊烤茄子。

餐館老闆是個五十多歲、圍著油漬圍裙的男人,聽到我用破爛的加泰隆尼亞語說「moltes gràcies」時笑得眼睛都彎了,用英語夾雜西班牙語跟我聊起他年輕時也常爬Sant Jeroni,他說現在膝蓋不行了,只能站在餐廳裡「用食物爬山」。這句話讓我笑了很久,也讓那頓飯多了一點人情味,不只是填飽肚子,更像是跟一位老登山客交換了一段記憶。

加拉夫海崖與地中海:健行到懸崖邊的透明海水

結束蒙特塞拉特的隔天,我把探險路線轉向海。從Sants車站搭區域火車R2南下約四十分鐘到Garraf車站,車票不到5歐元,一出站就能聞到海藻與鹹味混合的氣息,比市區沙灘的味道濃烈許多。加拉夫這一段海岸線屬於Garraf自然公園,石灰岩懸崖直接插進地中海,顏色從土黃色漸層到近乎白色,跟巴塞隆納市區的沙灘完全是兩種風景。

我沿著懸崖邊的健行步道往Sitges方向走,步道有些路段緊貼崖邊,腳下碎石滑動時會發出讓人心跳漏一拍的聲響,右手邊的海水在正午陽光下呈現一種近乎透明的土耳其藍,能看見海底礁岩的輪廓。走到一處被當地人稱為「Platja del Home Mort」的小灣——名字直翻是「死人灘」,據說是因為過去有船難漂流上岸的水手——我脫了鞋直接踩進水裡,水溫涼得讓人瞬間清醒,腳底踩到的礫石粗糙但不刺痛。

這段懸崖健行單程約兩小時,中途沒有任何補給站或遮蔭,我建議自備至少一公升水與帽子,夏季正午氣溫容易飆過33度,岩壁反射的熱氣會讓體感更悶熱。健行途中遇到一群當地的浮潛客,他們熟練地從崖邊一個一個跳進海裡,笑聲跟水花聲混在一起,我沒有跟著跳(懸崖高度加上不熟悉水深讓我猶豫),但光是坐在礁岩上看他們玩水、聽海浪拍打岩壁的節奏聲,就覺得整段疲憊的健行值回票價。

加泰隆尼亞的海岸線從來不是拿來曬太陽的背景板,它是被浪與石灰岩共同刻出來的地形課。

聖胡安之夜:沙灘篝火與跳火儀式

我這趟旅程刻意選在六月下旬,只為了趕上「Nit de Sant Joan」聖胡安之夜——這是加泰隆尼亞一年裡最重要的節慶之一,慶祝夏至與聖若翰洗者誕辰,幾乎整座城市會在六月二十三日晚上湧向海邊。傍晚七點多,我沿著Passeig Marítim走到Bogatell海灘,發現沙灘上已經有家庭在鋪野餐墊、堆柴火,空氣裡混著炭火煙味與海風的鹹味。

晚上十點多,天色終於完全暗下來,整條海岸線像被點燃的引信,一堆又一堆篝火同時竄起火光,煙花聲從四面八方炸開,連續不斷,像整座城市在放鞭炮跟自己道別春天。旁邊一家人熱情招呼我加入他們的野餐,遞給我一片「coca de Sant Joan」——一種灑滿糖漬水果與松子的扁平甜麵包,口感介於布里歐許跟蛋糕之間,甜度不算重,松子烤過的香氣特別明顯。他們告訴我傳統上這晚要跳過篝火三次象徵驅趕厄運,我半信半疑地也跟著年輕人跳了一次矮一點的火堆,火焰的熱度撲在小腿上,眾人大笑鼓掌。

一位當地媽媽跟我解釋,聖胡安之夜對加泰隆尼亞人來說像是一種集體的心理重開機,一年的煩惱在這晚被寫在紙上丟進火裡燒掉,孩子們熬夜不睡覺是被允許的,整座城市默契地允許喧鬧到凌晨。凌晨一點多,海面上煙花仍斷斷續續炸開,我坐在沙灘上,鞋子裡又是沙又是海水,聞著空氣裡硝煙與烤肉混合的味道,忽然覺得這才是巴塞隆納給我最深刻的畢業考——山、海與火,它把最原始的自然力量,直接交還給每一個普通人的手裡。

回程的地鐵上滿滿都是拖著疲憊笑容的家庭與遊客,我靠著車窗,手機裡存了將近六百張照片,腦中反覆播放的卻是那三段畫面:柯利塞羅拉山脊上的那道日出、蒙特塞拉特頂峰被風吹得幾乎站不穩的自己,還有加拉夫懸崖下那片近乎透明的藍。巴塞隆納不會只給你高第跟海鮮飯,如果你願意在天還沒亮時穿上登山鞋,它會把整座山海一起攤開給你看。

ENGLISH

It was half past eleven at night when the plane touched down at El Prat, and yet the first thing on my mind wasn't the bed waiting at the hotel — it was the hiking boots I'd packed at the bottom of my bag. For this trip to Barcelona, I deliberately set Gaudí's candy-colored facades aside and drew a different route across the map: the ridgeline of Collserola, the stone teeth of Montserrat, the limestone cliffs of Garraf, and the bonfires of Sant Joan night. Barcelona is rarely written about as an outdoor destination, yet it sits wedged between the Mediterranean and the mountains with almost absurd intimacy — that proximity was exactly what I'd come to test with my own legs.

Dawn on Collserola: The Ridge Before the City Wakes

At 5:40am my phone buzzed inside a converted apartment guesthouse in El Born. Outside the window the sky was still ink-blue, and the bakery on the corner hadn't lifted its shutter even a centimeter. I grabbed a bottle of water and two slices of olive bread bought the night before, walked to the nearest metro, switched to the FGC line toward Peu del Funicular, then boarded the blue Tibidabo funicular — a one-way ticket costs about €4.5. The only other passenger was an elderly man walking his dog, its paws sliding rhythmically across the wooden floor of the carriage.

The funicular dropped me halfway up Tibidabo, the sky just beginning to shift from deep navy to slate purple. Instead of heading toward the amusement park, I veered off toward the entrance of Collserola Natural Park, where tourists simply don't go — only a handful of reflective-vested joggers and locals walking dogs. I followed the red-and-white paint markers of the long-distance GR92 trail toward the Collserola communications tower, the dry red earth and pine needles releasing a resin-heavy scent thickened by the morning humidity. After about forty minutes the slope steepened, my calves began to burn, and my breathing grew louder than I expected.

Around 6:50am I climbed onto an open rock ledge, and all of Barcelona unrolled below like a crumpled blanket smoothed flat. Sagrada Família's spires were just a blurred silhouette in the haze, and the Mediterranean's color bled so completely into the sky that I couldn't tell where the horizon actually was. The instant the sun broke over the sea, the city's orange rooftops ignited into a burning copper glow. I heard myself gasp out loud — there was no one else around, only wind hissing through dry grass and a distant, half-heard church bell.

Barcelona's most honest face isn't found in the crowds along La Rambla — it's those five unseen minutes of sunrise on a ridge no one else bothers to climb.

The round trip, sunrise wait included, took roughly three and a half hours. By the time I got back into the city it was exactly breakfast hour, my shoes full of fine red dust. It was the clean kind of exhaustion — nothing like the tired legs you get from queueing at a monument.

Montserrat: A Full Record of Climbing the Rock City

If there's one adventure route near Barcelona that you truly have to walk yourself to understand, it's Montserrat without question. This serrated mountain range, roughly an hour from the city center, looks from a distance like a row of stone columns carved by a giant's blade — Catalans call it Muntanya Màgica, the Magic Mountain. I caught the FGC R5 line from Plaça Espanya station, selecting the 'Trans Montserrat' combo ticket at the machine, which bundles the round-trip train with either the rack railway or cable car for about €27. I'd recommend arriving thirty minutes early, because on weekends the ticket queue spills out past the platform gates.

The train ride takes about an hour, the scenery shifting gradually from suburban apartment blocks to vineyards and olive groves, until it finally stops at Monistrol de Montserrat. From there I switched to the red Cremallera rack railway, which climbs to the monastery at a gradient steep enough to make your knees feel weak — through the window, individual rock pillars genuinely seem to brush past the tracks. At the monastery plaza, I skipped the long line of pilgrims waiting to see the Black Madonna and walked straight toward the trail marker for Sant Jeroni, ready to take on the range's highest peak.

The route starts with the Funicular de Sant Joan, a cable car that saves you the initial brutal climb for about €8.5 one way, depositing you at roughly 1,000 meters. From there it's pure hiking — about four kilometers along a ridge trail that at points narrows to bare exposed rock, wide enough for exactly one person, with a sheer drop of several dozen meters on the right. Occasionally there are iron handrails and carved footholds bolted into the stone, a rudimentary form of assisted climbing, but no safety ropes. A local guide told me that after rain or in fog it's best to cancel the hike altogether, since the limestone turns dangerously slick.

I reached the 1,236-meter summit of Sant Jeroni just before noon, the wind snapping my jacket like a flag, the view utterly unobstructed — the faint snow line of the Pyrenees on one side, the deep water of the Mediterranean on the other, and beneath my feet, countless stone peaks standing like a giant's fingers. Between the hike, the cable car, and photo stops, the whole round trip took nearly five hours. My legs ached for two days afterward, but standing on that exposed rock spine, wind-whipped and speechless, gave me something no indoor attraction ever could.

Montserrat's rock isn't scenery to be admired from a distance — it's terrain meant to be memorized by your own legs.

A Home-Style Meal by the Monastery: Restaurant Abat Cisneros

By the time I got back down, my knees were visibly trembling and my stomach was loud enough to hear. I skipped the crowded self-service cafeteria at the monastery plaza and instead rode the rack railway back down to the small town of Monistrol de Montserrat, walking into a restaurant called Abat Cisneros, attached to the small hotel of the same name. The wood-paneled dining room was quiet enough that I could hear the clatter of pans from the kitchen.

I ordered escalivada amb botifarra — roasted eggplant and red peppers, peeled and laid out cold on the plate, drizzled with olive oil and a touch of sea salt, alongside a whole grilled botifarra sausage, its skin faintly charred and sizzling with rendered fat. The first bite cracked open with a crisp skin, then the juices mingled with the smoky sweetness of the eggplant, a smokiness that wasn't seasoned in but genuinely burned into the meat over open flame. On the side, I added a bowl of escudella, a hearty Catalan stew with chicken, pork meatballs, and thick pasta tubes — warming from the first spoonful straight through my chest. The whole meal, with a glass of local Penedès white wine, came to about €22, and the portions were so generous I couldn't finish the last few pieces of roasted eggplant.

The restaurant's owner, a man in his fifties in an oil-stained apron, broke into a wide grin when I clumsily said 'moltes gràcies' in Catalan. Switching between English and Spanish, he told me he used to climb Sant Jeroni often when he was younger — his knees don't allow it anymore, so now he 'climbs the mountain through food' instead, from behind the counter. That line stuck with me for the rest of the trip, turning a simple meal into something closer to trading memories with an old mountaineer.

Garraf Cliffs and the Mediterranean: Hiking to Water You Can See Through

The day after Montserrat, I turned my adventure route toward the sea. From Sants station I took the R2 regional train south for about forty minutes to Garraf, a ticket costing under €5. Stepping off the platform, the smell of seaweed and salt hit immediately, far more concentrated than anything you'd smell on the city beaches. This stretch of coastline belongs to Garraf Natural Park, where limestone cliffs plunge straight into the Mediterranean, their color shifting from ochre to almost white — a completely different landscape from Barcelona's flat urban sand.

I followed the cliffside trail toward Sitges, parts of it hugging the edge closely enough that loose gravel sliding underfoot made my heart skip a beat. To the right, under the midday sun, the water turned an almost transparent turquoise, clear enough to make out the shapes of submerged rocks below. Eventually I reached a small cove locals call Platja del Home Mort — literally 'Dead Man's Beach,' named, supposedly, for sailors once washed ashore after shipwrecks. I took off my shoes and stepped straight into the water; the cold hit instantly, sharp enough to wake me up completely, the pebbles rough but not painful underfoot.

This cliffside hike takes about two hours one way, with no supply stations or shade along the route — I'd strongly recommend carrying at least a liter of water and a hat, since midday summer temperatures easily exceed 33°C, and heat reflecting off the rock face makes it feel even more oppressive. Along the way I ran into a group of local snorkelers who leapt off the cliff edge one by one with practiced ease, laughter mixing with the splash of water. I didn't jump myself — the height combined with my uncertainty about the water's depth made me hesitate — but just sitting on the rocks watching them play, listening to the rhythmic slap of waves against limestone, made the entire tiring hike feel worth it.

Catalonia's coastline was never meant as a backdrop for sunbathing — it's a geology lesson carved jointly by waves and limestone.

Sant Joan Night: Bonfires on the Sand and the Ritual of Jumping Fire

I deliberately timed this trip for late June just to catch Nit de Sant Joan, one of Catalonia's most important festivals, celebrating the summer solstice and the birth of Saint John the Baptist. On the night of June 23rd, nearly the entire city pours toward the beaches. Around 7pm I walked along Passeig Marítim to Bogatell beach and found families already spreading picnic blankets and stacking wood, the air thick with charcoal smoke mixed with salty sea breeze.

By 10pm, once the sky had gone fully dark, the whole coastline seemed to catch light like a lit fuse — bonfire after bonfire flaring up almost simultaneously, fireworks cracking from every direction in an unbroken rhythm, as if the entire city were setting off firecrackers to say goodbye to spring. A family nearby waved me over to join their picnic and handed me a slice of coca de Sant Joan, a flat sweet bread topped with candied fruit and pine nuts, somewhere between brioche and cake in texture, not overly sweet, with the toasted pine nuts standing out clearly. They told me tradition says you should jump over the bonfire three times to ward off bad luck for the coming year. Half-skeptical, I joined a group of younger locals jumping over a smaller fire, the heat licking at my shins as everyone burst into laughter and applause.

A local mother explained to me that Sant Joan night functions almost like a collective psychological reset for Catalans — worries from the past year get written on paper and thrown into the flames, children are allowed to stay up all night, and the entire city tacitly agrees to let the noise run until dawn. Past 1am, fireworks were still popping intermittently over the water. I sat on the sand, my shoes full of both grit and seawater, breathing in air thick with gunpowder smoke and grilled meat, and it suddenly struck me that this was Barcelona's real final exam for me — mountain, sea, and fire, handing the rawest forces of nature directly back into ordinary people's hands.

The metro ride home was packed with families and tourists all wearing the same exhausted smile. Leaning against the window with nearly six hundred photos saved on my phone, the images replaying in my head weren't the famous ones — they were that sunrise on the Collserola ridge, myself barely able to stand in the wind atop Sant Jeroni, and that almost transparent blue water beneath the Garraf cliffs. Barcelona won't only hand you Gaudí and paella. If you're willing to lace up your boots before the sun comes up, it will lay its entire mountain-and-sea self open for you instead.
