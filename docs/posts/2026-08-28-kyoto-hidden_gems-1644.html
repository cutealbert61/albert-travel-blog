中文

這是我第四次來京都,前三次都在清水寺的人潮裡擠得滿身是汗,在嵐山竹林被自拍棒戳到後腦勺,拍出來的照片裡永遠有陌生人的後腦勺入鏡。這一次,我把Google地圖收進背包深處,改帶一本朋友手寫的筆記本——她嫁到京都十二年,筆記本裡寫的不是景點,是巷弄的名字、開店時間、還有「這家只有本地人會去」這種備註。我告訴自己,這趟不趕行程,只跟著京都人的生活節奏走一遍,看看沒有觀光巴士的京都長什麼樣子。

清晨五點半,真如堂的鐘聲比觀光巴士早

清晨五點二十分,我從岡崎地區的小旅館走出來,天色是那種介於墨藍與灰白之間的顏色,街上一輛車也沒有,連平常聒噪的蟬都還沒醒。空氣涼涼的,帶著前一晚灑水留下的濕土味。我沿著白川旁的小路走,水聲比腳步聲還清楚,十分鐘後爬上一段長長的石階,眼前是真如堂——正式名稱「真正極樂寺」,一座連很多京都人自己都要想一下才記得怎麼寫的寺院。售票亭當然還沒開,也沒有人攔我,我就這樣穿過朱紅色的三重塔下,踩在滿地還沒被踩碎的松針上。一個穿著灰色作務衣的僧人正在用竹掃把清掃參道,那種竹枝掃過石板的沙沙聲,是我在京都聽過最安靜卻最清楚的聲音。他抬頭看我一眼,點了點頭,沒說話,又繼續掃他的地。我在階梯上坐了大概二十分鐘,看著天光一點一點把本堂的輪廓從黑影裡拉出來。接著我沿著小徑走到隔壁的金戒光明寺,當地人叫它「黑谷」,墓地區的高處可以俯瞰整個京都市區,清晨薄霧還沒散,屋瓦一片一片浮在霧裡,像有人把整座城市泡在牛奶般的光線裡。遠處傳來一聲鐘響,不知道是哪座寺院敲的,悶悶的、拖得很長,那一刻我站在墓園邊上,忽然覺得比前三次擠在清水寺舞台上看到的「京都全景」還震撼十倍。

京都最好的風景,往往在售票口還沒開門之前就已經演完了。

進進堂的老木桌,京大生吃了一世紀的早餐

七點十五分,我從真如堂慢慢走下坡,大概二十分鐘後抵達京都大學北門前的「進進堂」。這家麵包店開業於大正二年,超過一百年歷史,店裡最出名的不是麵包,是那張從歐洲海運進口的長木桌——聽說戰前就在了,京大學生一屆換過一屆,在同一張桌子上啃麵包、寫報告、失戀。我推門進去時,烤箱的奶油香混著剛磨好的咖啡味撲面而來,一位頭髮花白的老先生已經坐在桌角看報紙,面前是一份摩卡咖啡跟一顆咖哩麵包,他大概每天都坐這個位置,店員上咖啡時連問都沒問就知道要不要加糖。我點了招牌咖哩麵包(260円)跟一份炭燒吐司套餐附奶油與咖啡(750円)。咖哩麵包外皮是那種炸過的酥脆感,咬下去先是「喀」一聲脆響,接著咖哩內餡帶著洋蔥的甜跟一點胡椒的辣,熱氣還冒著煙;吐司則是厚切三公分,外層烤得微焦帶苦香,內裡卻鬆軟得像雲,塗上那抹微鹹的奶油,配一口手沖咖啡的酸香,整個人瞬間清醒。長木桌另一頭坐著兩個穿制服的高中生在趕暑期作業,沒有人多看我這個外國人一眼,那種被當成鄰居而不是觀光客的感覺,比任何美食都讓人滿足。

迷路嵯峨野後巷,祇王寺的苔與寂靜

吃完早餐,我搭京都市巴士到京都駅,轉JR嵯峨野線到嵯峨嵐山駅,車程約十六分鐘,票價240円。出站後跟著人潮走向天龍寺北門,一踏進竹林大道,瞬間被塞成沙丁魚罐頭——這段路我早就知道會塞爆,所以只是穿過去而已,沒有停留。真正的目的地在竹林盡頭再往山裡走的小路,經過二尊院方向的岔口左轉上坡,大概走十五到二十分鐘,路上幾乎沒有其他遊客,只有偶爾錯身而過的當地老先生牽著狗。祇王寺的木門很小,不注意會直接走過去,入場費300円,一位坐在小木亭裡的老太太收了票,遞給我一張手繪地圖,笑著用不太流利的英文說「請安靜」。園內是一片幾乎鋪滿整個地面的苔蘚,顏色從墨綠到翠綠層層疊疊,踩上木棧道時完全沒有聲音,只聽得到竹葉被風吹動的細碎聲響跟鳥叫。寺裡供奉的是平安時代平清盛寵妾祇王的故事,她被拋棄後遁入空門,寺內一間茅草小屋至今還供著她跟母親妹妹的木像。我在裡面待了將近四十分鐘,全程只遇到三位遊客,而十分鐘腳程外的竹林大道當時大概擠了幾百人。要提醒的是,雨後苔徑會濕滑,建議穿包覆式的鞋子;寺院下午四點半就關門,最好抓兩點到三點這段時間造訪,遊覽車還沒把嵐山下午場的人潮送過來。

十分鐘的腳程,卻像是從一張明信片,走進了真正的呼吸聲裡。

巷弄裡的地藏盆,一整條街的孩子與神明

傍晚五點左右我搭公車回到市區,原本只是想穿過六原一帶的住宅巷弄找晚餐,卻在姉小路附近撞見一整條街掛滿紅白燈籠,燈籠上用毛筆寫著小孩子的名字。一位坐在路邊摺紙椅上的婆婆田中太太看我探頭探腦,主動招手要我過去看,她說今天是「地蔵盆」,每年農曆或陽曆八月二十三、二十四日前後,幾乎每個京都的町內會都會辦這個屬於孩子與地藏菩薩的小型祭典——地藏被京都人視為守護孩童的神明,平常立在巷口不起眼的小祠堂,這兩天會被居民擦洗乾淨、披上新做的紅色圍兜,前面擺滿供品跟糖果。田中太太拉著我加入一圈大人小孩,大家手拉著一條巨大的念珠繩,一邊念經一邊順時針傳動,珠子轉到面前時要低頭合掌,這叫「大念珠繰り」,她說她從小做到現在,已經六十幾年。旁邊還有孩子在玩賓果遊戲,輸贏都能拿到一包點心,烤玉米跟線香混合的味道飄在巷子裡,完全沒有一個觀光客,只有我一個外國臉孔。田中太太笑著說,這種祭典不會登上旅遊手冊,因為它本來就不是辦給外人看的,是巷子裡的人辦給巷子裡的孩子跟神明看的。那一刻我忽然明白,京都最私密的風景,從來不是某個景點,而是一整條街願不願意讓你站進去。

柳小路神馬,深夜立飲的一杯清酒

晚上九點多,我沿著木屋町往南走,拐進一條窄到只夠兩人側身而過的柳小路,盡頭亮著一盞紅燈籠,寫著「神馬」二字,是京都人口耳相傳的老字號立飲屋,沒有座位,客人全部站著喝。我點了一杯樽酒(350円)跟三樣關東煮——白蘿蔔、蒟蒻、竹輪各150円,老闆用長筷子從冒著熱氣的大鍋裡撈起來放進小碟子,湯頭是柴魚跟醬油的深棕色,喝一口高湯,鹹甜先到,接著白蘿蔔的甜味在嘴裡化開,外頭夜風涼,那口熱湯讓整個人從喉嚨暖到胃。旁邊一位穿西裝的上班族喝到微醺,主動跟我聊起他小時候也在自己巷子裡的地藏盆抽過獎,說現在住的公寓大樓已經沒有這種活動了,語氣裡有點懷念又有點自嘲。站著喝酒的人來來去去,沒有人久留,也沒有人趕著離開,那種鬆散又溫暖的節奏,大概就是京都人下班後真正的生活。我站在那條窄巷裡喝完最後一口清酒,忽然覺得比起前三次在清水寺看到的京都,這一天走過的巷弄、苔徑跟燈籠,才是真正屬於這座城市的呼吸。

回旅館的路上已經接近午夜,商店街的鐵門一間間拉下,只有便利商店的燈還亮著。我想起田中太太說的那句話——這座城市不是辦給外人看的,但只要你願意放慢腳步、放下地圖,它偶爾會讓你站進去看一眼。這大概就是我這趟京都私房路線最珍貴的收穫。

ENGLISH

This was my fourth trip to Kyoto. The first three times I sweated through the crowds at Kiyomizu-dera and got jabbed in the back of the head by selfie sticks in the Arashiyama bamboo grove, every photo I took ruined by some stranger's ponytail in the frame. This time I buried my map at the bottom of my bag and carried instead a handwritten notebook from a friend who married into a Kyoto family twelve years ago. Her notebook had no landmarks in it—just alley names, opening hours, and margin notes like 'only locals go here.' I told myself I wasn't going to chase an itinerary this time. I was just going to follow the rhythm of the city itself and see what Kyoto looks like once the tour buses are gone.

5:30 AM: Shinnyo-do's Bell Rings Before the Tour Buses Wake

I stepped out of my guesthouse in the Okayama district at 5:20 in the morning, the sky still that ink-blue color halfway to dawn, the streets completely empty, not even the cicadas awake yet. The air was cool and smelled faintly of wet earth from someone's evening watering the night before. I followed the small canal along the Shirakawa river, the sound of the water louder than my own footsteps, and after ten minutes climbed a long stone staircase to Shinnyo-do—formally Shinsho Gokuraku-ji, a name even most Kyoto residents have to pause to spell correctly. The ticket booth was, of course, still closed, and nobody stopped me from walking straight through beneath the vermilion three-story pagoda, my shoes crunching on pine needles that hadn't yet been trampled flat. A monk in a gray work robe was sweeping the approach with a bamboo broom, and the dry rasp of twigs on stone was, I think, the clearest sound I have ever heard in this city. He glanced at me once, nodded, and kept sweeping. I sat on the steps for about twenty minutes and watched the light slowly pull the silhouette of the main hall out of the darkness. Then I wandered next door to Konkai Komyo-ji, which locals simply call Kurodani, where the upper cemetery gives you a view over the entire city. The morning mist hadn't burned off yet, and the tiled rooftops floated in it in overlapping layers, as if the whole city had been dipped in milk-colored light. Somewhere a temple bell rang once, low and drawn out—I never found out which temple it belonged to—and standing there at the edge of the graveyard, I felt something ten times more powerful than the famous 'panoramic view of Kyoto' I'd photographed from the Kiyomizu stage three trips ago.

The best view in Kyoto is usually the one that's already over by the time the ticket booth opens.

An Ancient Wooden Table: Breakfast the Way Kyoto University Students Have Eaten for a Century

By 7:15, I'd walked down from Shinnyo-do and, about twenty minutes later, arrived at Shinshindo, a bakery just outside the north gate of Kyoto University that has been open since 1913. The bread isn't even the main attraction here—it's the long wooden communal table, reportedly shipped over from Europe before the war, that generations of Kyoto University students have eaten at, written papers on, and nursed heartbreaks over. When I pushed open the door, the smell of butter and yeast from the oven mixed with freshly ground coffee hit me immediately. A gray-haired man was already seated at the corner of the table with a newspaper, a mocha coffee, and a curry bun in front of him—clearly the same seat, the same order, every single day, because the staff brought his coffee without even asking if he wanted sugar. I ordered the signature curry bun (260 yen) and a charcoal-grilled toast set with butter and coffee (750 yen). The curry bun's fried exterior gave a satisfying crack on the first bite, followed by a filling of sweet onion and a hit of black pepper, still steaming. The toast was cut a full three centimeters thick, faintly bitter and caramelized on the outside, impossibly soft and cloud-like inside, and with a smear of slightly salted butter and a sip of pour-over coffee, I felt genuinely awake for the first time that day. Two students in school uniforms sat at the far end of the same table cramming through summer homework, and nobody so much as glanced twice at the foreigner in their midst. Being treated like a neighbor instead of a tourist was, honestly, more satisfying than the bread itself.

Getting Lost in Back-Alley Sagano: The Moss and Silence of Gio-ji

After breakfast I took a city bus to Kyoto Station, transferred to the JR Sagano Line, and rode sixteen minutes to Saga-Arashiyama Station for 240 yen. Following the crowd toward Tenryu-ji's north gate, I stepped into the bamboo grove and was immediately packed in like a sardine—I already knew this stretch would be jammed, so I simply walked through without stopping. My real destination was a small path further into the hills past the bamboo, forking off toward Nison-in and climbing uphill for fifteen to twenty minutes, along which I passed almost no other tourists, only the occasional elderly local walking a dog. The wooden gate at Gio-ji is easy to miss if you're not looking for it. Admission is 300 yen, collected by an old woman sitting in a tiny wooden booth who handed me a hand-drawn map and said, in halting English, 'please, quiet.' Inside, the ground is almost entirely carpeted in moss, layered in shades from deep emerald to jade, and walking the wooden boardwalk made almost no sound at all—just the rustle of bamboo leaves and birdsong. The temple commemorates Gio, a mistress of the twelfth-century warlord Taira no Kiyomori, who retreated here after being abandoned and took vows as a nun; a small thatched hut still holds wooden statues of her, her mother, and her sister. I stayed almost forty minutes and encountered only three other visitors the entire time, while the bamboo grove ten minutes away was hosting what looked like several hundred people. Worth noting: the mossy paths get slippery after rain, so wear closed shoes, and the temple closes at 4:30 PM, so aim for the window between 2 and 3 PM, before the afternoon tour buses unload into Arashiyama.

Ten minutes on foot, and I'd walked straight out of a postcard and into the sound of my own breathing.

A Whole Street for the Children and the Guardian Deity: Jizo-bon

I caught a bus back into town around five in the evening, planning only to cut through the residential lanes of the Rokuhara neighborhood to find dinner, when I stumbled onto a street near Anekoji strung with red and white paper lanterns, each one hand-painted with a child's name. An elderly woman named Tanaka-san, sitting on a folding chair, waved me over when she caught me staring. She explained that this was Jizo-bon, a small neighborhood festival held every year around August 23rd and 24th, dedicated to Jizo, the guardian deity of children, whose modest little roadside shrines—usually easy to overlook—get scrubbed clean and dressed in fresh red bibs for the occasion, surrounded by offerings and candy. Tanaka-san pulled me into a circle of adults and children all holding a single enormous rosary, chanting and passing it hand over hand in a clockwise rotation; when a large bead reached you, you bowed your head. This ritual is called o-nenju-mawashi, and she'd been doing it since she was a little girl—more than sixty years now. Nearby, kids played a bingo-style game, everyone winning some small candy regardless of the outcome, and the smell of grilled corn and incense drifted through the alley. There wasn't a single tourist in sight; I was the only foreign face there. Tanaka-san laughed and said this festival never makes it into guidebooks, because it was never meant to be seen by outsiders—it's a street putting on a show for its own children and its own god. Standing there, I understood that the most private view Kyoto has to offer isn't a landmark at all. It's whether an entire street is willing to let you step inside it.

A Cup of Sake at Shinba, Standing in a Alley Called Yanagi-no-Kouji

Past nine at night, I walked south along Kiyamachi and turned into an alley so narrow two people could barely pass shoulder to shoulder—Yanagi-no-Kouji—and at the end of it hung a single red lantern reading Shinba, a standing bar Kyoto locals have passed down by word of mouth for generations, with no seats at all; everyone drinks on their feet. I ordered a cup of cask sake (350 yen) and three pieces of oden—daikon, konnyaku, and chikuwa, 150 yen each—which the owner fished out of a steaming pot with long chopsticks into a small dish. The broth was a deep brown of bonito and soy sauce; the first sip hit salty-sweet, then the daikon's own sweetness bloomed underneath, and against the cool night air outside, that hot broth warmed me from my throat down to my stomach. A salaryman beside me, a little tipsy, struck up a conversation and told me he used to win prizes at his own neighborhood's Jizo-bon as a kid, but that his current apartment building doesn't hold the festival anymore—there was a trace of nostalgia and self-deprecation in how he said it. People drifted in and out of the bar, nobody lingering too long, nobody rushing to leave either, and that loose, warm rhythm felt like the real texture of a Kyoto evening after work. I finished my last sip of sake standing in that narrow alley and realized that everything I'd walked through that day—the alleys, the moss paths, the paper lanterns—belonged far more truly to this city than anything I'd photographed at Kiyomizu-dera on my first three visits.

By the time I walked back to the guesthouse it was nearly midnight, the shutters of the shopping arcade rolling down one by one, only the convenience store lights still on. I kept thinking about what Tanaka-san had told me—that this city isn't performed for outsiders, but if you're willing to slow down and set your map aside, it will occasionally let you stand inside it for a moment. That, I think, was the real souvenir of this particular route through Kyoto.
