中文

清晨五點五十分,我摸黑推開宅邸旅館(riad)厚重的雪松木門,潮濕的石板路面還留著昨夜灑水的痕跡,空氣裡混著茉莉花與柴火的味道。我這趟來馬拉喀什,行李裡沒帶血拼清單,只帶了一台相機和一個念頭:把這座紅城的建築紋理、色彩邏輯與街頭手作,一寸一寸看進心裡。

破曉前的庫圖比亞:光影裡的幾何密碼

宅邸位在卡斯巴(Kasbah)區,步行到庫圖比亞清真寺(Koutoubia Mosque)只要十二分鐘,但那十二分鐘我走得很慢,因為巷弄裡幾乎沒有其他人,只有麵包店老闆搬出剛出爐的圓餅,烤箱的熱氣撲在臉上。六點十五分,喚拜聲從尖塔頂端傳來,先是一個沙啞的男聲起音,接著整個舊城的清真寺此起彼落地應和,聲音在窄巷的石牆之間來回反彈,像是被建築本身重新編曲過。庫圖比亞尖塔高七十七公尺,建於十二世紀阿爾摩哈德王朝,塔身四面雕滿不同的幾何浮雕,沒有一面重複——這是我後來查資料才知道的細節,但站在塔下抬頭看時,單憑肉眼就能感覺到那種比例的精準,像是有人用圓規和直尺推導出一種近乎音樂節奏的秩序。當地導遊阿布杜(Abdou)後來告訴我,這座塔的比例後來被複製到西班牙塞維亞的希拉達塔,「我們馬拉喀什人常開玩笑說,是我們教會了安達魯西亞怎麼蓋高塔。」

七點半回到宅邸屋頂吃早餐,主人準備了千層薄餅姆斯蒙(msemen)配杏仁醬阿姆魯(amlou),還有一壺插滿新鮮薄荷葉的甜茶。薄荷茶要倒得夠高、夠遠,茶湯落入玻璃杯時會激出一層細泡,主人說這是判斷茶泡得好不好的標準之一。屋頂的視野正對著舊城起伏的土黃色天際線,晨光把牆面染成一種介於磚紅與赭橙之間的顏色,我後來才明白,這正是馬拉喀什被稱為「紅城」的原因——一項延續近百年的城市法規,規定舊城與部分新城建築外牆必須維持這種赭紅色調,連麥當勞的招牌在這裡都得低調地換成同色系。

九點整,我走進巴西亞王宮(Bahia Palace),門票七十迪拉姆,排隊大約十五分鐘。這座十九世紀晚期的宮殿是我此行「怎麼玩」花最多時間細看的地方,前後待了將近兩個半小時。從入口的窄巷轉進第一進庭院時,光線忽然變得極其柔和,四面牆壁貼滿了彩色鋯克利瓷磚(zellige),每一片都是工匠手工敲鑿成星形或菱形後再拼嵌而成,近看會發現接縫處其實並不完全對齊,那種手工的不精確反而讓整面牆有了呼吸感。往裡走是大宰相西迪穆薩的私人起居區,天花板是整片雕花香柏木,顏色因年代久遠而深沉,但雕刻的葉紋與阿拉伯書法卻依然銳利,導覽員說這種天花板叫「artesonado」,工匠必須先在地面上拼好整套模組,再吊上去逐塊嵌合,一間房頂往往要花上一年。宮殿深處有四位妻妾各自獨立的庭院,大小、採光、瓷磚花色都不相同,據說是按受寵程度分配資源——這種把建築當作權力語言的細節,比任何導覽手冊上的文字都更讓我印象深刻。中庭正午陽光直射進來時,水池倒映著雕花拱門,我在那裡站了快二十分鐘,只是看著光線隨雲影移動,拱門的影子像時鐘一樣慢慢挪動位置。要提醒的是,宮殿沒有太多遮蔭,夏季正午最好避開,我後來聽同行旅人說十二點到兩點之間人潮與熱度都會爆增,建議像我一樣選在九點到十一點之間進場。

馬拉喀什的建築從不試圖說服你相信永恆,它讓你看見時間如何一層一層被雕刻進牆面。

藍色庭園與設計朝聖:馬若雷勒花園、聖羅蘭博物館與秘密花園

下午一點,我搭計程車前往新城古利茲區(Gueliz)的馬若雷勒花園(Jardin Majorelle),車程約十五分鐘,車資殺價到三十迪拉姆(建議上車前先講好價錢,跳表計程車在馬拉喀什並不普及)。花園門票一百五十迪拉姆,加購聖羅蘭博物館聯票另加一百迪拉姆,我覺得非常值得。畫家賈克·馬若雷勒在一九三〇年代將整座別墅漆成一種濃度極高的鈷藍色,後人稱之為「馬若雷勒藍」,那種藍近看其實帶了一點紫調,在正午陽光下會反射出幾乎刺眼的飽和感,和周圍種植的仙人掌、竹林、九重葛形成強烈對比。一九八〇年代伊夫·聖羅蘭與夥伴皮耶·貝傑買下這座花園並修復,聖羅蘭晚年常說這裡是他唯一感到平靜的地方,他過世後骨灰的一部分依照遺願灑在園內玫瑰園旁的紀念柱下。緊鄰花園的聖羅蘭博物館建築本身就是一件當代設計作品,建築師以柏柏爾織品的經緯紋理為靈感,外牆用了近兩萬七千塊紅磚,拼出類似織布機經線的浮雕紋路,館內常設展陳列聖羅蘭從摩洛哥色彩汲取靈感的手稿與服裝,其中一件參考柏柏爾婚禮服飾設計的斗篷,展櫃燈光刻意調暗,只留一束光打在刺繡上,像是要人放慢呼吸去看清每一針的走向。

離開花園,我特地繞去舊城穆阿辛街(Rue Mouassine)附近的秘密花園(Le Jardin Secret),門票五十迪拉姆。這座宅邸花園在近年修復後重新對外開放,分為伊斯蘭幾何庭園與異國植物庭園兩個區塊,最讓我著迷的是它把十六世紀的「khettara」地下引水系統原樣保留並用透明地板展示——那是一套利用重力從阿特拉斯山引水到城市的古老水利工程,馬拉喀什能在沙漠邊緣維持花園城市的美名,靠的正是這套看不見的地下管線。庭園中央的水道把整個空間切成四等份,對應可蘭經中描述天堂的四條河流,設計邏輯與西班牙阿爾罕布拉宮如出一轍,兩地同屬安達魯西亞—馬格里布的造園傳統。傍晚時分,園內的柑橘樹開始飄出淡淡的花香,混著剛澆過水的泥土味,那種安靜與街外的市集喧囂形成極端反差,我在長椅上坐了快半小時捨不得離開。稍早我也去了本優素福伊斯蘭學院(Medersa Ben Youssef),門票五十迪拉姆,中庭的雪松木雕花與灰泥浮雕(stucco)幾乎覆蓋每一寸牆面,幾何星形圖案往內延伸出無限重複的視覺效果,據說工匠故意不讓任何一組圖案完整對稱收尾,理由是「只有真主才配得上完美」。

廣場與巷弄的手作美學:染坊、鐵匠與烤肉巷

下午四點,我鑽進哈巴卡迪瑪(Rahba Kedima)附近的染坊市集(Souk Sebbaghine)。還沒轉進巷子就先聞到味道——一種混合了羊毛脂、靛藍與燒炭的刺鼻氣味,巷子兩側掛滿剛染好的羊毛與絲線,從深靛藍到番紅花黃層層疊疊地垂掛在頭頂,像一整條會滴水的彩虹隧道。一位染匠正把整束羊毛浸進滾燙的染缸,他的手臂因常年接觸染料而呈現一種洗不掉的青灰色,他用柏柏爾語混著法文跟我解釋,靛藍染料仍是取自當地種植的木藍植物,染色前要先用明礬固色,一束羊毛前後要浸染五到七次才能達到市場要的深度。再往前走是鐵匠巷,叮叮噹噹的敲擊聲從清晨持續到日落,老師傅用最原始的風箱式炭爐打製傳統銅盤與燈具,火星四濺時我下意識後退,他倒是連護目鏡都沒戴,笑著說「這聲音我聽了四十年,比鬧鐘準時。」這些巷弄裡的手作痕跡,某種程度上比任何美術館展品都更接近這座城市真正的「街頭藝術」——不是牆上的塗鴉,而是雙手日復一日留下的紋理。

逛到有點餓,我特地繞去舊城巴布杜卡拉市場(Bab Doukkala)後方那條被當地人稱為「烤肉巷」(Mechoui Alaouiyine)的小巷。這裡從清晨就開始用地窖式土窯慢烤整隻羊肉,一烤就是六到七小時,巷子還沒走到底,油脂滴落炭火的焦香味就已經先鑽進鼻腔。我選了一家看起來最多本地人排隊的攤位,老闆從窯裡吊出整片羊肩肉,徒手撕成小塊,秤重計價,我點了大約三百克,配上孜然鹽、烤麵包和一小碟醃漬檸檬,總共花了大約七十迪拉姆。肉質軟到幾乎不用咀嚼就能化開,外層帶著炭烤的焦脆邊,沾一點孜然粉與粗鹽,鹹香直接撞進味蕾,配著剛出爐還帶著麥香的圓麵包,我一口氣吃完整份,連盤底的油脂都想用麵包抹乾淨。旁邊一位常客告訴我,這條巷子的攤位大多是家族三代傳下來的生意,「我爺爺在這裡吃,我爸爸在這裡吃,現在換我帶兒子來吃」,那種食物與家族記憶疊合在一起的說法,讓一份簡單的烤羊肉突然有了重量。

華燈初上的德吉瑪廣場:說書人、蛇笛與街頭劇場

傍晚六點半,天色開始轉橘,我走到德吉瑪廣場(Jemaa el-Fnaa),整座廣場像被按下播放鍵一樣,以肉眼可見的速度從空曠變得擁擠。賣柳橙汁的推車一字排開,四塊迪拉姆一杯,現榨、冰涼、酸甜直接;耍蛇人把眼鏡蛇倒進籃子前,先用嗩吶般的雙簧笛吹出一段顫音,圍觀的人群自動讓出一圈空地;畫亨娜的女人們攤開手繪圖樣本,一個手掌大小的圖案大約五十到八十迪拉姆,價錢會因為觀光客身份而浮動,議價是這裡心照不宣的禮貌。廣場中央最讓我駐足的是說書人(halqa)的圈子,一位年邁老者盤腿坐在小板凳上,用阿拉伯方言講述一段關於狡猾商人與精靈的故事,聲音忽高忽低,聽不懂內容的我卻被他手勢與停頓的節奏牽著走,直到他停在一個懸念處攤開手掌等打賞,眾人才紛紛掏出零錢,我才明白這套「說到一半收錢」的技巧本身就是一種活生生的表演設計,聯合國教科文組織把德吉瑪廣場的口述文化列為非物質文化遺產,理由正是如此。

這裡沒有一幅畫掛在牆上等你欣賞,整座廣場本身就是一齣每天重演、卻永遠不會被寫成劇本的即興劇場。

里雅德的星期五庫斯庫斯:好客之道與三杯茶的哲學

回程前的最後一個星期五,宅邸女主人法蒂瑪邀我留下來吃午餐——她說星期五是全家人一定要聚在一起吃庫斯庫斯的日子,這一天清真寺的主麻聚禮結束後,幾乎每戶人家的廚房都會飄出同樣的味道。她端出一大盤蒸過三次的細粒庫斯庫斯,鋪滿燉南瓜、胡蘿蔔、櫛瓜與燉得軟爛的羊肉,最上面撒了葡萄乾和炸洋蔥酥,吃法是用麵包或手指把庫斯庫斯捏成小球送入口中,湯汁順著手心流下也不必在意。法蒂瑪一邊幫我盛第二碗一邊解釋,摩洛哥人待客有句老話,「第一杯茶像生命一樣苦,第二杯像愛情一樣甜,第三杯像死亡一樣溫柔」,指的是傳統上薄荷茶要連續斟三杯,主人反覆添茶不是客套,而是一種不容拒絕的心意,如果客人只喝一杯就婉拒,在許多家庭眼裡近乎失禮。她也提醒我,若受邀進入摩洛哥人家中作客,最好用右手接遞食物與茶杯,左手在傳統觀念裡被視為不潔;若逢齋戒月造訪,白天在公共場合飲食也應盡量避免,以示對正在齋戒的人的尊重。窗外傳來鄰居孩子放學嬉鬧的聲音,法蒂瑪的丈夫哈金在庭院裡修剪柑橘樹,一家人的午後時光和我早幾天在博物館裡看到的雕花與瓷磚一樣,都是這座城市美學的一部分——只是這一種美,是活在餐桌與茶壺之間,不需要門票。

離開馬拉喀什那天清晨,我又繞回庫圖比亞廣場,喚拜聲照常從尖塔傳來,清潔工正沿街灑水,空氣裡是同樣的茉莉與塵土氣味。五天前我以為自己是來看建築與博物館,離開時才發現真正留在記憶裡的,是染坊裡洗不掉的靛藍手指、烤肉巷裡三代同堂的排隊隊伍,以及法蒂瑪那句「再喝一杯茶吧」。這座紅城的設計美學,原來從來不只在牆上,也在人與人之間反覆遞出的那只玻璃茶杯裡。

ENGLISH

At ten to six in the morning, I pushed open the heavy cedar door of my riad in the Kasbah quarter of Marrakech. The stone lane outside still held puddles from last night's watering, and the air carried a mix of jasmine and woodsmoke. I hadn't come to Marrakech for the souvenir shopping or the spice bargaining everyone talks about. I came with a camera and one simple intention: to read this red city through its architecture, its color logic, and the handwork still alive in its alleys.

Before Dawn at the Koutoubia: Geometry Written in Light

The walk from my riad to the Koutoubia Mosque takes twelve minutes, but I walked it slowly that morning because the streets were nearly empty except for a baker pulling round loaves from his oven, the heat of it brushing my face as I passed. At quarter past six, the call to prayer rose from the minaret, a single hoarse voice at first, then answered by mosque after mosque across the old city, the sound bouncing between the narrow stone walls as though the architecture itself were rearranging it into something closer to music. The Koutoubia's minaret stands seventy-seven meters tall, built under the Almohad dynasty in the twelfth century, and each of its four faces bears a different geometric relief pattern — a detail I only confirmed later in my research, but one I could feel simply standing beneath it, sensing a precision that felt almost rhythmic, as if worked out with compass and straightedge toward something resembling musical proportion. My guide Abdou told me later, half-joking, that this tower's proportions were later echoed in Seville's Giralda: 'We like to say we taught Andalusia how to build tall.'

Back on the riad's rooftop for breakfast at half past seven, my host had laid out msemen, the flaky layered flatbread, alongside amlou, the almond-argan spread, and a pot of mint tea stuffed with fresh leaves. Pouring it well means lifting the teapot high so the stream falls from a distance, raising a thin froth on the glass — a small test, my host said, of whether the tea has been made properly. The rooftop looked out over the undulating ochre skyline of the medina, and the morning light washed the walls in a color somewhere between brick and burnt orange. This is the reason Marrakech is called the Red City: a nearly century-old municipal code requires that buildings in the old city and parts of the new town keep this exact reddish tone, so even the McDonald's sign here has been quietly recolored to match.

At nine o'clock sharp I entered the Bahia Palace, paying seventy dirhams for admission after a fifteen-minute queue. This nineteenth-century palace became the place where I spent the most deliberate time on this trip — nearly two and a half hours moving room by room. Stepping through the narrow entrance corridor into the first courtyard, the light suddenly softened, and every wall was covered in hand-cut zellige tilework, star and diamond shapes assembled piece by piece by craftsmen. Looking closely, the seams are never perfectly aligned, and that human imprecision somehow gives the whole wall a sense of breathing. Further in, the private quarters of the grand vizier Si Moussa are topped by an entire ceiling of carved cedar, darkened with age but still sharp in its calligraphic and floral detail. My guide called this ceiling style 'artesonado' — craftsmen assemble the whole modular pattern on the ground first, then hoist it into place piece by piece, a single ceiling sometimes taking a year to complete. Deeper still are four separate courtyards, each built for a different wife, each with different proportions, light, and tile patterns — allegedly distributed according to favor, which struck me as a far more telling detail than anything printed in a guidebook: architecture here as a language of power. At midday, sunlight fell directly into the central courtyard, reflecting the carved archways in the pool below, and I stood there for nearly twenty minutes simply watching the shadows creep across the tiles like a slow clock. One practical note: there's little shade inside, and by noon the crowds and heat both spike sharply, so I'd recommend visiting between nine and eleven, as I did.

Marrakech's architecture never tries to convince you of permanence — it lets you watch time being carved into the wall, layer by layer.

Blue Gardens and a Pilgrimage in Design: Majorelle, Saint Laurent, and the Secret Garden

At one in the afternoon I took a taxi to the Jardin Majorelle in the new town district of Gueliz, a fifteen-minute ride I negotiated down to thirty dirhams before getting in — metered taxis aren't the norm here, so agreeing on a fare beforehand is essential. Garden admission runs about one hundred fifty dirhams, with a combined ticket to the Yves Saint Laurent Museum for another hundred, which I found entirely worth it. Painter Jacques Majorelle painted his villa in the 1930s in an intensely saturated cobalt blue that now bears his name; up close it carries a faint violet undertone, and under midday sun it throws back an almost blinding brightness against the cacti, bamboo, and bougainvillea planted around it. Yves Saint Laurent and his partner Pierre Bergé bought and restored the garden in the 1980s, and Saint Laurent reportedly said this was the only place he ever felt truly at peace; after his death, part of his ashes were scattered here, near a memorial column beside the rose garden. The adjacent Saint Laurent Museum is itself a piece of contemporary design, its architects drawing on the weave structure of Berber textiles — nearly twenty-seven thousand terracotta bricks form a relief pattern echoing the warp threads of a loom. Inside, the permanent collection traces how Saint Laurent drew color and silhouette from Morocco, including a cape inspired by Berber wedding dress, displayed under deliberately dimmed lighting with a single beam trained on the embroidery, as if asking visitors to slow their breathing to see each stitch properly.

Afterward I detoured into the old city near Rue Mouassine to visit Le Jardin Secret, admission fifty dirhams. Recently restored, this riad garden is split into an Islamic geometric courtyard and an exotic planting courtyard, and what fascinated me most was the preserved khettara — an underground gravity-fed irrigation system dating to the sixteenth century, now shown beneath glass flooring. This is the hidden hydraulic engineering that let Marrakech maintain its reputation as a garden city on the edge of the desert, channeling water down from the Atlas Mountains through invisible underground lines. A central channel divides the courtyard into four equal parts, echoing the four rivers of paradise described in the Quran, a design logic shared with the Alhambra in Spain, both belonging to the same Andalusian-Maghrebi garden tradition. By late afternoon the citrus trees released a faint floral scent mixed with damp earth from recent watering, and the quiet there felt like an extreme contrast to the market noise outside; I sat on a bench for nearly half an hour, reluctant to leave. Earlier that day I'd also visited the Medersa Ben Youssef, admission fifty dirhams, its courtyard covered almost entirely in carved cedar and stucco relief, star patterns extending outward in what feels like infinite repetition — craftsmen, it's said, deliberately avoided completing any single pattern in perfect symmetry, on the belief that 'only God deserves perfection.'

Dye Vats, Blacksmiths, and Roasted Lamb: The Craft Aesthetics of Souk Alleys

At four in the afternoon I slipped into the dyers' souk, Souk Sebbaghine, near Rahba Kedima. I smelled it before I turned into the lane — a sharp mix of wool grease, indigo, and charcoal smoke. Freshly dyed wool and silk hung overhead on both sides, ranging from deep indigo to saffron yellow, forming a dripping rainbow tunnel over the alley. A dyer was submerging a bundle of wool into a boiling vat; his forearms carried a permanent bluish-grey stain from years of contact with the dye. Mixing Berber with French, he explained that the indigo here still comes from locally grown woad, fixed with alum before dyeing, and that a single bundle of wool needs five to seven dips before reaching market depth. Further along is the blacksmiths' alley, where hammering rings out from dawn to dusk, old craftsmen forging traditional copper trays and lamps over primitive bellows-fed charcoal forges. Sparks flew and I instinctively stepped back, while one smith, working without so much as safety goggles, laughed and said, 'I've heard this sound for forty years — more reliable than any alarm clock.' In some ways these alley traces of handwork feel closer to the city's real street art than any painted mural — not marks on a wall, but the residue of hands repeating the same motion, day after day, for generations.

Growing hungry, I made my way to a lane behind the Bab Doukkala market known locally as Mechoui Alaouiyine, or 'roast lamb alley.' Here, whole lambs are slow-roasted from early morning in underground clay ovens for six to seven hours, and long before I reached the end of the alley the smell of dripping fat hitting hot coals had already found my nose. I chose the stall with the longest line of locals, and watched the owner haul out a whole shoulder from the pit oven, tearing it apart by hand and weighing it on an old scale. I ordered roughly three hundred grams, served with cumin salt, fresh bread, and a small dish of preserved lemon, for about seventy dirhams total. The meat was tender enough to dissolve without much chewing, its charred edge crackling against the cumin and coarse salt, and I finished the whole portion in one sitting, practically wiping the plate clean with the last piece of bread. A regular customer next to me mentioned that most stalls in this alley are three generations deep in the same family — 'my grandfather ate here, my father ate here, now I bring my son' — and suddenly a simple plate of roast lamb carried the weight of family memory layered on top of it.

Lights Up Over Jemaa el-Fnaa: Storytellers, Snake Charmers, and a Street-Level Theater

By half past six the sky had turned orange, and I walked into Jemaa el-Fnaa just as the square seemed to switch on, visibly filling from empty to packed within minutes. Orange juice carts lined up in rows, four dirhams a glass, fresh-squeezed and sharply sweet; snake charmers tipped cobras out of baskets after a wavering blast on their double-reed pipes, and the crowd instinctively cleared a circle of space; henna artists spread out hand-drawn sample sheets, a palm-sized design running fifty to eighty dirhams, the price flexing depending on whether you're recognized as a tourist, with haggling an unspoken courtesy here. What held me longest was a circle formed around a halqa storyteller, an elderly man cross-legged on a low stool, spinning a dialect tale about a cunning merchant and a spirit, his voice rising and falling in a rhythm that pulled me in even though I couldn't follow the words. He stopped mid-suspense and opened his palm for coins, and only then did I realize this mid-story pause for payment is itself part of the performance's design — UNESCO listed the square's oral traditions as intangible cultural heritage precisely for reasons like this.

Nothing here hangs on a wall waiting to be admired. The entire square is a theater that replays itself every evening and is never written down as a script.

Friday Couscous at the Riad: The Etiquette of Hospitality and the Philosophy of Three Teas

On my last Friday before leaving, my riad host Fatima invited me to stay for lunch — Friday, she explained, is the day the whole family gathers for couscous, after the midday communal prayer at the mosque, and on this day nearly every kitchen in the neighborhood releases the same smell into the street. She brought out a large plate of couscous steamed three times until fine-grained, topped with stewed pumpkin, carrots, courgette, and lamb cooked to falling-apart tenderness, finished with raisins and crisp fried onion. The custom is to roll the couscous into small balls with bread or fingers and eat it that way, and if the juice runs down your hand, no one minds. While refilling my bowl, Fatima recited an old saying: the first glass of tea is as bitter as life, the second as sweet as love, the third as gentle as death — referring to the tradition of pouring three consecutive glasses of mint tea, where the host's repeated insistence isn't mere politeness but a gesture that shouldn't be refused; in many households, stopping after one glass borders on impolite. She also reminded me that when visiting a Moroccan home, it's proper to accept food and tea with the right hand, since the left is traditionally considered unclean, and that during Ramadan, eating or drinking in public during daylight hours should be avoided out of respect for those fasting. Outside, neighborhood children came home from school shouting with laughter, and her husband Hakim trimmed the citrus trees in the courtyard. That Friday afternoon felt, in its own way, as much a part of this city's design language as any carved ceiling or tiled courtyard I'd photographed — except this beauty lives at the dinner table and inside the teapot, and it doesn't require an admission ticket.

On the morning I left Marrakech, I circled back once more to the Koutoubia square. The call to prayer rose again from the minaret as usual, a street cleaner sprayed water along the lane, and the air carried the same jasmine and dust. Five days earlier I'd thought I'd come to look at architecture and museums. Leaving, I realized what actually stayed with me was the indigo that wouldn't wash off a dyer's fingers, the three generations queuing at roast lamb alley, and Fatima's insistence — 'have one more glass of tea.' The design aesthetic of this red city, it turns out, was never only on the walls. It was also in the glass of tea passed back and forth, again and again, between one person and another.
