中文

我在京都的最後一趟旅程,其實是從一個「不去哪裡」的決定開始的。行前我把清水寺、伏見稻荷、嵐山竹林這些名字從行程表上一一劃掉,傳訊息給住在西陣、經營一家小型町屋民宿的朋友純子問她:「如果只有妳自己,妳會去哪裡?」她隔天回我一句話:「早上五點半,鞍馬車站見。」我後來才明白,這句簡短的回覆,幾乎定義了我接下來八天在京都看到的一切。

那是十月底,楓葉還沒紅透,但空氣裡已經有一種乾冷的、帶著杉木香氣的味道,是我在市區完全沒聞過的。

清晨五點半:鞍馬山的寂靜小徑

從出町柳站搭乘叡山電鐵的鞍馬線,車程約三十分鐘,單程票價四百二十日圓。這班電車本身就是一場預演——車廂中段有一大片弧形車窗,行經「紅葉隧道」段時,連車內燈都會刻意調暗,讓乘客好好看那片被拱起的楓樹枝葉。十月底葉子還青,純子說十一月中旬會整片轉紅,「但那時候車廂會擠到妳連呼吸都困難」。清晨六點過後的車廂裡,只有我們和三個揹著登山杖的老先生。

鞍馬站出來,空氣冷得讓我下意識縮起肩膀。純子帶我先繞去鞍馬寺山門旁的一座不起眼小社,她說這是當地人上山前拜的「息災」神,遊客幾乎不會停留超過三十秒就往仁王門走去。我們則沿著鞍馬寺境內小路一路往上,腳下是被踩得發亮的石階與交錯的樹根,山裡非常安靜,只有風吹過杉木林的聲音,偶爾夾雜遠處啄木鳥敲樹幹的節奏聲。整段從鞍馬到貴船的越嶺步道大約四公里,我們走了將近兩個半小時,中途在「木の根道」歇了腳——那是一整片盤根錯節、幾乎浮出地表的巨大杉樹根,踩上去要非常小心,純子提醒我這段路下雨後會濕滑,建議穿有抓地力的健走鞋而非時尚感的靴子。

翻過山頭下坡到貴船一側時,已經快九點,山谷裡雲霧還沒散,貴船川的水聲從很遠的地方就聽得到,清脆、持續、像有人在山谷裡不斷倒水。

在鞍馬的杉林裡,我第一次理解「寂靜」不是沒有聲音,而是每一種聲音都被聽見。

川床上的流水麵線:貴船「ひろ文」的心跳一餐

下山後我們直接走到貴船神社參道旁的「ひろ文」,這是一間有超過百年歷史的料理旅館,夏季以「流しそうめん」(流水麵線)聞名——店家把青竹剖半架成一道長長的滑水道,冰涼的山泉水從高處注入,麵線隨著水流一節一節滑下來,客人要用筷子在水裡精準夾住。十月底已經過了流水麵線的季節,但純子提前打了電話問老闆娘能否通融,老闆娘笑著說「妳們是本地人吧,那就破例」,於是我們坐在川床棧台上,吃到了店家私下保留的最後一輪。

那碗麵線用的是山泉水鎮涼,吸進嘴裡的瞬間先是冰,接著才嚐到蕎麥麵線本身微微的穀物甜味,沾醬是柴魚與淡口醬油調的,鹹鮮但不搶味,配著切得極細的青蔥與現磨山葵。套餐另外附上一小碗鯖魚棒壽司與炊飯,價位大約三千八百日圓,比市區蕎麥麵貴上不少,但坐在幾乎貼著溪水的木棧道上,聽著水流拍打橋墩、偶爾有山鳥飛過頭頂,那頓飯吃得極慢,反而成了整趟旅程裡我印象最深的一餐。老闆娘後來端出一小碟醃漬紫蘇梅,說是「給走完山路的人壓壓胃」,那種被在地人順手照顧的感覺,是任何米其林餐廳都給不了的。

一乘寺的午後書香與靜謐轉角

下午兩點,我們搭巴士回到市區北邊的一乘寺,這一帶因為詩仙堂與曼殊院而被京都人視為「秋天真正該去賞楓,但沒人愛講」的地方——理由很現實:停車位少、巴士班次不密集,旅行團嫌麻煩。詩仙堂的入場費五百日圓,進門後要脫鞋踩上光滑的木地板,坐在緣側望向枯山水庭園,砂紋被耙成一圈圈同心圓,幾株楓樹才剛開始泛紅邊。我們在那裡坐了將近四十分鐘沒說話,只聽見竹製「添水」(鹿威し)固定時間敲擊石頭的「叩」一聲,悠長得像整座庭園都在呼吸。

走出詩仙堂,純子帶我拐進一條幾乎沒有店面招牌的小巷,盡頭是獨立書店「惠文社一乘寺店」。這裡不像蔦屋書店那樣被拍照打卡淹沒,書架分類是店員手寫的字條,角落擺著在地陶藝家的小型展售,我買了一本只有日文版、介紹京都町屋修復工法的攝影集,店員用棉紙細心包好,還附了一張手繪的一乘寺散步地圖,標出附近三間她私心推薦的甜點店。

西陣町屋裡的節分:與老奶奶一起撒豆驅邪

傍晚我們回到純子在西陣經營的町屋民宿,巧遇隔壁八十二歲的阿嬤智子婆婆在整理她那袋炒大豆——原來我到訪那天恰逢附近廬山寺舉行的「鬼法樂」節分儀式前一天的社區小型撒豆練習。智子婆婆說,西陣一帶許多老家庭至今仍嚴格遵守「年男年女」撒豆的傳統,也就是由家中生肖與當年對應的男女成員負責在門口喊著「鬼は外、福は内」把炒豆撒出去,絕不能讓外人代勞,否則「福氣會認錯人」。她也提醒我,傳統上撒完豆要按自己的實歲數再加一顆吃下去,「才算把福氣吃進肚子裡」,而且千萬別把生豆撒出去,「鬼會嫌棄我們不用心,隔天又跑回來」。

隔天清晨我們真的去了廬山寺,看穿著紅、青、黑三色鬼面的表演者在境內被僧侶與信眾持杖驅趕、撒豆轟出寺門,鼓聲、笑聲與豆子打在地上的聲音混在一起,完全不是觀光噱頭,而是附近居民全家老小穿著厚外套、帶著保溫瓶站在原地看了快一小時的日常儀式。

京都最迷人的從來不是庭園本身,而是庭園旁邊,那個八十二歲的婆婆願意把她家的驅鬼豆分我一把。

船岡温泉的夜色收尾:錢湯裡的京都日常

旅程最後一夜,純子帶我走進西陣巷子深處的「船岡温泉」——一棟建於大正時期、被列為國家登錄有形文化財的公共澡堂,外觀還留著唐破風式的木造玄關,天花板上的透雕木刻是原封不動保留下來的古物。入場費大人一次四百九十日圓,比一般錢湯稍貴,但完全值得。更衣室裡幾位收工的計程車司機邊擦身體邊用京都腔閒聊今天的天氣,浴池分成藥湯、電氣風呂與露天湯,水溫燙得我第一次下水差點縮腳,泡到第三次才敢完全坐進去,皮膚被熱水泡得微微發紅,毛孔裡全是淡淡的硫磺與草藥味。

走出澡堂時已近晚上十點,巷子裡路燈昏黃,幾隻貓窩在關了燈的和菓子店門口,身體還帶著溫泉的餘熱,冷空氣打在臉上格外清醒。純子說她從小跟著媽媽來這裡洗澡,「觀光客覺得京都是神社寺廟,我們覺得京都是這鍋熱水。」我站在那條安靜的巷子裡,忽然覺得這趟避開所有著名景點的旅程,反而讓我第一次真正靠近了這座城市的呼吸節奏。

ENGLISH

My final trip to Kyoto began with a decision about where not to go. Before I even boarded the plane, I crossed Kiyomizu-dera, Fushimi Inari, and the Arashiyama bamboo grove off my itinerary and texted Junko, a friend who runs a small machiya guesthouse in the Nishijin textile district. I asked her a simple question: if it were just you, where would you go? Her reply came the next morning, just one line: 5:30am, Kurama Station. I didn't know it yet, but that single sentence would shape everything I saw over the next eight days. It was late October, the maples hadn't turned yet, but the air already carried a dry, cedar-scented chill I never once smelled downtown.

5:30am: The Silent Trail Over Mount Kurama

From Demachiyanagi Station, the Eizan Railway's Kurama Line takes about thirty minutes and costs 420 yen one way. The train itself is a kind of rehearsal for the day — midway through the ride, the carriage passes through a curved section of track nicknamed the Maple Tunnel, where the interior lights dim automatically so passengers can watch the arching branches overhead. The leaves were still green in late October; Junko told me they'd be fully red by mid-November, but by then the carriage would be too packed to breathe. That morning, past six a.m., our car held only us and three elderly hikers with trekking poles.

Stepping off at Kurama, the cold made me instinctively pull my shoulders in. Before heading up the main gate to Kurama-dera, Junko led me to a small, unmarked shrine locals stop at for travel safety — the kind of place tourists walk past without a second glance. From there we climbed the mountain path connecting Kurama to Kifune, roughly four kilometers, threading between polished stone steps and exposed cedar roots. The forest was extraordinarily quiet; the only sounds were wind moving through the cedars and, occasionally, the distant tapping of a woodpecker. It took us nearly two and a half hours, with a stop at the Konone-michi, a stretch where massive roots surface above the soil like knotted cables — Junko warned me it gets slick after rain and that proper hiking shoes matter far more than stylish boots here. By the time we descended into the Kifune valley, it was nearly nine, mist still pooling low over the river, and we could hear the water long before we saw it — bright, continuous, like someone endlessly pouring from a great height.

In the cedar forest above Kurama, I understood for the first time that silence isn't the absence of sound — it's the presence of every sound, finally audible.

A Heartbeat of a Meal: Flowing Noodles at Hirobun in Kifune

After the descent, we walked straight to Hirobun, a ryokan-restaurant more than a century old along the approach to Kifune Shrine, famous in summer for nagashi somen — noodles sent sliding down a length of split bamboo filled with icy mountain water, which diners chase and catch with chopsticks. Late October is technically past the season, but Junko called ahead and asked the owner if she might make an exception. The owner laughed and said, since you're locals, fine — and seated us on the riverside platform for what turned out to be her last private serving of the year.

The noodles, chilled in mountain spring water, hit first as pure cold, then gave way to the faint grain-sweetness of soba underneath. The dipping sauce, a light soy and bonito reduction, was savory without overpowering, garnished with finely sliced scallion and freshly grated wasabi. The set also included a small mackerel pressed sushi and a bowl of seasoned rice, running about 3,800 yen — considerably more than a bowl of soba in the city, but sitting on wooden planks nearly level with the rushing water, listening to the current slap against the pillars while mountain birds occasionally cut overhead, we ate slower than I have anywhere else in Japan. Near the end, the owner brought out a small dish of pickled shiso plum, unprompted, telling us it would settle our stomachs after the hike. That instinct to look after us, without being asked, is something no Michelin dining room has ever offered me.

A Quiet Afternoon of Books and Moss in Ichijoji

By two in the afternoon we'd taken a bus back into the city's northern edge, to Ichijoji, an area locals consider the real place to see autumn color — precisely because almost no one bothers to go. Parking is scarce, buses are infrequent, and tour groups find it inconvenient. Entry to Shisen-do, the seventeenth-century villa and garden, costs 500 yen. Inside, we removed our shoes onto polished wood floors and sat at the veranda's edge facing a dry rock garden raked into concentric circles, a handful of maples just beginning to redden at their edges. We sat there for nearly forty minutes without speaking, listening only for the periodic knock of a bamboo shishi-odori striking stone — a long, hollow sound that seemed to make the whole garden breathe.

Leaving Shisen-do, Junko turned us down an alley with no visible signage, ending at Keibunsha's Ichijoji branch, an independent bookstore that has somehow avoided becoming an Instagram pilgrimage site the way Tsutaya has. The shelves are organized by handwritten notes from staff, and a corner display features small ceramics from local artisans. I bought a Japanese-only photography book on the restoration techniques used in old machiya townhouses; the clerk wrapped it carefully in washi paper and slipped in a hand-drawn map of the neighborhood marking three dessert shops she personally recommends.

Setsubun in a Nishijin Machiya: Throwing Beans with a Neighbor

That evening, back at Junko's guesthouse in Nishijin, we ran into her eighty-two-year-old neighbor, Tomoko-san, sorting a bag of roasted soybeans on her doorstep. It turned out my visit coincided with a small neighborhood rehearsal ahead of the Setsubun bean-throwing ritual held the following day at nearby Rozan-ji temple. Tomoko-san explained that many old families in Nishijin still strictly observe the toshi-otoko and toshi-onna custom — only family members whose zodiac year matches the current one are permitted to throw the beans at the doorway while shouting oni wa soto, fuku wa uchi, demons out, fortune in. Letting an outsider do it, she said, risks the fortune arriving at the wrong door. Afterward, tradition holds you eat one bean for each year of your age, plus one extra, so the fortune settles properly in your stomach — and never, under any circumstance, throw raw, unroasted beans, or the demons will think you weren't serious and come straight back the next day.

The next morning we actually made it to Rozan-ji, where performers in red, blue, and black demon masks were chased through the temple grounds by monks and parishioners wielding sticks and handfuls of beans, drums and laughter mixing with the rattle of beans hitting the stone paths. It wasn't staged for tourists at all — entire families from the neighborhood stood bundled in winter coats with thermoses, watching an hour-long ritual that, for them, was simply Tuesday.

The most beautiful thing in Kyoto was never the garden itself — it was the eighty-two-year-old woman next door, willing to hand me a fistful of her own demon-repelling beans.

A Night at Funaoka Onsen: The City's Quiet Heartbeat

On our last evening, Junko led me deep into a Nishijin backstreet to Funaoka Onsen, a public bathhouse built in the Taishō era and registered as a national tangible cultural property. Its wooden entrance still bears an ornate karahafu gable roof, and the carved wooden ceiling panels inside are original, untouched. Admission runs 490 yen for adults, slightly more than an average sento, and entirely worth it. In the changing room, a few taxi drivers finishing their shift toweled off while chatting in thick Kyoto dialect about the day's weather. The baths themselves are divided into a medicinal soak, an electric bath, and an open-air pool — the water was hot enough that I flinched on my first attempt, and only managed a full soak on my third try, my skin flushed pink, my pores filled with the faint scent of sulfur and herbs.

We stepped out close to ten p.m., the alley lit dim orange, a few cats curled outside a darkened wagashi shop, our bodies still radiating warmth against the cold night air, which felt almost bracingly clarifying on the skin. Junko told me she'd been coming here with her mother since childhood. Tourists think Kyoto is temples and shrines, she said. We think Kyoto is this pot of hot water. Standing in that quiet alley, having spent eight days deliberately avoiding every famous landmark, I felt for the first time that I had actually gotten close to the city's real, breathing rhythm.
