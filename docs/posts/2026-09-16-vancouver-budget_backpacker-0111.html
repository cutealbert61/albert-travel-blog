中文

清晨六點四十分,飛機還沒完全停穩,我就已經看見機艙窗外那片灰藍色的海岸線——溫哥華國際機場(YVR)被夾在喬治亞海峽與雪山之間,像是被人隨手放在那裡。入境行李轉盤旁的空氣有一種奇特的乾淨感,混著海水鹹味與潮濕木頭的氣味,那是我後來十天都沒有真正擺脫掉的溫哥華氣味。背包客的旅程從這一刻正式開始,而我的第一個任務,是搞清楚怎麼花最少的錢進城。

落地溫哥華:機場快線與背包客的第一夜

YVR航廈裡有清楚的黃色指標寫著Canada Line,這條天車線直通市中心的Waterfront站,單程車資加上機場附加費是11.55加幣(2024年價格,一般市區段是2.5加幣左右,機場多收約5加幣附加費),車程大約26分鐘。我拖著28吋登機箱走進月台時是清晨七點半,車廂裡除了幾個西裝筆挺趕早班會議的上班族,還有兩個跟我一樣一臉沒睡飽、揹著大背包的背包客,我們在Broadway-City Hall站互看一眼,笑了一下,那種「我們都是同類」的默契,是背包旅行裡很奇妙的瞬間。我訂的是Thurlow街上的HI Vancouver Downtown青旅,六人混合宿舍一晚45加幣,櫃檯要到下午三點才能check-in,但他們讓我先寄放行李。於是我沿著Davie街一路走到Denman街和英吉利灣(English Bay)海邊,九月初的溫哥華早晨,空氣冷得讓人清醒,沙灘上已經有人在慢跑、遛狗,海面上飄著薄霧,我站在那裡啃著便利商店買的肉桂捲當早餐,心裡想著:這座城市步調比我想像中慢很多。

下午終於拿到房卡,床位在上舖靠窗,窗外正對著一條巷子,能聽見隔壁咖啡館研磨咖啡豆的聲音。青旅公共廚房晚上八點左右總是最熱鬧,韓國背包客在煮泡麵、澳洲女生在切蔬菜準備義大利麵,大家一邊煮飯一邊交換情報:哪個健行步道免費、哪個博物館學生證有打折。我很快就從隔壁床位的德國男生口中得到了第一個重要情報——Grouse Grind。

Grouse Grind:溫哥華人的天然運動階梯

隔天清晨六點我就走出旅館,天色還沒完全亮,Thurlow街上只有清潔車在灑水。我先走路到Waterfront站,搭SeaBus橫越布勒內灣(Burrard Inlet)到北溫哥華的Lonsdale Quay,船程十二分鐘,單程用Compass卡是3.15加幣,而且能跟一般公車轉乘不用加錢。海面上晨霧還沒散,遠方的Grouse山頭若隱若現,甲板上冷風吹得我縮起脖子,卻莫名覺得很清醒。下船後轉236號公車到Grouse Mountain纜車站,車程約二十分鐘,車資同樣算在轉乘裡。

Grouse Grind的登山口就在纜車站旁邊一個不起眼的木頭階梯,沒有售票口、沒有柵欄,只有一塊牌子寫著「2.9公里,爬升853公尺,平均完成時間1.5至2小時」。我八點十五分正式踏進步道,一開始的階梯又高又陡,踩下去是濕滑的泥土混著木頭碎屑,兩旁是又高又密的針葉林,陽光被完全遮住,只剩斑駁的光點灑在地上。大概走到三分之一處,我開始聽到自己的心跳聲蓋過鳥叫聲,汗水浸濕了背後的排汗衣,手機完全沒有訊號。步道中段有一小段被本地人戲稱為「Death Cookie」的陡坡,是連續的巨大裸露樹根與石塊,必須手腳並用。我在那裡遇到堵車——沒錯,健行也會塞車,因為步道基本上只有一人寬,速度快的當地人會喊「on your left」示意超車,我趕緊側身讓路,才知道原來很多溫哥華人把這條步道當成每天下班後的健身房,一週爬三次是常態。我花了整整一小時五十分鐘才爬上山頂,腿在發抖,但一走出樹林、看到觀景台外整片喬治亞海峽與市區天際線攤在腳下時,那種喘不過氣的感動蓋過了所有痠痛。下山我選擇搭續車纜車,因為Grouse Grind規定只能上不能下(下坡對膝蓋和步道生態都是負擔),纜車票對「剛爬完的人」有優惠價,出示手上蓋的Grind完成章可以用約15加幣的折扣價下山,比原價的65加幣門票便宜非常多——這是我後來才知道、事先完全沒查到的省錢細節。

爬到一半聽見自己心跳蓋過鳥叫聲的那一刻,我才真正明白為什麼溫哥華人管健身房叫多餘。

Granville Island市場到深夜的Japadog:三餐的真實滋味

中午時分我搭乘從Hornby街碼頭出發的迷你渡輪Aquabus,單程約4加幣,五分鐘就到了Granville Island。公眾市場(Public Market)裡人聲鼎沸,魚販在冰塊上排列剛到貨的太平洋鮭魚,叫賣聲跟磅秤敲擊聲混在一起。我先在Oyama Sausage攤位買了一份野牛肉香腸搭配酸豆芥末醬,7.5加幣,肉質紮實帶點煙燻香氣;接著又忍不住排隊買了Lee's Donuts的蘋果肉桂甜甜圈,現炸的外皮還燙手,咬下去外酥內軟,肉桂香混著油炸香氣直衝鼻腔,3.75加幣一個,我站在市場外的碼頭邊吃,海鷗在頭頂盤旋伺機而動,旁邊的加拿大大叔笑著提醒我「千萬別把甜甜圈舉太高,牠們會直接飛下來搶」。晚餐我特地走到市中心Burrard街和Smithe街口的Japadog餐車,這台餐車在溫哥華已經是傳奇等級的存在,我點了招牌的Terimayo——照燒醬淋在德式香腸上,加美乃滋、海苔絲跟蔥花,6.75加幣。第一口咬下去,鹹甜的照燒醬跟濃郁美乃滋在嘴裡化開,海苔的鮮味把整體味道往「日式」拉,完全顛覆我對熱狗堡的想像,排隊的隊伍裡有西裝上班族,也有跟我一樣的背包客,大家站在路邊就這樣吃了起來,晚風夾著油煙味跟隔壁酒吧飄出的音樂聲,是我在溫哥華吃過最有記憶點的一餐。

史丹利公園海堤:圖騰柱下的原住民故事與雨中日常

第四天下起了溫哥華式的細雨——不是傾盆大雨,而是那種綿綿密密、幾乎感覺不到落下卻能把外套慢慢浸濕的雨。我發現路上幾乎沒有人撐傘,青旅老闆笑著跟我說「真正的溫哥華人不用傘,只用Gore-Tex外套」,我這才注意到街上一半以上的人都穿著同樣款式的機能外套,這幾乎是一種無聲的城市制服。我租了一台Spokes Bicycle Rentals的腳踏車,一小時9加幣,沿著史丹利公園的海堤(Seawall)騎,全長大約9公里,右手邊是布勒內灣的浪拍打著石堤,左手邊是巨大的西部側柏與花旗松,樹幹粗得三個人合抱不住。騎到布洛克頓角(Brockton Point)時,我看到一整排高聳的原住民圖騰柱矗立在草地上,雕刻的渡鴉、熊與雷鳥圖案在雨中顯得更加深沉。剛好遇到Talaysay Tours的一場小型導覽,帶隊的是一位Squamish族的年輕嚮導,他讓我們在開始前先安靜站著,聞一聞雨後雪松的味道,他說這種味道是他從小到大「回家」的氣味。他解釋這些圖騰柱其實大多不是史丹利公園原生的,而是從其他部落遷移保存至此,而整座史丹利公園所在的土地,其實從未被正式割讓,至今仍是Musqueam、Squamish與Tsleil-Waututh三個原住民族的傳統領域——這句話溫哥華人幾乎在任何公開場合開場前都會提到,叫做「Land Acknowledgement」,一開始我覺得只是形式,聽完導覽才明白這是這座城市認真在處理的歷史傷口。

雨後雪松的味道,對他來說是回家的氣味;對我來說,是第一次真正聽懂這座城市歷史的瞬間。

騎完海堤回程經過蓋士鎮(Gastown),那座著名的蒸氣鐘準時在整點噴出白色蒸氣、吹響汽笛,遊客們舉著手機圍過去拍照,我卻更著迷於鵝卵石街道旁一間間工業風咖啡館飄出的烘豆香氣。溫哥華人對咖啡極度講究,JJ Bean跟Revolver這類獨立咖啡館幾乎每條街都有,一杯手沖大約5到6加幣,店員會很自然地跟你聊起今天的日曬處理法,這種對咖啡近乎信仰的態度,跟這座城市對戶外運動的執著,其實出自同一種性格——講究、慢工出細活,卻又低調得不張揚。

帳單攤開:十天花費全記錄與避雷清單

回旅館整理收據那晚,我把十天的花費攤開在床上算了一遍。住宿:HI Vancouver Downtown青旅六人房均價每晚48加幣(含稅),十天約480加幣;交通:買了月票不划算,單次Compass卡搭配轉乘,平均每天10到12加幣;飲食:如果像我一樣以市場、餐車跟超市self-cook交替,平均每天35到40加幣就能吃得很滿足,若餐餐上餐廳,一天輕鬆花到70加幣以上;活動:Grouse Grind本身免費(只有下山纜車要付費),Capilano吊橋公園門票要價65加幣起,老實說我後來去了免費的Lynn Canyon吊橋公園,搖晃程度差不多刺激,完全不用錢,這是我覺得最值得分享的省錢情報。要提醒的避雷點還有幾個:溫哥華餐廳結帳時稅金是額外加上去的(GST 5%加PST 7%,餐廳有時還會另外收酒精稅),加上小費文化非常固定,一般是15%到20%,結帳前一定要心裡先加個三成再決定要不要點;市中心東側的Downtown Eastside(especially East Hastings街一帶)白天觀光經過沒問題,但晚上建議避開獨自穿越,那裡是加拿大知名的無家可歸與藥物問題最集中的街區,青旅員工也再三提醒過我;另外溫哥華雖然大麻合法,但公共場合吸食其實仍有地區限制,常常聞到路邊飄來的氣味,但別誤會可以隨處點燃。整體十天下來,我抓的預算是每天平均110到130加幣(含住宿交通餐飲),比起加拿大東岸城市已經算相對親民,尤其只要願意早起、願意流汗爬山、願意在市場攤位前排隊,溫哥華其實是一座對背包客很友善的城市。

離開的那天早上,我又搭了一次Canada Line到機場,車窗外掠過的還是那片灰藍色海岸線,只是這次我聞著自己身上還沒散去的雪松與海水味道,忽然覺得,這座城市教會我的不只是怎麼省下幾塊加幣,而是怎麼放慢腳步,去聽懂一個地方真正的呼吸節奏。

ENGLISH

At six forty in the morning, before the plane had fully touched the tarmac, I could already see it through the window — that grey-blue coastline where Vancouver International Airport sits wedged between the Georgia Strait and a wall of snow-capped mountains, as if someone had simply set it down there. Near the baggage carousel the air had a strange cleanness to it, salt water mixed with something like wet cedar, a smell I never quite shook off for the next ten days. My backpacking trip officially began right there, and my first task was figuring out the cheapest way into the city.

Touchdown in Vancouver: The Canada Line and My First Night as a Backpacker

Clear yellow signs inside YVR point toward the Canada Line, the SkyTrain that runs straight into downtown's Waterfront Station. A one-way fare with the airport surcharge runs CAD 11.55 (2024 pricing — a normal in-city fare is closer to CAD 2.50, with about a CAD 5 airport add-on), and the ride takes roughly 26 minutes. I dragged my 28-inch suitcase onto the platform at half past seven, sharing the car with a few suit-and-tie commuters and two other backpackers who looked as sleep-deprived as I felt. We caught each other's eye at Broadway-City Hall station and just laughed — that quiet, instant recognition of belonging to the same tribe is one of the strange little joys of backpacking. I'd booked a bed at HI Vancouver Downtown on Thurlow Street, CAD 45 a night for a six-bed mixed dorm, but check-in wasn't until 3pm, so I dropped my bag and walked down Davie Street toward Denman and English Bay. Early September mornings here are sharp enough to wake you up instantly — joggers and dog walkers already out on the sand, a thin fog sitting over the water. I stood there eating a gas-station cinnamon roll for breakfast, thinking: this city moves a lot slower than I expected.

By afternoon I finally got my key card, a top bunk by the window, close enough to hear the espresso grinder from the café next door. The hostel kitchen came alive around eight that night — Korean backpackers cooking instant noodles, an Australian girl chopping vegetables for pasta, everyone trading intel between bites: which hike was free, which museum gave student discounts. It was my German bunkmate who first mentioned the name that would define my next morning: Grouse Grind.

The Grouse Grind: Vancouver's Natural StairMaster

I walked out of the hostel at six the next morning, the sky still dim, only a street-cleaning truck out on Thurlow. I walked to Waterfront Station and took the SeaBus across Burrard Inlet to Lonsdale Quay in North Vancouver — a twelve-minute crossing for CAD 3.15 one-way on a Compass card, transfers included. Morning fog still clung to the water, Grouse Mountain barely visible in the distance, the deck wind cold enough to make me hunch my shoulders, though somehow it cleared my head completely. From Lonsdale Quay I caught bus 236 to the Grouse Mountain base, about twenty minutes, the fare already covered by my transfer.

The Grind trailhead is an unassuming wooden staircase right beside the gondola station — no ticket booth, no gate, just a sign reading '2.9 km, 853m elevation gain, average completion time 1.5 to 2 hours.' I stepped onto the trail at 8:15am. The stairs start steep and slick, packed dirt mixed with wood chips, dense conifer forest blocking out most of the light, just scattered patches of sun on the ground. By about a third of the way up, my own heartbeat was louder than the birdsong, sweat soaking through my base layer, phone signal completely gone. Midway through there's a section locals call the 'Death Cookie' — a brutal stretch of exposed roots and boulders where you're basically climbing with your hands. That's where I hit a bottleneck — yes, hiking trails have traffic jams too, because the path is barely wide enough for one person, and faster locals shout 'on your left' to pass. I stepped aside, and realized many Vancouverites treat this trail as their after-work gym, climbing it three times a week like it's nothing. It took me an hour and fifty minutes to reach the top, legs shaking, but stepping out of the tree line to see the entire Georgia Strait and the downtown skyline spread out below wiped out every ache instantly. Descending on foot is discouraged (hard on knees and on trail erosion), so I took the gondola down. A stamp on my hand proving I'd just finished the Grind got me a discounted descent fare of around CAD 15, far below the standalone CAD 65 gondola ticket — a money-saving detail I only discovered on the mountain, not in any guidebook beforehand.

The moment I heard my own heartbeat drown out the birdsong halfway up, I finally understood why Vancouverites think gym memberships are a waste of money.

From Granville Island Market to a Midnight Japadog: What Three Meals Actually Cost

Around noon I hopped on the tiny Aquabus ferry from the Hornby Street dock — about CAD 4 one-way, a five-minute hop over to Granville Island. The Public Market was loud and alive, fishmongers laying out fresh Pacific salmon on ice, the sound of scales clinking against metal trays mixing with the calls of vendors. I grabbed a bison sausage with caper-mustard sauce from the Oyama Sausage stall for CAD 7.50 — dense, meaty, with a good smoky edge — then couldn't resist queuing at Lee's Donuts for an apple-cinnamon donut, fried to order, still hot to the touch, CAD 3.75. Biting through the crisp shell into the soft interior, cinnamon and hot oil hit my nose at the same time. I ate it standing at the edge of the dock while seagulls circled overhead, and a local guy nearby laughed and warned me, 'Don't hold that donut up too high, or one of them will just dive straight for it.' For dinner I walked to the corner of Burrard and Smithe downtown, home to the legendary Japadog cart. I ordered the signature Terimayo — a bratwurst drenched in teriyaki sauce, mayo, shredded seaweed and green onion — for CAD 6.75. The first bite was a collision of sweet-salty teriyaki and thick mayo, the seaweed pulling the whole thing distinctly Japanese, completely rewriting what I thought a hot dog could be. The line had office workers in suits standing right next to backpackers like me, everyone just eating on the sidewalk, evening air thick with grill smoke and music leaking out of the bar next door. It was, without question, the single most memorable meal of my trip.

The Stanley Park Seawall: Totem Poles, Indigenous Stories, and Life in the Rain

On day four came a distinctly Vancouver kind of rain — not a downpour, just a fine, near-invisible mist that quietly soaked through your jacket without you noticing. Almost nobody on the street carried an umbrella. My hostel manager laughed and told me, 'A real Vancouverite doesn't use an umbrella, just a Gore-Tex shell,' and I suddenly noticed that more than half the people on the street were wearing nearly identical technical jackets — an unspoken uniform for the city. I rented a bike from Spokes Bicycle Rentals for CAD 9 an hour and rode the Stanley Park Seawall, roughly 9km of paved path — waves slapping the stone wall on one side, towering western red cedars and Douglas firs on the other, trunks too thick for three people to wrap their arms around. At Brockton Point I found a row of towering Indigenous totem poles standing on the grass, ravens, bears, and thunderbirds carved into the wood, looking even darker and more solemn in the rain. I happened onto a small Talaysay Tours group led by a young Squamish guide, who had us stand quietly before starting, breathing in the smell of rain-soaked cedar — a scent he said had meant 'home' to him his whole life. He explained that most of these totem poles weren't originally from Stanley Park at all, but relocated here from other nations for preservation, and that the land the entire park sits on has never actually been formally ceded — it remains the traditional territory of the Musqueam, Squamish, and Tsleil-Waututh Nations to this day. That line — the Land Acknowledgement — gets spoken almost reflexively at the start of public events across the city. I'd assumed it was just a formality, until that tour made me understand it as a city genuinely, if imperfectly, wrestling with its own history.

The smell of rain on cedar means 'home' to him. For me, it was the moment I finally understood the history this city carries with it every day.

Riding back through Gastown, the famous Steam Clock hissed and whistled right on the hour, tourists crowding around with their phones out, but I was more drawn to the smell of roasting coffee drifting from the row of industrial-chic cafés along the cobblestone street. Vancouverites take coffee seriously — independent shops like JJ Bean and Revolver seem to appear on every block, a pour-over running about CAD 5 to 6, and baristas will happily chat about today's sun-dried processing method without being asked twice. That same quiet obsession — meticulous, unhurried, but never showy — is the same personality trait that drives this city's devotion to the outdoors.

Opening the Receipts: A Ten-Day Budget Breakdown and What to Watch Out For

That last night, I laid all ten days of receipts out on my bunk and did the math properly. Accommodation: HI Vancouver Downtown, six-bed dorm, averaging CAD 48/night with tax, about CAD 480 total. Transit: a monthly pass wasn't worth it for ten days; paying per ride with a Compass card, including transfers, averaged CAD 10-12/day. Food: alternating between markets, food carts, and self-cooked meals like I did, CAD 35-40/day was enough to eat well; eating out for every meal easily pushes past CAD 70/day. Activities: the Grouse Grind itself is free (only the gondola descent costs money), while the Capilano Suspension Bridge charges upward of CAD 65 for entry — I later found the free Lynn Canyon Suspension Bridge Park, nearly as thrilling and swaying just as much, at zero cost, probably the single best money-saving tip I picked up on this whole trip. A few things to watch for: restaurant bills add tax on top of the listed price (5% GST plus 7% PST, sometimes an additional liquor tax), and tipping culture is firmly fixed at 15-20%, so mentally add roughly 30% to any menu price before deciding what to order. The Downtown Eastside, particularly around East Hastings Street, is fine to pass through during the day but locals and hostel staff repeatedly warned me against walking through alone at night — it's one of Canada's most concentrated areas for homelessness and drug use. And while cannabis is legal in the city, public consumption is still restricted by location; you'll smell it drifting from street corners constantly, but that doesn't mean it's fair game to light up anywhere. All told, I budgeted around CAD 110-130 per day including accommodation, transit and food — genuinely reasonable by Canadian standards, especially for anyone willing to wake up early, sweat through a mountain trail, and queue patiently at a market stall.

On my last morning I rode the Canada Line back to the airport one more time, watching that same grey-blue coastline slide past the window. Only this time, catching the last traces of cedar and salt water still clinging to my jacket, I realized this city hadn't just taught me how to save a few Canadian dollars — it had taught me how to slow down enough to actually hear the rhythm a place breathes in.
