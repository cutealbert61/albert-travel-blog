中文

飛機降落瑙索里國際機場(Nausori International Airport)那天,雨剛停,跑道邊的積水映著雲縫裡透出來的橘光。我在斐濟蘇瓦(Suva)待了六天,原本只打算去大家都推薦的市場、公園、教堂晃晃,結果每天都被在地朋友帶去地圖上找不到名字的角落。這篇不是要告訴你蘇瓦有什麼「必去景點」,而是想記下那些連背包客棧公佈欄都沒寫過的小路——那些讓我覺得自己不是遊客,而是暫時被收留的鄰居的瞬間。

清晨五點半,努布卡洛溪的心跳聲

清晨五點半,天色還是那種濕潤的深藍,我從Domain區的民宿走出來,主人安娜(Ana)前一晚特別交代:「市場的心臟在後面,不在前面。」蘇瓦市立市場(Suva Municipal Market)正面朝著羅德威爾路(Rodwell Road),觀光客白天看到的是整齊的蔬果攤,但真正的戲在清晨的努布卡洛溪(Nubukalou Creek)後巷上演。我沿著烏舍街(Usher Street)走到溪邊,柴油引擎的味道混著鹹水氣息撲面而來,十幾艘漆著鮮豔藍紅色的小漁船正一艘接一艘靠岸,漁夫赤腳跳下船,把裝著鮪魚、紅鯛和整簍章魚的塑膠籃扛上岸,籃子擦過石階發出刮擦聲,魚鱗在還沒被陽光照到的空氣裡泛著淡淡的銀光。沒有一個攤位有招牌,交易靠吼的——「五塊!五塊一條!」批發商和餐廳採購員蹲在地上摸魚鰓判斷新鮮度,動作快得像在打拍子。我站在溪邊的小橋上待了將近一小時,沒有人多看我一眼,直到一位賣章魚的婦人瑪雷(Mele)遞給我一個煮熟的小章魚腳,笑著說「試試看,免費」。那股帶著海水鹹味與淡淡椰奶香的滋味,是我對蘇瓦的第一個味覺記憶。

真正的蘇瓦不在導覽手冊的粗體字裡,而在清晨六點漁夫喊價的聲音裡。

沿雷瓦河而上:觀光地圖遺忘的納西萊陶村

第二天我決定去一個安娜的表哥吉米(Jimi)提到的地方——納西萊村(Nasilai Village),雷瓦河三角洲(Rewa Delta)裡少數還維持傳統製陶技藝的村落。上午八點我先從蘇瓦中央巴士站搭乘開往瑙索里(Nausori)方向的地方巴士,車程約四十五分鐘,票價一塊二斐濟幣(約新台幣18元),車廂裡放著雷鬼混編的斐濟流行樂,窗外景色從市區水泥樓房漸漸換成甘蔗田與紅樹林。抵達瑙索里巴士總站後,我照著吉米畫的手繪地圖走到河邊碼頭,找到一位叫希米(Simi)的船夫,他開著一艘漆皮剝落的長尾小艇,單程載我沿雷瓦河(Rewa River)往下游走大約二十五分鐘,來回喊價二十斐濟幣(約新台幣310元),沒有救生衣,只有一件被太陽曬到褪色的橘色背心塞在船頭,我硬是自己穿上。河面很平靜,偶爾有漁網浮標點綴,兩岸紅樹林密得幾乎看不見天空,希米一路指著岸邊說哪棵樹是「dakua」,樹脂是村民燒陶後上光防水的祕方。船在正午前抵達納西萊,岸邊沒有任何指標或紀念品攤,只有幾間傳統木屋和曬著陶坯的木架。村裡的長者米莉安(Miriam)蹲坐在自家屋簷下,手上正搓著一團灰褐色黏土,她說這土是退潮時從河床挖出來的,混入細沙增加韌性,完全不用轆轤,靠手掌一圈圈盤築塑形,再用貝殼壓出紋路。她邀我坐下看她把陶坯放進用椰殼堆成的露天窯火中,火苗竄起時的劈啪聲和煙燻味,和我在博物館玻璃櫃前看到的展示品完全是兩個世界。臨走前我用五塊斐濟幣買下一個掌心大小的陶罐,米莉安用斐濟語笑著說了句「vinaka」(謝謝),那語氣裡有種不需要翻譯也懂的溫暖。

回程的船在下午一點多出發,途中遇上一陣午後雷陣雨,雨滴打在河面像撒了一把碎銀,希米把船速放慢,兩人縮在僅有的塑膠布下等雨勢變小,雨聲、引擎聲、遠處村落的狗吠聲混在一起,那十分鐘的狼狽,反而成了整趟旅程裡我記得最清楚的畫面之一。

市場三樓的午餐:一碗庫克達換來的鄉愁

回到蘇瓦已是下午兩點半,飢腸轆轆的我直奔市立市場三樓的熟食區,這裡沒有英文菜單,牆上貼的是手寫斐濟語與印地語混雜的價目表。我學著隔壁桌的老先生用手指比劃,點了一份庫克達(kokoda)——用萊姆汁醃到半透明的生魚肉,拌入椰奶、洋蔥丁和小辣椒,盛在半個椰殼裡,價格三塊五斐濟幣(約新台幣55元)。第一口酸香衝進鼻腔,接著椰奶的滑順把酸味包裹住,魚肉的彈牙口感讓人一口接一口停不下來。旁邊還加點了一份都魯卡(duruka,一種斐濟蘆筍)椰汁咖哩,纖維帶著微苦的清香,和椰奶的甜膩形成奇妙的平衡。用餐區其實只是幾張塑膠桌椅擠在窗邊,窗外正對努布卡洛溪,風扇轉得吱嘎作響,鄰桌的印裔斐濟大叔一邊喝著馬沙拉茶(masala tea)一邊跟我聊起他每週三次來這裡吃午餐,「這裡比城裡任何一家餐廳都便宜,而且是我阿嬤那個年代就有的味道」。吃完後我又繞去卡明街(Cumming Street)口一間沒有招牌、只掛著印度教神像貼紙的小店,老闆娘遞給我一份現烤的洛提(roti)包咖哩馬鈴薯,一塊五斐濟幣,外皮微焦帶油香,咬下去內餡濕潤辛香,那種家常的隨性感,是我在遊客聚集的旅館早餐吧永遠吃不到的。

蘇瓦的美味不在餐廳評分網站上,而在市場三樓那台吱嘎作響的老風扇底下。

星期天的鐘聲與格羅格:走進蘇瓦人家

隔天正好是星期天,吉米邀我一起去他家附近的世紀衛理公會教堂(Centenary Methodist Church),就在斯圖爾特街(Stewart Street)上,白色木造建築在陽光下亮得刺眼。上午十點,信眾魚貫而入,男士穿著素色蘇魯裙(sulu),女士包著頭巾,整場禮拜以斐濟語進行,唱詩班沒有樂器伴奏,純靠人聲堆疊出四部和聲,那種厚實的共鳴讓我起了雞皮疙瘩,比我在任何音樂廳聽過的合唱都更震動胸口。禮拜結束後,吉米沒有直接帶我回市區,而是拐進納瑟斯區(Nasese)一戶平房,說要帶我體驗真正的「格羅格」(grog,當地人對亞克那kava的暱稱)聚會。屋內地上鋪著蓆子(ibe),十幾個男人盤腿圍坐成圈,中間放著一個木盆,裡面是灰褐色、帶著泥土與胡椒香氣的亞克那汁液。主持人先用椰殼碗(bilo)舀起一碗遞給我,吉米低聲提醒我:「接碗前先拍三下手掌(cobo),喝完之後再拍一下,說『bula』。」我照做,一口喝下,舌尖先是麻,接著喉頭泛起一股土味的苦,現場的人齊聲拍手叫好。接下來的兩個小時,碗一輪接一輪傳,話題從橄欖球賽聊到隔壁村的婚禮,沒有人急著說話,也沒有人急著離開,時間感在這裡完全鬆開。吉米的姑姑端出一鍋用石窯悶烤的洛沃(lovo)——豬肉、雞肉和樹薯裹著香蕉葉悶了三小時,肉質軟到用手一撕就開,煙燻味滲進每一絲纖維裡。那天下午我才明白,格羅格聚會不是觀光表演,而是蘇瓦人真正處理人際關係與消息流通的場域,外人若沒有本地朋友引薦,幾乎不可能被邀請進門。

黃昏:勞塔卡灣海堤上的無名觀景點

離開蘇瓦前的最後一個傍晚,吉米帶我繞到南太平洋大學(University of the South Pacific)後方,勞塔卡灣(Laucala Bay)一段沒有名字的海堤。地圖上這裡什麼都不是,只是本地學生下課後會來坐著發呆的地方。傍晚六點左右,夕陽把整片海染成橘紅,幾個赤腳男孩在及膝的海水裡撒著手網捕小魚,網子甩開的瞬間在逆光裡像一朵綻放的傘。海堤水泥還帶著白天曬過的餘溫,坐下去屁股底下暖烘烘的,海風帶著鹹味和不遠處烤玉米攤飄來的焦香。吉米指著遠方雲霧繚繞的山頭說那是「約斯克拇指峰」(Joske's Thumb),是本地人才會去爬的健行路線,入口藏在納卡里村(Naqali)後方的甘蔗田小徑裡,沒有官方步道標示,得靠村裡的孩子帶路。我沒有時間再去爬那座山,但光是坐在這片海堤上看完整個日落,聽著海浪拍打消波塊的規律聲響,以及遠處教堂晚禱的鐘聲隱約傳來,就已經讓我明白,蘇瓦真正迷人的地方,從來不是那些被寫進手冊裡的坐標,而是本地人願意把你順路帶去的、那些連名字都懶得取的角落。

回程的飛機上,我把米莉安給我的小陶罐用毛衣包好塞進背包最底層,想著它會不會在轉機時碎掉。後來它安然無恙地回到台灣,現在放在我書桌上裝迴紋針。每次看到它,我就會想起雷瓦河上那場突如其來的雷陣雨,以及吉米說過的一句話:「蘇瓦不會在你的行程表上,它只會在你放慢腳步之後,自己找上你。」

ENGLISH

The plane touched down at Nausori International Airport just after the rain had stopped, puddles along the runway catching a thin orange light breaking through the clouds. I spent six days in Suva, Fiji's capital, originally planning to visit the markets, parks and churches every guidebook mentions. Instead, local friends kept pulling me off the map, day after day, into corners that don't have names on any tourist app. This isn't a list of must-see attractions. It's a record of the back lanes that never make it onto a hostel noticeboard—the moments that made me feel less like a visitor passing through and more like a neighbor being temporarily taken in.

5:30 AM: The Pulse of Nubukalou Creek

At five-thirty in the morning the sky still holds that damp, deep-blue color that hasn't decided whether it's night or day. I left my homestay in the Domain area after my host Ana told me the night before, 'the heart of the market isn't in the front, it's around the back.' Suva Municipal Market faces Rodwell Road with its tidy produce stalls that tourists see by daylight, but the real theater happens at dawn along Nubukalou Creek behind it. I walked down Usher Street toward the water, diesel fumes mixing with the smell of brine, as more than a dozen brightly painted fishing boats came in one after another. Barefoot fishermen leapt onto the dock, hauling crates of tuna, red snapper and octopus up the stone steps, the plastic baskets scraping against concrete, fish scales catching a faint silver glint in light that hadn't reached full brightness yet. None of the stalls had signs; deals were shouted across the crowd—'Five dollars! Five dollars each!'—while wholesale buyers crouched down to check gill color with practiced speed, like keeping time to a rhythm only they could hear. I stood on the little footbridge over the creek for nearly an hour, invisible to everyone, until a woman named Mele selling octopus handed me a boiled tentacle and said, smiling, 'try it, on the house.' The taste—brine cut with a faint sweetness of coconut—became my very first flavor memory of this city.

The real Suva doesn't live in the bold print of a guidebook. It lives in the sound of fishermen shouting prices at six in the morning.

Up the Rewa River: Nasilai, the Pottery Village Maps Forgot

On my second day I set out for a place Ana's cousin Jimi had mentioned—Nasilai Village, one of the few settlements left in the Rewa Delta still practicing traditional Fijian pottery. At eight in the morning I caught a local bus from Suva's central bus stand heading toward Nausori, a forty-five minute ride costing about 1.20 Fijian dollars, reggae-inflected Fijian pop playing over the speakers as concrete buildings gave way to sugarcane fields and mangroves. From Nausori's bus station I followed Jimi's hand-drawn map down to the river jetty and found a boatman named Simi, running a long-tail skiff with peeling paint. He charged twenty Fijian dollars round trip for a twenty-five minute ride downriver—no life jackets except one faded orange vest wedged at the bow, which I insisted on wearing myself. The river was calm, dotted occasionally with fishing net buoys, mangroves pressing so close on both banks the sky nearly disappeared. Simi pointed out the dakua trees along the way, whose resin villagers use to seal and waterproof finished pots. We reached Nasilai just before noon—no signposts, no souvenir stalls, just a few wooden houses and racks of drying clay vessels. An elder named Miriam sat under her eaves, rolling a lump of gray-brown clay dug from the riverbed at low tide and mixed with sand for strength. She builds entirely by coil, no wheel, shaping each piece by hand and pressing shell patterns into the surface. She let me watch her load finished pieces into an open fire built from coconut husks, the crackle and smoke utterly different from anything I'd seen behind museum glass. Before leaving I bought a palm-sized pot for five Fijian dollars; Miriam said 'vinaka'—thank you—in a tone that needed no translation at all.

We left around 1:30 PM and got caught in a sudden afternoon downpour halfway back, raindrops hammering the river's surface like scattered silver coins. Simi slowed the engine and we huddled under the only tarp we had, rain, engine noise and a distant village dog's barking blending together—ten uncomfortable, unforgettable minutes that turned out to be one of the clearest memories of the whole trip.

Lunch on the Market's Top Floor: A Bowl of Kokoda and a Taste of Nostalgia

Back in Suva by half past two, starving, I went straight to the food stalls on the third floor of the Municipal Market, where menus are hand-written in a mix of Fijian and Hindi with no English in sight. Following the lead of the elderly man at the next table, I pointed and ordered kokoda—raw fish cured in lime until nearly translucent, tossed with coconut milk, diced onion and chili, served in half a coconut shell for 3.50 Fijian dollars. The first bite hit with bright acidity, then the coconut milk rolled in to soften it, the fish springy and fresh enough to keep me reaching for more. I added a side of duruka, Fijian asparagus, simmered in coconut curry, its faintly bitter fiber balancing the richness beautifully. The dining area was nothing more than a few plastic tables by a window overlooking Nubukalou Creek, a ceiling fan creaking overhead. An Indo-Fijian man at the next table, sipping masala tea, told me he comes here three times a week: 'cheaper than anywhere in town, and it tastes like my grandmother's cooking.' Afterward I wandered to an unmarked stall near Cumming Street, its only decoration a sticker of a Hindu deity, where the owner handed me a freshly griddled roti stuffed with curried potato for 1.50 Fijian dollars—slightly charred, faintly oily, the filling warm and fragrant inside. That kind of unpretentious, everyday deliciousness is something no hotel breakfast buffet could ever replicate.

Suva's best meals aren't rated on any app—they're served under a creaking old fan on the market's third floor.

Sunday Bells and Grog: Stepping Into a Suva Household

The next day happened to be Sunday, and Jimi invited me to Centenary Methodist Church on Stewart Street, its white timber facade glaring bright under the morning sun. At ten o'clock the congregation filed in, men in plain sulu, women with headscarves, the entire service conducted in Fijian. The choir sang without instruments, stacking four-part harmonies purely by voice, a resonance so full it raised goosebumps on my arms—more moving than anything I'd heard in a concert hall. After the service, instead of heading back into town, Jimi took me into a house in Nasese for what he called a proper 'grog' session, the local nickname for kava. Woven mats covered the floor, a dozen men sitting cross-legged in a circle around a wooden bowl filled with a grayish, peppery-smelling liquid. The host ladled a bowlful into a coconut-shell cup and handed it to me; Jimi whispered instructions—clap three times before taking the cup, once after finishing, and say 'bula.' I did as told, the first sip numbing my tongue before a earthy bitterness settled in the back of my throat, met by a round of approving claps from the circle. Over the next two hours the bowl kept circulating, conversation drifting from rugby matches to a neighboring village's upcoming wedding, nobody rushing to speak or to leave—time loosened completely in that room. Jimi's aunt brought out a lovo she'd cooked in an earth oven—pork, chicken and cassava wrapped in banana leaf and slow-roasted for three hours, meat so tender it pulled apart with bare hands, smoke worked deep into every fiber. That afternoon I understood that a grog session isn't a performance for outsiders; it's how Suva residents actually manage relationships and share news, and without a local friend's introduction, an outsider has almost no chance of being invited in.

Dusk: The Unnamed Seawall at Laucala Bay

On my last evening in Suva, Jimi took me behind the University of the South Pacific to a stretch of seawall at Laucala Bay that has no name on any map—just a spot where local students come to sit and stare at nothing after class. Around six, the sunset turned the whole bay orange-red as barefoot boys cast hand nets in knee-deep water, the nets blooming open against the light like opening umbrellas. The concrete of the seawall still held the day's warmth against my legs, sea breeze carrying salt and the char of a nearby corn-roasting stall. Jimi pointed to a fog-wrapped peak in the distance and called it Joske's Thumb, a hike locals take through a trailhead hidden behind sugarcane fields in Naqali village, with no official markers—you need a village kid to show you the way. I didn't have time to climb it, but sitting on that seawall through the whole sunset, listening to waves hitting the breakwater in steady rhythm and the faint toll of evening church bells somewhere behind us, I finally understood what makes Suva compelling: never the coordinates printed in a guidebook, but the nameless corners locals are willing to take you to simply because it's on their way home.

On the flight home I wrapped Miriam's small clay pot in a sweater and buried it at the bottom of my bag, worrying it might crack during the layover. It made it back to Taiwan in one piece and now sits on my desk holding paperclips. Every time I see it, I remember that sudden downpour on the Rewa River, and something Jimi said to me: 'Suva was never going to be on your itinerary. It only finds you once you slow down.'
