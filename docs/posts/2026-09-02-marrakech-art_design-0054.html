中文

回到台北的第三個晚上,我還在想那面牆的顏色——不是紅色,也不是橘色,是介於兩者之間、被摩洛哥人稱作「terre de Marrakech」的赭土色,隨著一天裡光線的角度不停變換。我是為了寫一篇關於建築與設計的稿子去的,結果帶回來的,是滿手機的牆面特寫、瓷磚紋樣,還有一位染坊老闆教我認靛藍的記憶。這篇筆記,想從美學這個切角,重新走一次那七天。

清晨五點半,紅城還沒醒

我住的里亞德(riad)在麥地那(Medina)靠近巴德廣場的一條無名巷子裡,方向感在抵達的第一晚就徹底報銷——巷弄窄到兩人並肩要側身,牆面全是同一種土色,沒有招牌,只有偶爾露出的一扇鑲著幾何雕花的木門洩漏這裡面可能是誰的家、誰的旅館。第一天清晨五點半,我被宣禮塔的喚拜聲吵醒,索性不睡了,披了件外套走出去。

那個時間點的德吉瑪廣場(Jemaa el-Fna)幾乎是空的,前一晚喧鬧到凌晨的耍蛇人、賣蝸牛湯的攤位、圍成一圈聽故事的人群全都散了,只剩下清潔隊的水管沖刷石板地,水痕在晨光裡反光。廣場邊的庫圖比亞清真寺(Koutoubia Mosque)宣禮塔還籠罩在一層淡紫色的天光裡,那種赭紅色的石造尖塔,比例上瘦而挺,塔身四面雕出馬蹄形拱窗與交錯的幾何飾帶,是十二世紀阿爾摩哈德王朝(Almohad)留下的原型,後來整個馬格里布地區的清真寺塔樓幾乎都在模仿它的比例。我站在廣場邊緣,看著這座塔從灰藍色慢慢被染成粉橘色,才第一次真正理解「紅城」這個稱號不是行銷詞,而是每天清晨都會重演一次的自然事件。

馬拉喀什的美,從來不在單一的地標裡,而在光線落在赭土牆上的那三十分鐘。

那天早上我沒有立刻回去補眠,反而沿著廣場往南邊的小巷晃,看見麵包店老闆娘正把一盤盤生麵團送去公共烤爐——馬拉喀什舊城裡很多人家沒有自己的烤箱,家家戶戶把整形好的麵團送到巷口的柴燒烤爐,付幾個迪拉姆請師傅代烤,再自己拿回家當早餐,這個畫面本身就是一種活的城市設計:公共與私人生活邊界的模糊,恰好也是里亞德建築內外翻轉邏輯的縮影。

巴伊亞宮:摩爾幾何的朝聖路線

上午九點半,我從住的里亞德走去巴伊亞宮(Bahia Palace),距離不到兩公里,但穿過麥地那的窄巷得靠手機導航加問路,實際走了大約二十五分鐘,中途兩次被拉車的騾子逼到牆邊讓路。宮殿入口在Rue Riad Zitoun el Jdid上,靠近費爾布蘭提廣場(Place des Ferblantiers),外觀低調到我差點錯過,只有一道不起眼的木門,直到踏進第一進庭院才被那種「豁然開朗」的空間感震住。

門票是七十迪拉姆(約新台幣二百二十元),開放時間是早上九點到下午四點四十五分,我特意選開門後半小時進去,避開遊覽車團客的尖峰時段,大概只花了七十迪拉姆和一個半小時,就走完整座十九世紀晚期由大維齊爾巴阿邁德(Ba Ahmed)擴建的宮殿群。整體參觀動線是自由開放式的,沒有強制路線,我大概照著人流走,先經過幾間側室,再進到中庭。中庭地面鋪著整片彩色澤利吉瓷磚(zellige),幾何星形圖案以八角星為核心向外無限延展,牆面下半段同樣是瓷磚,上半段轉為手工雕刻的灰泥(gebs)與彩繪雪松木天花板,顏色以靛藍、赭紅與孔雀綠為主,是典型十九世紀摩洛哥宮廷裝飾的配色邏輯——瓷磚代表永恆與數學秩序,灰泥雕花代表植物紋樣的自然生機,兩者刻意並置。

最讓我駐足的是後宮部分一間採光極好的房間,四面牆的瓷磚拼貼在正午陽光斜射進來時會產生一種視覺震動感,盯著看幾秒鐘,眼睛會自動把平面圖案「拉」出立體錯覺,這正是伊斯蘭幾何裝飾藝術刻意追求的效果:透過數學上無限延展的圖樣,暗示神聖秩序超越肉眼可見的邊界。我在那間房裡站了快十分鐘,直到後面排隊的參觀者開始有點不耐煩才挪開。庭院角落養著幾隻慵懶的貓,窩在瓷磚台階上曬太陽,成了無數遊客鏡頭裡的固定班底,我後來才知道當地人普遍認為貓是先知穆罕默德喜愛的動物,舊城裡到處可見居民擺食物餵養牠們,這種對貓的寬容也算是這座城市待人接物的隱性註腳。提醒帶現金,園區內不收信用卡;正午前後陽光直射庭院會很曬,建議帶帽子;整體不需要導遊也能看懂大概脈絡,但如果想聽懂雕花背後的典故,門口有官方導遊可以現場議價,行情約一百五十迪拉姆一小時。

馬約爾花園與YSL美術館:一抹藍色的設計革命

下午我搭了一趟大約三十迪拉姆的小黃計程車,從麥地那出來到新城區古利兹(Gueliz),去看馬約爾花園(Jardin Majorelle)。這座花園原是法國畫家雅克・馬約爾(Jacques Majorelle)在一九二〇到三〇年代親手打造的私人花園兼畫室,他調配出一種飽和到近乎螢光的鈷藍色,後世直接以他的名字命名為「馬約爾藍」(Majorelle Blue),整座畫室建築、花盆與步道欄杆都漆成這個顏色,與園中大片仙人掌、竹林和九重葛的綠紫對比,視覺衝擊力強到我一走進園區大門就忍不住停下腳步拍照,而我平常其實很抗拒觀光客式的打卡拍照。

這座花園在一九八〇年代被時裝設計師伊夫聖羅蘭(Yves Saint Laurent)與他的伴侶皮耶・貝爾傑(Pierre Bergé)買下並修復,兩人晚年常駐馬拉喀什,聖羅蘭過世後骨灰的一部分依照遺願灑在園中的一處玫瑰花壇。二〇一七年,園區旁新開了伊夫聖羅蘭美術館(Musée Yves Saint Laurent Marrakech),建築由法國建築事務所Studio KO操刀,外牆用赭紅色與米白色的陶土磚拼接出類似編織紋理的立面,刻意呼應摩洛哥傳統紡織圖案,同時整棟建築沒有一扇對外開窗,完全靠中庭天井引光,是我這趟旅程裡看到對「內向型建築」詮釋得最現代、也最節制的一個案例——傳統里亞德的內庭邏輯,被搬進了一座當代美術館裡。

聖羅蘭花了三十年時間收藏摩洛哥的色彩,而這座城市把那份收藏,原封不動地還給了世界。

館內常設展陳列聖羅蘭生涯裡受北非啟發的設計手稿與服裝,其中一件一九六七年的「摩洛哥系列」長袍,直接把澤利吉瓷磚的幾何拼貼轉譯成刺繡圖樣,站在展櫃前我才意識到,整趟旅程裡我在宮殿牆面、地毯市集、染坊布匹上反覆看到的圖形邏輯,原來早就透過時裝,悄悄流進了全世界的伸展台。花園門票加美術館聯票約為兩百多迪拉姆,建議下午三點後入園,人潮會比中午稀疏許多,光線也更柔和,那種藍色在斜射的暖光下會轉出一種近乎絲絨的質感,和正午刺眼的飽和藍完全是兩種心情。

德吉瑪廣場入夜:氣味與蝸牛湯的劇場

傍晚六點半,天還沒全黑,德吉瑪廣場已經開始搭起一整排流動攤位,發電機的柴油味混著炭烤羊肉的煙,還有現榨柳橙汁攤位堆成小山的橙子香氣,三種味道在空氣裡打架,這是我在世界任何一個廣場都沒聞過的組合。我照著背包客論壇上流傳的建議,找到編號十四號攤位附近一個賣蝸牛湯(babouche)的小攤,老闆用長柄勺從一鍋深褐色的湯裡舀出一整碗連殼帶肉的蝸牛,湯底加了大茴香、肉桂和一點薄荷,滾燙上桌,一碗大概五到八迪拉姆,老闆遞給我一根牙籤挑肉,湯喝起來帶點藥膳感的甘甜,肉質彈牙帶點土腥,是那種第一口猶豫、第二口就上癮的滋味。

吃完湯我沿著攤位往深處走,找到一攤專做塔吉鍋(tagine)的老店,老闆是位六十歲上下的伯伯,鍋蓋掀開瞬間蒸氣裹著孜然與番紅花的香氣撲面而來,我點了一份杏桃燉羊肉塔吉,肉燉到用叉子一碰就散開,杏桃的甜味滲進肉汁裡,配著一旁烤到微焦的圓麵包(khobz)撕來沾湯汁吃,一整份加一壺薄荷茶大約六十迪拉姆,摺疊塑膠桌椅擺在廣場正中央,抬頭就能看到說書人(halqa)身邊圍了一圈本地觀眾,還有格納瓦(Gnawa)樂手敲著鐵製響板,節奏又急又密。廣場上完全沒有座位隔間,陌生人肩並肩坐在同一張長桌上吃飯,隔壁一位摩洛哥大叔看我對著蝸牛湯露出猶豫的表情,笑著用簡單英文說了句「first time scary, second time you miss it」,後來果然如他所說,回台北後我最想念的味道,正是那碗其貌不揚的蝸牛湯。

染坊巷弄裡的靛藍手:哈桑教我的待客之道

隔天上午我特地繞去染坊市集(Souk des Teinturiers),巷弄比其他市集更窄,頭頂拉著繩索晾著一片片剛染好的羊毛與布料,靛藍、番紅花黃、石榴紅層層疊疊掛在頭上遮出一整片天篷,踩過的石板地被染料浸得斑駁。我在一處染缸邊停下腳步,一位染坊師傅哈桑主動用法語混英語跟我攀談,見我對他手上洗不掉的深藍色感興趣,索性把我拉到染缸邊,示範怎麼把一綹羊毛浸進靛藍染液,拉出來瞬間是青綠色,接觸空氣氧化幾十秒後才慢慢轉成深藍,他說這門手藝是跟他父親學的,家裡三代都在同一條巷子染布,「顏色會騙人,要等它自己決定變成什麼」,這句話他說得雲淡風輕,我卻記到現在。

示範結束後,哈桑沒有直接推銷商品,反而先轉身進屋端出一壺薄荷茶,倒茶時特意把壺舉得老高,茶水拉出一道細長的弧線注入小玻璃杯,他解釋這樣做是為了讓茶湯表面起泡、也讓茶更快降溫方便入口,摩洛哥人待客的禮數裡,茶要斟三巡,第一巡淡如生命、第二巡濃如愛情、第三巡苦如死亡,客人若只喝一杯就起身告辭會被視為失禮。那天正好是週五,他提到週五中午全家人會聚在一起吃庫斯庫斯(couscous),這是齋戒月之外全年不變的家庭儀式,店鋪多半週五中午前後會提早打烊讓員工回家吃飯,我後來確實在好幾條巷子撞見拉下一半的鐵捲門,才明白這不是店家偷懶,而是刻意留出的家庭時間。臨走前哈桑堅持不肯多收我買的一小塊靛藍染布的錢,只收了原本談好的四十迪拉姆,還說著「你是我的客人,不是我的顧客」,這句話後來我在其他地毯店、香料鋪都聽過類似版本,才意識到這是整座城市待客邏輯的核心:先建立關係,交易只是順帶發生的事。

離開前,在露台看夕陽落下宣禮塔

離開馬拉喀什前的最後一個傍晚,我特地繞去古利茲區一間由舊殖民時期銀行改建的當代藝廊Comptoir des Mines Galerie,裝飾藝術風格的挑高大廳裡展著摩洛哥當代畫家的作品,和城郊那間馬阿登非洲當代藝術博物館(MACAAL)遙相呼應——後者選在高爾夫球場旁的新開發區,建築低矮簡潔,收藏的是撒哈拉以南非洲當代藝術家的作品,兩間機構加起來,拼出這座城市除了古蹟之外,正在悄悄成形的當代藝術版圖。天色轉暗前,我爬上一間老里亞德改建的露台咖啡館,點了一杯薄荷茶坐下,庫圖比亞清真寺的宣禮塔又一次被染成粉橘色,和清晨那次剛好首尾呼應,喚拜聲從四面八方的擴音器同時響起,交疊出一種略帶失真、卻異常動人的和聲。

飛機起飛前,我在筆記本上寫下一句話:馬拉喀什教會我的,不是某一棟建築或某一件展品,而是這座城市把數學、色彩與人情世故縫在同一塊布料裡的方式。回台北後,我書桌前貼著那塊哈桑染的靛藍布,每次抬頭看到,就想起那句「顏色會騙人,要等它自己決定變成什麼」——也許旅行本身也是這樣。

ENGLISH

Three nights after landing back in Taipei, I was still thinking about the color of that wall — not quite red, not quite orange, but the ochre the locals call 'terre de Marrakech', a hue that seemed to shift with every degree the sun moved. I'd gone to Marrakech to write about architecture and design, and came home with a phone full of close-ups of tile patterns and plaster carvings, and the memory of a dye-master teaching me how indigo changes its mind. This is that trip, retraced through the lens of aesthetics.

5:30 AM, Before the Red City Wakes

My riad sat on a nameless alley in the medina near Bab Doukkala, and my sense of direction had collapsed by the first night — the lanes are narrow enough that two people must turn sideways to pass, every wall the same clay color, no signage, just the occasional carved wooden door hinting that someone's home, or guesthouse, lay behind it. On the first morning I was woken at 5:30 by the call to prayer and gave up on sleep, pulling on a jacket and stepping outside.

At that hour Jemaa el-Fna was nearly empty. The snake charmers, snail-soup carts, and storytelling circles that had packed the square until past midnight were gone, replaced by cleaning crews hosing down the flagstones, water catching the early light. The minaret of the Koutoubia Mosque still stood wrapped in pale violet dusk — a slender, precisely proportioned ochre tower, its four faces carved with horseshoe arches and interlacing geometric bands, a twelfth-century Almohad prototype that nearly every minaret across the Maghreb has echoed since. I watched it shift from grey-blue to salmon pink over half an hour and understood, for the first time, that 'the Red City' isn't a marketing phrase — it's a daily event that repeats itself at dawn.

Marrakech's beauty was never in a single landmark — it lived in those thirty minutes when the light first touched the ochre walls.

I didn't go back to sleep. I wandered south of the square instead and found a bakery owner carrying trays of raw dough to the communal wood-fired oven down the lane — many homes in the medina have no oven of their own, so families shape their bread and send it to be baked for a few dirhams, then carry it home for breakfast. That single scene felt like a piece of living urban design: the blurred line between private and public life, mirrored in the inverted logic of riad architecture itself.

Bahia Palace: A Morning Pilgrimage Through Moorish Geometry

At 9:30 I walked from my riad to Bahia Palace, less than two kilometers away, though navigating the medina's alleys took roughly twenty-five minutes of phone-map guessing and asking directions, and twice I flattened myself against a wall to let a mule cart pass. The entrance sits on Rue Riad Zitoun el Jdid, near Place des Ferblantiers, so understated I nearly walked past it — just a plain wooden door, until the first courtyard opened up and the sense of scale hit me all at once.

Admission was 70 dirhams (roughly US$7), open from 9am to 4:45pm. I timed my visit for half an hour after opening to dodge the tour-bus crowds, and spent about ninety minutes wandering the palace complex built in the late nineteenth century by Grand Vizier Ba Ahmed. There's no fixed route — I simply followed the flow of foot traffic through a series of side chambers into the main courtyard, its floor entirely paved in hand-cut zellige tilework, eight-pointed stars radiating outward in an endlessly extending geometric field. The lower walls carry more tile; above them, hand-carved plaster (gebs) gives way to painted cedar ceilings in indigo, ochre red, and peacock green — the classic palette of nineteenth-century Moroccan court decoration, where tile represents mathematical, eternal order and plaster carving represents the organic vitality of plant motifs, deliberately placed side by side.

What stopped me longest was a sunlit chamber in the former harem quarters, where midday light raked across the tiled walls and produced a strange optical vibration — stare for a few seconds and the flat pattern seems to lift into three dimensions. That's precisely the effect Islamic geometric ornament is built to achieve: an infinitely extending pattern hinting that divine order exceeds what the eye alone can register. I stood there nearly ten minutes until the queue behind me grew audibly impatient. Cats dozed on the tiled steps in every corner, part of the permanent cast in everyone's photographs — I later learned locals widely believe cats were favored by the Prophet Muhammad, and residents across the medina routinely leave out food for them, a small but telling footnote on this city's broader hospitality. Bring cash — no cards accepted inside — and a hat, since the courtyards get punishingly bright by noon. You don't need a guide to follow the general narrative, but if you want the stories behind the carvings, official guides wait at the entrance, negotiable around 150 dirhams an hour.

Jardin Majorelle and the YSL Museum: A Shade of Blue That Rewrote a City's Design Vocabulary

That afternoon I took a small yellow petit taxi for about 30 dirhams from the medina out to the new town, Gueliz, to see Jardin Majorelle. French painter Jacques Majorelle built this as his private garden and studio in the 1920s and 30s, mixing a saturated, almost fluorescent cobalt blue that later generations simply named 'Majorelle Blue.' The studio, plant pots, and railings are all painted in it, set against dense stands of cacti, bamboo, and bougainvillea — a visual jolt strong enough that I stopped in my tracks at the entrance gate, despite my usual resistance to obviously photogenic tourist spots.

In the 1980s, fashion designer Yves Saint Laurent and his partner Pierre Bergé bought and restored the garden, spending long stretches of their later years in Marrakech; after Saint Laurent's death, part of his ashes were scattered, per his wishes, in a rose bed within the garden. In 2017 the Musée Yves Saint Laurent Marrakech opened next door, designed by the French firm Studio KO, its façade built from interlocking terracotta and cream brick arranged to evoke woven textile patterns, with no exterior windows at all — light enters entirely through an internal courtyard. It's the most rigorous, contemporary interpretation of 'introverted architecture' I saw on the whole trip: the interior-facing logic of the traditional riad, reborn inside a museum.

Saint Laurent spent thirty years collecting the colors of Morocco, and this city simply handed that collection back to the world, unedited.

The permanent collection traces Saint Laurent's North African-inspired sketches and garments; one 1967 'Moroccan Collection' caftan directly translates the geometric tile-mosaics into embroidery. Standing in front of the display case, I realized that the same visual logic I'd been chasing across palace walls, carpet souks, and dye-stained fabrics had already quietly slipped, decades ago, onto runways worldwide. The combined garden-and-museum ticket runs a little over 200 dirhams; I'd recommend entering after 3pm, when crowds thin considerably and the light turns softer — that blue takes on an almost velvet quality in the late slanting sun, an entirely different mood from its glare at noon.

Jemaa el-Fna After Dark: A Theatre of Smoke and Snail Soup

By 6:30pm, before the sky had fully darkened, Jemaa el-Fna was already filling with rows of pop-up stalls — diesel fumes from the generators mixing with charcoal smoke from grilling lamb and the citrus perfume of fresh-squeezed orange juice piled into small mountains at every juice cart. Three smells fighting in the air at once, a combination I hadn't encountered in any other square in the world. Following a tip from a backpacker forum, I found a small stall near booth 14 selling snail soup, babouche — the vendor ladled a bowlful of shell-on snails from a deep brown broth spiced with anise, cinnamon, and a touch of mint, served scalding hot with a toothpick to pick out the meat. A bowl cost around 5–8 dirhams; the broth tasted faintly medicinal and sweet, the meat chewy with a mineral edge — the kind of dish you hesitate over on the first spoonful and crave by the second.

After the soup I kept walking deeper into the stalls and found an old-timer running a tagine stand — a man in his sixties who lifted the conical lid with a flourish, releasing a cloud of steam scented with cumin and saffron. I ordered a lamb-and-apricot tagine; the meat fell apart at the touch of a fork, the apricots' sweetness bleeding into the sauce, which I mopped up with a slightly charred round of khobz bread. The whole meal, plus a pot of mint tea, ran about 60 dirhams. Folding plastic tables sat right in the middle of the square, and I could look up mid-bite to see a storyteller (halqa) drawing a circle of locals, and Gnawa musicians clattering iron castanets in tight, urgent rhythms. There are no dividers between tables — strangers sit shoulder to shoulder — and the Moroccan man beside me, noticing my hesitation over the snail soup, laughed and said in simple English, 'First time scary, second time you miss it.' He was right: back in Taipei, that unassuming bowl of soup is the flavor I miss the most.

Indigo Hands in the Dyers' Souk: What Hassan Taught Me About Hospitality

The next morning I made a point of detouring through Souk des Teinturiers, the dyers' quarter, its lanes even narrower than the rest of the market, ropes strung overhead hung with freshly dyed wool and cloth — indigo, saffron yellow, pomegranate red — layered into a canopy that blocked out the sky, the flagstones underfoot stained in irregular patches from decades of runoff. I stopped by one dye vat where a craftsman named Hassan struck up a conversation in a mix of French and English, noticing my curiosity about the permanent blue staining his hands. He waved me over and dipped a hank of wool into the indigo vat — pulled out, it was a shocking green, only turning deep blue after thirty seconds of contact with the air. He learned the trade from his father; three generations of his family have dyed cloth on the same lane. 'Color lies,' he told me, almost offhand. 'You have to wait for it to decide what it wants to become.' I've thought about that line more than almost anything else from the trip.

After the demonstration, Hassan didn't push a sale — he disappeared inside and came back with a pot of mint tea, pouring it from a theatrical height so the stream frothed the surface and cooled faster for drinking. He explained the custom of pouring three rounds: the first as gentle as life, the second as strong as love, the third as bitter as death — a guest who leaves after just one cup is considered impolite. It happened to be a Friday, and he mentioned that families gather at noon for couscous, a ritual observed every week of the year, not only during Ramadan; many shops half-lower their metal shutters around midday on Fridays so staff can go home to eat, something I'd mistaken for laziness in other alleys until then. Before I left, Hassan refused to charge me more than the 40 dirhams we'd agreed for a small square of indigo cloth, saying, 'You are my guest, not my customer' — a phrase I'd hear, in slightly different words, from carpet sellers and spice merchants across the medina, until I understood it as the real operating logic of this city: relationship first, transaction incidental.

One Last Evening: Watching the Minaret Fade From a Rooftop Terrace

On my final evening I detoured into Gueliz to see Comptoir des Mines Galerie, a contemporary art space housed in a converted Art Deco-era bank, its high colonial ceilings now hung with works by contemporary Moroccan painters — a fitting counterpart to MACAAL, the Museum of African Contemporary Art Al Maaden, tucked beside a golf-resort development on the city's edge, its low modernist buildings holding a serious collection of contemporary art from across sub-Saharan Africa. Together, the two institutions sketch a quieter, newer art map layered on top of all the historic monuments. As the light began to fade, I climbed to a rooftop café inside a converted old riad and ordered mint tea. The Koutoubia's minaret turned salmon pink one more time, mirroring that first dawn, the calls to prayer rising from loudspeakers across the city and overlapping into a slightly distorted, oddly moving chorus.

Before my flight, I wrote one line in my notebook: what Marrakech taught me wasn't any single building or object, but the way this city stitches mathematics, color, and human decency into the same piece of cloth. Back home, that square of indigo fabric from Hassan now hangs above my desk, and every time I look up at it I remember his words — color lies, you have to wait for it to decide what it wants to become. Maybe travel works the same way.
