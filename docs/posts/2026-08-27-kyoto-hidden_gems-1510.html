中文

飛機起飛前,我在關西機場的免稅店前站了很久。行李箱裡塞著兩罐山椒粉、一件洗到發舊的浴衣,還有怎麼洗都洗不掉、滲進毛衣纖維裡的檜木與線香味。這是我第三次來京都,前兩次都跟著旅遊書跑清水寺、伏見稻荷千本鳥居、嵐山竹林,拍了跟全世界觀光客一模一樣的照片,回家後翻相簿卻想不起自己到底站在哪裡。這一次,我把地圖收進背包深處,改成跟著京都朋友口中「這個真的不會有人知道」的路線出發——換來的,是六天裡最安靜、也最貼近真實京都的時光。

清晨五點半的貴船——比觀光巴士早一步醒來

鬧鐘設在五點十分,我摸黑走出出町柳附近的民宿,街上只有送報的摩托車聲。叡山電車出町柳站的第一班車大約五點五十分發車,搭到貴船口只要二十七分鐘、車資四百一十円,車廂裡除了我只有兩位看起來像是上山修行的老人家。貴船口下車後沒有早班公車,我沿著貴船川往上游徒步約三十分鐘,河水聲從左手邊一路陪著我,霧氣還沒散,路燈昏黃,除了偶爾一隻鷺鷥飛過,整條路只有我一個人。

六點五十分抵達貴船神社本宮時,連賣水占卜籤的小屋都還沒開燈,我索性先坐在石階上等,聽著鹿威し(竹筒引水的咚一聲)規律地敲著寂靜。七點半社務所開了,我花兩百円買了一張「水占い」籤紙,浸到御神水裡才會顯字,籤紙上寫的是「中吉,足元に気をつけて進め」——後來證明這句話真的說中了我接下來的行程。

在貴船的晨霧裡,我才明白京都真正的安靜,不是沒有聲音,而是那些聲音都還沒被觀光巴士的喧嘩蓋過。

從貴船神社奧宮旁的登山口進入,就是連接鞍馬寺的山道,入口處立了一塊牌子提醒山道會在下午四點半關閉,我在早上八點出發完全沒問題。這段山道叫「木の根道」,盤根錯節的杉樹根像血管一樣爬滿地面,前一晚下過雨,土路濕滑,我慶幸自己穿了防滑健行鞋而不是前一天逛街的帆布鞋。全程大約一個半小時,途中會經過鞍馬寺境內需要繳交三百円環境維護費的入口,山中幾乎沒有訊號,只遇到一位揹著大背包、看起來像每天固定巡山的郵差,他對我點頭說了聲「おはようさんどす」,那是我第一次聽到真正的京都腔問候。九點左右抵達鞍馬寺本殿金堂,回頭望去整座京都盆地還籠罩在一層薄霧裡。下山可以搭鋼索纜車(單程二百円)或再走二十分鐘到仁王門,我選擇用走的,順便讓膝蓋喘口氣,再從鞍馬站搭叡電回出町柳,單程四百二十円。整趟貴船鞍馬半日行程,交通加門票大約花了一千五百円,時間抓四個半小時最剛好,建議一定要在中午前完成穿越,山中無廁所也無便利商店。

糺之森與一顆還溫熱的豆餅

回到出町柳已近十一點,雙腿有點痠,我沒有直接回旅館,而是拐進車站旁的下鴨神社參道——這片名為「糺の森」的原生林,樹齡動輒三、四百年,是京都市區裡少數保留平安時代地貌的森林。走進去的瞬間氣溫像是降了兩三度,陽光被葉隙切成細碎的光斑灑在石板路上,一位巫女正拿著竹掃把清掃參道,掃帚劃過碎石的沙沙聲幾乎是森林裡唯一的雜音。沒有旅行團的擴音器,沒有自拍棒,我第一次覺得自己不是在「觀光」,而是在「路過別人真實的生活場景」。

肚子餓得咕嚕作響,我想起京都朋友再三交代:「出町ふたば的豆餅一定要吃,但別在假日排隊,平日近中午人潮反而會退一波。」出町ふたば就在出町柳站步行三分鐘處,招牌不大,木造店面低調得像雜貨店。我到的時候是平日十一點半,排隊人數大概十五人,等了二十分鐘左右,買到一顆「名代豆餅」,兩百一十円。豆餅外皮是帶點鹹味的赤豌豆麻糬,咬下去先是紅豆顆粒的顆粒感,接著才是溫熱綿密的紅豆餡,鹹與甜在舌尖拉扯的那幾秒鐘,竟讓我站在店門口笑了出來——這絕對是我吃過最不甜膩的和菓子。我捧著豆餅走到鴨川三角洲,那裡有一排烏龜與千鳥造型的過河踏石,幾個放學早的小學生正一格一格跳過河,一隻柴犬追著飛盤,我坐在石階上邊吃邊曬太陽,那一刻突然覺得,京都人的星期二下午,大概就是這樣度過的。

哲學之道深處的貓與鼠——大豊神社的午後閒晃

下午一點多,我沿著鴨川往北走了約四十分鐘,轉進哲學之道。這條路一到銀閣寺那端就會塞滿觀光團,但我特意從南端的若王子橋開始走,沿路只有寫生的老先生和遛狗的主婦,水渠裡錦鯉緩緩游動,水聲比人聲還大。走到中段一個不起眼的巷口,我拐進去找到了大豊神社——這座神社的鎮守不是常見的狛犬,而是一對石雕老鼠「狛鼠」,傳說是因為大國主神被野火圍困時受老鼠指引逃生而得名,神社另一側還供奉著猴子與老鷹造型的守護獸。境內小得幾步就能走完,我遇見一位正在掃落葉的阿嬤,她見我盯著石鼠看得出神,主動用帶著京都腔的口吻笑說:「珍しいやろ、これ。」(這個很少見吧。)

我們聊了將近半小時。她叫田中,住在附近町內超過五十年,提到每年八月二十三、二十四號,她們那條巷子會辦「地蔵盆」——每個街角供奉的小小地藏菩薩會被重新換上圍兜、掛上紙燈籠,孩子們在封街的巷子裡玩套圈圈、抽籤,大人們煮素麵給小朋友吃,是專屬於孩子的社區祭典,外地遊客幾乎不會知道。她也半開玩笑提醒我,京都人講話拐彎抹角是出了名的,如果哪天有人客氣地問你「ぶぶ漬けでもどうどす?」(要不要吃碗茶泡飯?),那其實是委婉地暗示你該回家了——這種被稱為「いけず」的說話藝術,是外地人住再久也未必參得透的京都潛規則。

比起明信片上金碧輝煌的寺院,那些藏在巷口、只在夏天亮起紙燈籠的小地藏,才是京都人真正日夜相伴的信仰。

先斗町裏巷,酒亭裡的京都夜

傍晚六點半,我依約在四条大橋跟京都朋友由紀會合。先斗町主道那條掛滿紅燈籠、擠滿舉著相機的觀光客的窄巷,她看都沒看一眼,直接領我拐進一條寬度大概只容一人通過的「抜け道」(穿越巷)。巷底藏著一間只有八個吧台座位的小酒亭,木門上連招牌都沒有,由紀說熟客都叫它「弥七」。老闆是位話不多、專心烤東西的中年男子,黑板上手寫著今日限定菜色,沒有印刷菜單。

我們點了賀茂茄子田楽味噌(八百円)、時令的鱧魚落とし配梅肉醬(當令價,約一千二百円)、出汁巻き玉子(七百五十円),再加一杯冰鎮的「玉乃光」純米大吟釀(九百円)。賀茂茄子肉質綿密,表面烤到微焦,甜味噌帶著炭香在嘴裡化開;鱧魚是初夏才有的季節味道,肉質彈牙細緻,沾一點酸梅醬瞬間解膩;出汁巻き玉子切開時還冒著熱氣,蛋汁裡吸飽了柴魚高湯,一口咬下幾乎是液態的鮮甜。隔壁兩位穿著和服的熟客用京都腔閒聊,窗外隱約傳來三味線練習的聲音——由紀說附近有花街的置屋,傍晚常聽得到藝妓練習的樂音。兩人份加酒總共大約六千円,比起主巷子裡標示英文菜單、價位翻倍的居酒屋,這裡便宜、安靜,而且完全沒有人舉起手機拍照。

船岡溫泉——泡進大正時代的京都日常

晚上九點多,酒足飯飽的我們散步到今宮神社附近的船岡溫泉。這是一間被登錄為有形文化財的錢湯,建於大正時代,玄關上方的欄間木雕刻著老鷹與唐獅子,磁磚拼貼的浴池牆面帶著昭和初期的復古花色。入場費四百九十円,租條毛巾再加五十円。更衣室裡瀰漫著檜木與洗髮精混合的氣味,幾位阿伯裸著上身用京都腔閒聊今天的天氣與棒球賽,完全不理會我這個外國臉孔——這種被自然融入日常的感覺,比任何觀光導覽都讓我安心。

浴池裡有一區「電気風呂」,通了微弱電流,踩進去的瞬間全身像被輕輕電了一下,旁邊一位老先生看我一臉驚訝,笑著用手比劃教我該站的位置才不會太刺激。露天風呂很小,只種了一棵楓樹,蒸氣升騰間偶爾能看到幾顆星星——在京都市中心,這已經算難得的夜空。走出錢湯時已近十一點,身體暖烘烘的,腦袋卻異常清醒。我站在今宮神社門口的參道上回頭望,巷弄裡幾扇窗還亮著燈,某戶人家傳出電視棒球轉播的聲音,那一刻我才真正確定:這趟旅程,我終於摸到了京都日常生活的邊角,而不只是它精心打扮好、給觀光客看的那張臉。

ENGLISH

Before my flight took off from Kansai Airport, I stood in front of the duty-free shop for a long time. My suitcase held two tins of sansho pepper, a yukata worn soft from washing, and a smell of hinoki wood and incense that had somehow soaked into the fibers of my sweater and refused to leave. This was my third trip to Kyoto. The first two times I followed the guidebooks obediently—Kiyomizu-dera, the vermillion tunnels of Fushimi Inari, the bamboo grove in Arashiyama—and came home with photographs that looked exactly like everyone else's, unable to remember where exactly I had been standing. This time I buried the map at the bottom of my backpack and instead followed the routes my Kyoto friends swore 'nobody knows about.' What I got back was the quietest, most honest six days I've ever spent in this city.

5:30 AM in Kibune: Waking Up Before the Tour Buses

My alarm went off at 5:10. I slipped out of my guesthouse near Demachiyanagi into streets empty except for a newspaper delivery scooter. The first Eizan Railway train from Demachiyanagi leaves around 5:50 AM, and the twenty-seven-minute ride to Kibuneguchi costs 410 yen. Only two elderly passengers, who looked like they were heading up for morning training, shared my car. There's no early bus from Kibuneguchi station, so I walked about thirty minutes upstream along the Kibune River, its rushing water my only companion in the mist. Streetlamps glowed dim orange, and aside from one heron gliding low over the water, I didn't see another soul the entire way.

By 6:50 AM I reached the main hall of Kifune Shrine, and even the little hut selling water-divination fortune papers hadn't opened yet, so I sat on the stone steps and listened to the rhythmic clack of a shishi-odoshi bamboo fountain marking the silence. When the shrine office opened at 7:30, I paid 200 yen for a mizuura mikuji—a blank fortune slip that only reveals its text when dipped in the sacred spring water. Mine read: 'Middling luck—watch your footing as you proceed,' which turned out to be uncannily accurate for the rest of the day.

In the fog at Kifune I finally understood what real quiet in Kyoto sounds like—it isn't the absence of noise, it's noise that hasn't yet been drowned out by tour buses.

From a trailhead beside Kifune's inner shrine, a mountain path connects directly to Kurama-dera, and a sign at the entrance warns that the crossing closes at 4:30 PM—no problem starting at 8 AM. This stretch is called Ki-no-ne-michi, the 'root path,' where centuries-old cedar roots snake across the ground like veins. It had rained the night before, so the dirt was slick, and I was grateful I'd worn hiking shoes instead of the canvas sneakers from the day before. The full crossing takes about ninety minutes, passing through a checkpoint on Kurama-dera's grounds where you pay a 300 yen conservation fee. There's no phone signal in the forest. I passed exactly one person—a mail carrier with an enormous backpack who clearly does this hike daily—who nodded and greeted me in unmistakable Kyoto dialect. I reached Kurama-dera's main hall around 9 AM, and looking back, the entire Kyoto basin was still wrapped in a thin blanket of mist. You can descend by cable car (200 yen one way) or walk twenty more minutes to the Niomon gate; I chose to walk, giving my knees a rest, then caught the Eizan line back to Demachiyanagi for 420 yen. The whole Kibune–Kurama loop cost roughly 1,500 yen in transport and fees and took about four and a half hours. My advice: finish the crossing before noon—there are no restrooms or convenience stores anywhere on the mountain.

Tadasu-no-Mori Forest and a Still-Warm Bean Mochi

I got back to Demachiyanagi just before 11 AM, legs aching, but instead of heading straight to my guesthouse I ducked into the approach to Shimogamo Shrine—the primeval forest known as Tadasu-no-Mori, where trees three or four hundred years old still stand as they did in the Heian period. The temperature seemed to drop two or three degrees the moment I stepped under the canopy. Sunlight fractured into scattered coins of light on the stone path, and a shrine maiden swept the gravel with a bamboo broom, its scratching sound nearly the only noise in the entire forest. No tour guide microphones, no selfie sticks—for the first time on this trip I didn't feel like a tourist observing a spectacle, I felt like I was simply passing through someone else's ordinary life.

My stomach was growling, and I remembered what my Kyoto friend had insisted: 'You have to try the mamemochi at Demachi Futaba, but never go on a weekend—go on a weekday around midday when the line thins out.' The shop sits a three-minute walk from Demachiyanagi station, its wooden façade so understated it could pass for a hardware store. I arrived at 11:30 on a weekday and found about fifteen people ahead of me; after a twenty-minute wait I finally held a 'Nadai Mamemochi' in my hands for 210 yen. The outer layer is a salted red-pea mochi, and the first bite delivers the texture of whole beans before giving way to warm, dense sweet red bean paste—the push and pull of salt and sugar on my tongue actually made me laugh out loud standing right there at the counter. It might be the least cloying wagashi I've ever eaten. I carried it to the Kamogawa Delta, where a row of stepping stones shaped like turtles and plovers cross the river. A few schoolkids let out early were hopping stone to stone, a shiba inu chased a frisbee, and I sat on the riverbank eating my mochi in the sun, thinking: this, probably, is exactly how a Kyoto local spends a Tuesday afternoon.

Cats, Mice, and an Afternoon Off the Philosopher's Path

Around 1 PM I walked north along the Kamogawa for about forty minutes and turned onto the Philosopher's Path. The stretch near Ginkaku-ji fills up with tour groups fast, so I deliberately started from the southern end at Nyakuoji Bridge, where I passed only an old man sketching and a housewife walking her dog, the sound of koi stirring the canal louder than any human voice. Partway along, I ducked down an unassuming side alley and found Otoyo Shrine—guarded not by the usual stone lion-dogs but by a pair of carved stone mice. Legend has it the god Okuninushi was once saved from a wildfire by mice who led him to safety, and the shrine's other side altars are watched over by a monkey and a kite. The grounds are tiny, walkable in under five minutes, and there I met an elderly woman sweeping fallen leaves. Catching me staring at the stone mice, she laughed in unmistakable Kyoto dialect: 'Unusual, aren't they?'

We talked for nearly half an hour. She introduced herself as Tanaka and told me she'd lived in the neighborhood for over fifty years. Every August 23rd and 24th, she said, her block holds Jizo-bon—the small stone Jizo statue on the street corner gets dressed in a fresh bib and strung with paper lanterns, the alley closes to traffic, and children play ring-toss and draw lottery tickets while the adults cook somen noodles just for the kids. It's a festival that belongs entirely to children, and one most visitors never even hear about. She also half-joked, warning me about how notoriously indirect Kyoto speech can be: if someone politely offers you 'a bowl of ochazuke,' it's actually a gentle hint that it's time for you to leave. This particular art of oblique speech, known locally as ikezu, is a code even long-term transplants never quite crack.

Compared to the gilded temples on every postcard, it's these small street-corner Jizo statues, lit with paper lanterns only in August, that Kyoto locals actually live alongside day and night.

A Kyoto Night in a Backstreet Sake Bar in Pontocho

At 6:30 that evening I met my friend Yuki at Shijo Bridge. The main lantern-strung lane of Pontocho, packed with tourists holding up cameras, didn't even earn a glance from her—she led me straight into a passage barely wide enough for one person, a nukemichi cutting between buildings. At the end sat a tiny sake bar with only eight counter seats, no sign on the unmarked wooden door. Yuki said regulars just called it Yashichi. The owner, a quiet middle-aged man focused entirely on his grill, kept no printed menu—only a handwritten chalkboard of the day's dishes.

We ordered grilled Kamo eggplant with sweet miso (800 yen), a seasonal dish of hamo pike conger with plum sauce (market price, around 1,200 yen), a dashi-soaked rolled omelet (750 yen), and a chilled glass of Tamanohikari junmai daiginjo (900 yen). The eggplant was silky through and through, its lightly charred surface giving way to caramelized sweet miso that dissolved on contact. The hamo—a delicacy only available in early summer—had a delicate, springy bite that turned refreshingly tart with a dab of plum sauce. The tamagoyaki arrived still steaming, cut open to reveal custard nearly liquid with dashi. Two regulars in kimono beside us chatted in soft Kyoto dialect, and through the open window I could faintly hear someone practicing shamisen—Yuki explained there's a geiko house nearby where apprentices rehearse in the evenings. Dinner for two, drinks included, came to about 6,000 yen—cheaper, quieter, and without a single phone camera in sight, unlike the English-menu izakayas one alley over charging double.

Funaoka Onsen: Soaking in Taisho-Era Everyday Life

Full and content, we walked to Funaoka Onsen near Imamiya Shrine around 9 PM. This is a registered tangible cultural property built in the Taisho era, its entrance carved with hawks and Chinese lions, its bathing hall lined with retro tile patterns from the early Showa period. Admission is 490 yen, with a towel rental for an extra 50. The changing room smelled of hinoki wood mixed with shampoo, and a few elderly men, shirts off, chatted in thick Kyoto dialect about the weather and that night's baseball game—paying absolutely no attention to the foreign face among them, which somehow made me feel more at ease than any tour guide ever could.

One section of the bath is a denki-buro, an 'electric bath' that runs a faint current through the water—stepping in gave me a jolt of surprise, and an old man beside me laughed and gestured to show me exactly where to stand to avoid the stronger current. The outdoor bath is small, shaded by a single maple tree, and through the rising steam I could actually glimpse a few stars—a rare sight for downtown Kyoto. We left just before 11 PM, bodies warm, minds unexpectedly clear. Standing at the entrance to Imamiya Shrine's approach, I looked back and saw a few lit windows down the lane, a television broadcasting a baseball game drifting from someone's house. That was the moment I knew for certain: on this trip, I had finally touched the edges of Kyoto's ordinary daily life, not just the carefully arranged face it shows to visitors.
