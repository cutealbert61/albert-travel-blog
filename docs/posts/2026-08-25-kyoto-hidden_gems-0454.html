中文

清晨四點五十分,鬧鐘在町家民宿的榻榻米房裡響起,我幾乎是摸黑爬起來的。窗外還是靛藍色的天,巷口的自動販賣機發出微弱的嗡嗡聲,是這條小路唯一醒著的東西。這是我第三次來京都,前兩次都在清水寺的人潮裡擠得喘不過氣,在金閣寺前排隊拍照排了四十分鐘。這一次,我把觀光地圖留在旅館,只帶了一本被前任住客留下、寫滿手繪筆記的舊本子——那是京都在地人私下流傳的路線。接下來這三天,我決定用當地人的步伐,重新認識這座城市。

清晨五點半,伏見稻荷「後山」的寂靜千本鳥居

從東福寺附近的民宿走到JR稻荷站只要十二分鐘,清晨五點半的月台上只有我跟一位提著菜籃的歐巴桑。從京都站搭JR奈良線兩站,車程五分鐘,票價一百五十日圓。我五點四十分抵達伏見稻荷大社,朱紅色的大鳥居前空無一人,只有石獅子在晨霧裡沉默地蹲著。穿過那條被拍到爛的千本鳥居隧道時,我才發現——沒有人潮的時候,木頭鳥居本身會發出細微的嘎吱聲,混著遠處竹林裡的鳥叫,像整座山在呼吸。

大部分遊客只走到「奧社奉拝所」就拍照折返,但我沿著手繪筆記指的方向繼續往上爬,經過熊鷹社,再到四つ辻的休息平台。這段山路大概要走五十分鐘,石階濕滑,爬到一半我的登山鞋已經被晨露浸濕,呼吸也開始急促,但四つ辻回望京都盆地的那一刻,晨霧正好散去,整座城市在腳下鋪展開來,連比叡山的輪廓都看得清楚。我在山頂附近一間清晨六點半就開門的小茶屋坐下,老闆娘遞給我一杯溫熱的甘酒,兩百日圓,她說她母親在這裡賣了四十年茶,山下那些遊客根本不知道山上有這種地方。整趟來回花了將近兩個半小時,除了甘酒跟入山前隨喜的三百日圓香油錢,幾乎不花什麼錢,卻是我在京都感覺最接近神域的一刻。

沒有旅行團的擴音器,只有木頭鳥居在風裡發出的嘎吱聲——那一刻我才明白,京都的靈魂從來不在正殿,而在後山。

上午九點,大原鄉間紫蘇田盡頭的寂光院

下山後我搭上七點五十分從京都站發車的京都巴士十七號系統,車程約一小時,票價五百六十日圓,九點左右抵達大原。這裡比嵐山安靜太多,巴士上除了我,只有兩三個看起來像是要去掃墓的當地老人。大原是京都近郊的山村,以紫蘇聞名,八月正是收成的季節。我沒有直接走向遊客較多的三千院,而是先繞去更僻靜的寂光院——這裡曾是平安時代建禮門院出家隱居之處,寺院不大,苔蘚石階濕潤,踩上去有種近乎柔軟的觸感,空氣裡滿是杉木與雨後泥土的氣味。

走出寂光院,我沿著田埂散步,遇見一位在自家院子裡曬紫蘇的婆婆,她叫田中,今年八十二歲,做柴漬已經做了六十年。她看我盯著竹篩裡紫紅色的葉子發呆,笑著遞給我一小片剛醃好的柴漬茄子讓我試吃——酸中帶鹹,紫蘇的香氣在舌尖炸開,茄子的脆度還在,完全不像超市買到的軟爛版本。她說現在村裡年輕人都搬去市區工作,只剩下老人家還守著這門手藝,「京都人自己都很少特地跑來大原買柴漬了,你能吃到,是運氣。」我在她的小攤位上買了一包三百五十日圓的柴漬,後來帶回台灣,成了整趟旅程最捨不得吃完的紀念品。

午後,西陣織屋巷弄與進々堂的一頓午餐

從大原搭巴士回到京都市區約中午十二點半,我特地在出町柳站下車,步行穿過西陣——這裡曾是京都織物工業的心臟地帶,如今許多老町家仍保留著吱嘎作響的木造織機聲。我經過一間開著窗的織屋,老師傅坐在木製投梭織機前,手腳並用地操作,梭子來回的聲音像規律的心跳,他抬頭看我,只是點了點頭,沒有停下手邊的工作——這門西陣織的手藝,現在整個京都不到百人還在做。

走了大約二十分鐘,我來到京都大學北門前的進々堂,這間麵包咖啡館從一九三〇年開業至今,店裡那張由京都木工大師黑田辰秋親手打造的長桌已經有近百年歷史,桌面被無數手肘磨得油亮。我點了招牌的葡萄乾食パン配一杯咖啡歐蕾,一份套餐八百日圓。吐司外皮烤得微焦帶脆,咬下去內裡卻濕潤綿密,葡萄乾的甜味跟麵包本身淡淡的鹹味中和得剛好;咖啡歐蕾則濃而不苦,溫度剛好燙口。隔壁桌是兩個京大學生,壓低聲音討論考古題,木頭地板吸收了大部分的回音,整間店只剩下咖啡杯碰到碟子的清脆聲響。這一餐,我坐了將近一個半小時沒有人來趕我走。

離開前我又忍不住加點了一碗洋蔥湯,五百五十日圓,湯底熬得濃稠帶焦糖甜味,撒了大量現磨黑胡椒——店員說這道湯從創店第一天就在菜單上,配方六十年沒變過。窗外午後陽光斜斜灑進來,照在木桌的刻痕上,那一刻我突然懂了,為什麼京都人形容老店不是用「歷史悠久」,而是用「還沒有改變」。

黃昏時分,吉田山「茂庵」與整座城市的輪廓

傍晚五點,我沿著吉田神社後方一條幾乎沒有路標的碎石小徑往上爬,約十五分鐘的陡坡,途中只遇到一位遛狗的老先生。爬到山頂,一棟大正時期的老木造建築出現在眼前——這裡曾是茶室,如今改成咖啡館,從市區搭車根本到不了,只能靠雙腳。店內光線昏黃,木頭地板每走一步都會發出聲響,老闆是位安靜的中年男子,我點了抹茶白玉紅豆湯,九百日圓,抹茶的苦與紅豆的甜在嘴裡輪流出現,白玉軟糯有嚼勁。窗外,整個京都盆地在夕陽下染成一片橘紅,五重塔的剪影隱約可見,店裡沒有其他遊客,只有風吹過木窗縫隙的聲音。

京都最好的風景,往往不在指標寫著絕景的地方,而在需要多爬十五分鐘、地圖上找不到名字的山頭。

深夜,巷弄裡屬於孩子的地藏盆

晚上八點,我在上京區一條窄巷裡迷路,卻意外撞見了一場地藏盆。巷口搭起紅白布幔,一尊平常安靜地立在轉角的地藏菩薩石像,這晚被換上了新的紅色圍兜,前面擺滿了西瓜、汽水跟糖果。町內會的會長山本先生,一位六十多歲穿著浴衣的男子,看我探頭張望,主動招呼我進去坐。他解釋,地藏盆是京都特有的地方習俗,每年盂蘭盆節前後,各町內會為守護孩子的地藏菩薩舉辦的小型祭典,外地遊客幾乎不會知道,因為它從不出現在觀光手冊上,規模也只限一條巷子、幾十戶人家。

孩子們圍坐成一圈,傳遞著一串比人還高的大念珠,一邊念經一邊轉動念珠祈福,老人們在旁邊搧著扇子閒聊,烤玉米跟炒麵攤的煙飄散在巷弄裡,混著線香的味道。山本先生說,他小時候也是這樣長大的,「這個晚上,巷子就是孩子們的整個世界。」我被邀請吃了一碗刨冰,五十日圓,是附設攤位的價格,淋滿藍色糖漿,冰涼刺激著喉嚨。蟬鳴漸漸被夜晚的蟲聲取代,燈籠一盞盞被點亮,那一刻我覺得,這才是京都真正日常的模樣——不是穿著和服拍照打卡的京都,而是巷子裡幾十戶人家,一起守護一尊小小石像的溫柔。

回程的路上,我沒有再看那本手繪筆記本,因為這幾天走下來的路線,已經變成我自己的記憶地圖。京都觀光局的官網永遠會告訴你楓葉何時最紅、伏見稻荷幾點人最少,但真正讓我念念不忘的,是那杯山頂的甘酒、田中婆婆手裡的柴漬,還有山本先生遞給我的那碗五十日圓刨冰。這座城市把最好的部分,都藏在觀光巴士到不了的地方。

ENGLISH

At ten to five in the morning, the alarm went off in my tatami room at a converted machiya guesthouse, and I got up in the dark almost by instinct. Outside the window the sky was still indigo, and the only thing awake on the narrow street was a vending machine humming faintly at the corner. This was my third trip to Kyoto. The first two times I had been swallowed by crowds at Kiyomizu-dera and stood forty minutes in line just to photograph Kinkaku-ji. This time I left the tourist map at the guesthouse and carried instead a battered notebook, left behind by a previous guest, full of hand-drawn routes passed quietly among locals. Over the next three days I decided to walk this city at a Kyoto resident's pace, not a visitor's.

5:30 a.m. — The Silent Torii Behind Fushimi Inari

It took twelve minutes to walk from the guesthouse near Tofuku-ji to JR Inari Station, and at five-thirty in the morning the platform held only me and an obasan carrying a vegetable basket. Two stops on the JR Nara Line from Kyoto Station, five minutes, one hundred fifty yen. I reached Fushimi Inari Taisha at five-forty, and the vermillion gate stood completely empty, its stone foxes crouching silently in the mist. Walking through the endlessly photographed tunnel of a thousand torii, I noticed something no photo ever captures — without the crowd, the wooden gates themselves creak softly, mixing with birdsong from the bamboo groves beyond, as if the whole mountain were breathing.

Most visitors turn back after reaching Okusha Hohaijo for their photo, but I kept climbing in the direction the notebook indicated, past Kumataka-sha, up to the rest platform at Yotsutsuji. This stretch takes roughly fifty minutes, the stone steps slick with dew that soaked through my hiking shoes halfway up, my breath growing ragged. But the moment I turned back at Yotsutsuji, the morning fog happened to lift, and the entire Kyoto basin unfolded below me, the silhouette of Mount Hiei visible in the distance. Near the summit I sat down at a small teahouse that had opened at six-thirty, where the owner handed me a warm cup of amazake for two hundred yen, telling me her mother had sold tea on this mountain for forty years — the tourists down below, she said, have no idea any of this exists. The whole round trip took nearly two and a half hours and cost almost nothing beyond the amazake and a three-hundred-yen offering at the gate, yet it was the closest I have ever felt to standing inside sacred ground.

There was no tour guide's megaphone, only wooden torii creaking in the wind — and that was when I understood that the soul of Kyoto has never lived in the main hall, but on the mountain behind it.

9:00 a.m. — Shiso Fields and Jakko-in at the Edge of Ohara

After descending the mountain, I caught the 7:50 Kyoto Bus, route 17, from Kyoto Station. The ride took about an hour and cost five hundred sixty yen, arriving in Ohara around nine. It was quieter here than anywhere in Arashiyama — besides me, only two or three elderly locals rode the bus, looking as though they were headed to tend family graves. Ohara is a mountain village on Kyoto's outskirts famous for shiso, and August is harvest season. Instead of heading straight to the more visited Sanzen-in, I first walked toward the quieter Jakko-in, once the retreat of the nun Kenreimon'in during the Heian period. The temple grounds are small, the moss-covered stone steps damp underfoot with an almost soft give, and the air was thick with cedar and wet earth after rain.

Leaving Jakko-in, I wandered along the paddy paths and came across an elderly woman drying shiso leaves in her own courtyard. Her name was Tanaka, eighty-two years old, and she had been making shiba-zuke pickles for sixty years. Seeing me stare at the deep purple leaves in her bamboo sieve, she laughed and handed me a slice of freshly pickled eggplant to try — sour and salty at once, the perfume of shiso bursting on my tongue, the eggplant still firm, nothing like the mushy version sold in supermarkets. She told me most young people in the village had moved to the city for work, leaving only the elderly to keep the craft alive. Even Kyoto locals, she said, rarely make the trip out to Ohara for shiba-zuke anymore — tasting it, she told me, was pure luck. I bought a small bag for three hundred fifty yen, and it became the souvenir I was most reluctant to finish once I got home.

Afternoon — Weaving Alleys of Nishijin and Lunch at Shinshindo

The bus brought me back into the city around twelve-thirty, and I got off deliberately at Demachiyanagi Station to walk through Nishijin, once the beating heart of Kyoto's textile industry, where old machiya townhouses still echo with the clatter of wooden looms. I passed a weaving workshop with its window open, an elderly craftsman seated at a wooden shuttle loom, hands and feet moving in practiced rhythm, the shuttle's back-and-forth sound like a steady heartbeat. He glanced up and gave a small nod without pausing his work — this particular Nishijin-ori technique, I later learned, is now practiced by fewer than a hundred people in all of Kyoto.

About twenty minutes later I arrived at Shinshindo, a bakery café near the north gate of Kyoto University that has operated since 1930. The long wooden table inside, hand-built by the renowned Kyoto woodworker Tatsuzo Kuroda, is nearly a century old, its surface polished to a soft sheen by decades of resting elbows. I ordered the signature raisin bread with a café au lait, a set costing eight hundred yen. The crust was lightly charred and crisp, but the inside was moist and dense, the sweetness of the raisins balanced perfectly against the bread's faint saltiness; the café au lait was rich without bitterness, served at just the right scalding temperature. At the next table, two Kyoto University students murmured over old exam papers, their voices absorbed by the wooden floor, leaving only the occasional clink of cup against saucer. I sat there for nearly an hour and a half, and nobody hurried me along.

Before leaving I couldn't resist ordering a bowl of onion soup for five hundred fifty yen — the broth reduced to a thick, caramel-sweet depth, finished with a heavy dusting of freshly ground black pepper. The staff told me it had been on the menu since opening day, the recipe unchanged for sixty years. Afternoon light slanted through the window onto the table's worn grooves, and it was then I understood why Kyoto locals never describe an old shop as merely historic — the phrase they use instead simply means it has not yet changed.

Dusk — Mo-an on Mount Yoshida and the Whole City in Silhouette

At five in the evening I climbed a gravel path with almost no signage behind Yoshida Shrine, a steep fifteen-minute ascent where I passed only one old man walking his dog. At the summit stood an old Taisho-era wooden building, once a teahouse, now a small café reachable only on foot, with no road for cars. Inside, the light was dim gold, the wooden floor creaking with every step. The quiet middle-aged owner served me matcha with shiratama dumplings and sweet red bean soup for nine hundred yen — the bitterness of the matcha and the sweetness of the red beans taking turns on my tongue, the dumplings soft and chewy. Outside the window, the entire Kyoto basin turned orange under the setting sun, the silhouette of a five-story pagoda barely visible in the distance. There were no other customers, only the sound of wind slipping through the gaps in the wooden window frames.

Kyoto's finest views are rarely at the spots marked with the word 'spectacular' — they wait fifteen extra minutes uphill, on a peak with no name on any map.

Late Night — A Children's Festival Hidden in the Alleys

By eight that night I had gotten lost in a narrow alley in Kamigyo-ku and stumbled, quite by accident, onto a Jizo-bon festival. Red and white cloth banners had been strung across the alley mouth, and a small stone statue of Jizo, the guardian deity of children, usually standing unnoticed on the corner, had been dressed for the occasion in a fresh red bib, offerings of watermelon, soda, and candy piled before it. The head of the neighborhood association, a man in his sixties named Yamamoto, wearing a yukata, noticed me peering in and waved me over to sit. He explained that Jizo-bon is a custom unique to the Kyoto region, held around the time of the old Obon festival each August by individual neighborhood associations to honor the Jizo who protects local children. Outsiders almost never encounter it, he said, because it never appears in any guidebook — its scale is limited to a single alley and a few dozen households.

Children sat in a circle passing around a giant string of prayer beads taller than themselves, chanting as they rotated the beads for good fortune, while elderly residents fanned themselves nearby, chatting idly. Smoke from grilled corn and yakisoba stalls drifted through the alley, mixing with the scent of incense. Yamamoto told me he had grown up exactly this way. On this one night, he said, the alley becomes the entire world for these children. I was offered a bowl of shaved ice for fifty yen, the festival-stall price, drowned in blue syrup that stung pleasantly against my throat. The cicadas' daytime drone slowly gave way to night insects, paper lanterns were lit one by one, and in that moment I felt I had finally found the real, everyday Kyoto — not the Kyoto of kimono rentals and photo backdrops, but dozens of households quietly guarding one small stone statue together.

On the walk back, I never opened that hand-drawn notebook again, because the routes I had walked over those few days had already become my own map of memory. Kyoto's official tourism sites will always tell you exactly when the maple leaves turn reddest or what hour Fushimi Inari empties out, but what I actually carry home is the cup of amazake at the summit, the shiba-zuke in Grandma Tanaka's hands, and the fifty-yen bowl of shaved ice that Yamamoto passed to me in the dark. This city keeps its best parts exactly where the tour buses cannot reach.
