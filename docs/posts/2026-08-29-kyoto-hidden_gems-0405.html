中文

清晨五點四十分,鬧鐘在京都御所旁的老旅館房間裡震動起來時,窗外還是一片墨藍色,連烏鴉都還沒開始叫。我知道自己在跟時間賽跑——賽的不是別人,是嵐山竹林入口那第一輛遊覽車。三年前我第一次來京都,正午時分被人潮推擠在渡月橋上動彈不得,手機裡存的「美照」全是陌生人的後腦勺。這一次出發前,我在旅館的老闆娘那裡纏了半小時,請她畫給我一張只有京都人才會走的路線圖,她一邊倒煎茶一邊笑我「觀光客都想拍照,你倒想走路」。這句話我記到了現在。

我發誓這一次要用另一種方式重新認識這座城市:早於巴士、晚於打卡人群,走進京都人自己會走的巷子與山徑,吃他們真正會排隊的那家店,而不是遊覽車導遊指定的伴手禮店。

清晨六點半,竹林盡頭的祇王寺

嵐山的竹林小徑幾乎是每個人來京都的必經之地,但很少人知道,穿過那片被踩得發亮的石板路,再往深山方向多走十分鐘,爬一小段緩坡,會抵達一座連不少京都人自己都要特地安排時間才會去的小寺——祇王寺。我六點半抵達時,天光才剛從杉木梢滲進來,苔蘚上還掛著露水,空氣裡混著濕土與杉木的氣味,連售票口的阿伯都才剛掀開木門,爐子上的水還沒燒開。整座庭院安靜到我能聽見自己踩在青苔邊緣的呼吸聲,只有一隻鵯鳥在竹梢跳來跳去,偶爾傳來遠處一聲鐘響,不知道是哪座寺廟敲的。我在草庵前的緣側坐了將近二十分鐘,看陽光一寸一寸移過那片著名的「一面苔庭」,那種綠不是鮮豔的綠,是被歲月泡軟了的深墨綠,濕潤到像可以擰出水來。守寺的老先生用帶著京都腔的日語跟我閒聊,說這裡供奉的是平清盛寵妃祇王出家後隱居的故事,「觀光客都往竹林拍照,很少人願意多走這十分鐘」,他笑著說,語氣裡帶著一點點慶幸,彷彿這座寺廟的清靜是他私藏的秘密,而我只是恰好被允許分享。門票三百日圓,我站在門口猶豫要不要多待一會兒,最後決定把接下來的行程整個往後挪,因為這種安靜太難得,不該用時間表去糟蹋。

真正的京都,不在竹林裡被拍爛的那條路上,而在你願意多走的那十分鐘。

由貴船古道走向雲端:鞍馬到貴船的健行路

吃過嵯峨豆腐店森嘉買的一塊冰豆腐當簡單早餐後,我搭上叡山電鐵,從出町柳站出發,車程約三十分鐘、單程票四百二十日圓,終點是鞍馬站。這條路線本身就是京都人假日遠足的日常選擇,車廂裡沒有觀光客的大行李箱,只有揹著登山杖的阿姨和抱著寶特瓶茶的高中生,窗外的景色從市區樓房逐漸換成一片又一片杉木林,車廂裡飄著淡淡的木頭與舊皮革混合的氣味。走出鞍馬站,迎面而來的巨大天狗面具嚇了我一跳,但真正的重頭戲在後面——買了三百日圓的門票走進鞍馬寺,沿著杉林包圍的參道往上爬,坡度比想像中陡,我大約走了四十分鐘才抵達本殿,途中經過的九十九折參道濕滑,雨後尤其要小心腳下裸露的木根;建議穿抓地力好的鞋,登山杖在這裡完全不是裝飾,山道也沒有電扶梯或纜車直達,想省力可以搭乘單程二百日圓的小型鋼索車到中途,但真正的山林氣味只有走路才聞得到。過了本殿之後,真正的健行才開始——翻越鞍馬山到貴船神社的山道全長約兩公里,林相從杉木轉為闊葉林,腳下滿是盤根錯節的樹根與碎石,我全程花了約一小時二十分鐘,途中沒有遇到半個外國遊客,只有兩位揹著相機的京都本地攝影愛好者跟我點頭致意。山路某段幾乎收不到手機訊號,建議提前下載離線地圖,水也要自己準備,山上沒有便利商店,也沒有廁所,務必在鞍馬寺境內先解決。翻過稜線下坡時,遠遠就能聽見貴船川的水聲,那聲音先是若有似無,接著越來越清晰,像是山谷在替我倒數抵達的距離,我加快腳步幾乎是小跑著下山,鞋底沾滿了泥土。

川床上的一碗流水麵

中午十二點半,我終於走出山道,踏進貴船神社下方沿著溪谷搭建的「川床」料理店群。我選了一家看起來最多在地人光顧、招牌有點斑駁的小店,店家把座位直接架在溪水上方半公尺處,水花濺起的涼氣隔著木板都感覺得到,坐下的瞬間我背後全是雞皮疙瘩,不是冷,是那種被山泉包圍的清爽。我點了招牌流水麵,一份一千三百日圓,店員把青竹剖半做成滑道,冰涼的麵條隨著引來的山泉水從上游滑下來,我得眼明手快用筷子撈,撈不到還會被隔壁桌的京都阿姨笑,笑聲混在水聲裡特別響。麵條本身是簡單的細素麵,但因為浸在幾乎零度的山泉水裡,咬下去的瞬間有種近乎清脆的口感,沾上帶著柴魚與淡口醬油香氣的沾醬,鹹鮮之中帶一點回甘,又追加了一份炭烤香魚,魚皮烤得焦脆,魚肉細嫩帶點苦香的內臟味,是夏天限定的滋味。配著溪谷傳來的水聲跟蟬鳴一起吃下肚,那種涼意是從舌尖一路涼到後頸的。旁邊桌的老夫婦跟我聊起來,他們說自己每年夏天都固定從大阪開車上來吃這一頓,「貴船比嵐山涼快五度,這是京都人夏天的秘密」,老先生邊說邊用扇子搧著風,語氣裡有種理所當然的驕傲,兩人份加上飲料,總共花了大約四千日圓,我心裡想著這筆錢花得值得。

祇園小巷與地蔵盆:阿婆教我的京都規矩

傍晚六點,我搭公車回到市區,特意繞進祇園白川一帶巷弄裡,那是觀光客拍完花見小路就會匆匆離開的區域。我在一條窄到只能容納兩人並肩走的小巷裡,撞見一群鄰居正在布置一尊小小的地藏菩薩石像,前面擺著提燈、供果跟一張紅布桌,幾個孩子蹲在旁邊摺紙。一位坐在自家門口摺紙燈籠的阿婆看我一臉好奇,主動跟我解釋這是「地蔵盆」——每年八月下旬,京都各個町內會的居民會在自己巷口的地藏尊前,連辦兩三天的小型祭典,附近的小孩會來念經、玩遊戲、領糖果,是完全屬於社區、幾乎不會出現在旅遊書上的活動。阿婆說她從小在這條巷子長大,地蔵盆是她記憶裡「暑假真正開始的信號」,比任何煙火大會都重要,她小時候最期待的就是能領到一整袋的什錦煎餅。她一邊摺著燈籠一邊跟我說起這條巷子的規矩:晚上十點後不能大聲說話,因為隔壁就是茶屋的女將休息的地方;垃圾要照町內會規定的日子分類拿出去,不然會被鄰居記住很多年;還有,像花見小路那種茶屋林立的巷子,很多店家門口沒有掛招牌菜單,是因為那本來就是「一見さんお断り」——不接待沒有熟客介紹的生客,這不是排外,而是京都人維持一種彼此信任關係的方式。這些瑣碎又具體的日常規範,是我在任何旅遊指南裡都沒讀過的京都——那種被巷弄、被鄰里、被一整年的祭典行事曆綁在一起的生活感。

京都最深的秘境不是某個地點,而是一整套只有住在巷子裡才懂的生活節奏。

夜晚的錢湯:船岡溫泉的收尾

晚上九點,雙腿早已痠得像灌了鉛,我搭計程車前往北區的船岡溫泉——一間有著超過百年歷史、被列為登錄有形文化財的錢湯,木造的唐破風屋頂在夜色裡意外氣派,完全不像一般認知裡的澡堂。推開拉門,老闆娘用氣音跟我說「いらっしゃい」,入浴費用是四百九十日圓,櫃檯旁擺著洗到發白的竹編置物籃,鑲嵌著彩色磁磚的浴池牆面畫著富士山跟老鷹,水溫燙得我倒抽一口氣,隔壁池的阿伯淡定地泡著,跟我說這裡的水質在京都少見,他每週固定來報到三次,「比健身房便宜,還能跟鄰居聊天」,說完自己先笑了。走出浴池時全身泛紅,皮膚上還帶著淡淡的礦物氣味,晚風吹過北區安靜的住宅街,遠處傳來自動販賣機的嗡嗡聲和一兩聲腳踏車鈴響。那一刻我突然明白,這趟旅程真正的紀念品,不是買到的任何東西,而是我終於走進了京都人下班後會去的地方,而不只是他們接待觀光客的舞台。回旅館的路上,我刻意繞了遠路,想讓這種微醺般的滿足感再多留一會兒,巷口的自動販賣機發著微弱的燈光,像是替這一天蓋上一個安靜的句點。

ENGLISH

My alarm buzzed at 5:40am in a small inn beside the old Kyoto Imperial Palace, and outside the window the sky was still ink blue—even the crows hadn't started their racket yet. I was racing against something specific: the first tour bus that pulls up to the Arashiyama bamboo grove. Three years earlier, on my first trip here, I'd been wedged shoulder to shoulder on Togetsukyo Bridge at noon, unable to move, and every 'beautiful' photo on my phone turned out to be the back of a stranger's head. Before setting out this time, I'd cornered the innkeeper for half an hour over green tea, begging her to sketch me a route that only Kyoto locals actually walk. She laughed while pouring the tea: 'Tourists all want photos. You actually want to walk.' That line stuck with me the whole trip.

I made myself a promise: this time I'd get to know the city differently—ahead of the buses, behind the crowds chasing photo spots, into the alleys and mountain paths that belong to the people who actually live here, eating at the shop locals queue for rather than the souvenir stop the tour guide points to.

6:30am, Beyond the Bamboo: Gio-ji Temple

The bamboo path in Arashiyama is practically mandatory on every Kyoto itinerary, but almost nobody realizes that if you keep walking past the stone paving worn shiny by a million footsteps, another ten minutes deeper into the hills, up a gentle slope, you reach a tiny temple that even many Kyoto residents have to deliberately set aside time to visit—Gio-ji. When I arrived at 6:30am, the light was just beginning to filter down through the cedar canopy, dew still clung to the moss, and the air carried a mix of damp earth and cedar bark. The old man at the ticket window had only just slid the wooden door open; the water on his stove hadn't even boiled yet. The whole garden was so silent I could hear my own breath as I stepped along the edge of the moss, with only a brown-eared bulbul hopping through the bamboo overhead and, somewhere far off, a single temple bell tolling once. I sat on the veranda in front of the thatched hermitage for nearly twenty minutes, watching sunlight creep inch by inch across the famous single sheet of moss—not a bright green, but a deep, ink-soaked green softened by centuries, damp enough that it looked like you could wring water out of it. The elderly caretaker chatted with me in a thick Kyoto dialect, telling me the temple honors Gio, a favored concubine of Taira no Kiyomori who retreated here after taking Buddhist vows. 'Tourists all photograph the bamboo,' he said with a small laugh, 'few are willing to walk the extra ten minutes.' There was a note of quiet relief in his voice, as if this stillness were a secret he kept, and I'd merely been allowed a glimpse. The entry fee was three hundred yen. I stood at the gate debating whether to linger longer, and ended up pushing my whole schedule back—silence this rare shouldn't be rushed for the sake of a timetable.

The real Kyoto isn't on the path worn shiny by every camera in the bamboo grove—it's in the extra ten minutes you're willing to walk.

Over the Mountain Pass: Hiking From Kurama to Kibune

After a simple breakfast of chilled tofu bought from Morika, the tofu shop near Saga, I boarded the Eizan Electric Railway from Demachiyanagi Station—about thirty minutes, four hundred twenty yen one way, terminating at Kurama Station. This line is simply what Kyoto locals ride on a day off; there were no oversized tourist suitcases in the carriage, only aunties with hiking poles and high schoolers clutching bottled tea, while the view outside shifted from city buildings to stretch after stretch of cedar forest, the carriage smelling faintly of old wood and worn leather. Stepping off at Kurama Station, I was startled by the enormous tengu mask waiting at the exit, but the real experience came after: a three-hundred-yen entry fee into Kurama-dera, then a steep climb up a cedar-lined approach that took me roughly forty minutes to reach the main hall, the switchback path slick underfoot after rain—sturdy, grippy shoes are essential, and a walking stick here is no mere accessory. There's no escalator or cable car straight up the mountain; a small funicular covers part of the route for two hundred yen one way if you want to save your legs, but the forest smell only reveals itself if you walk it yourself. Past the main hall, the real hike begins—the trail over Mount Kurama to Kibune Shrine runs about two kilometers, the forest shifting from cedar to broadleaf, the ground a tangle of exposed roots and loose stone. It took me about an hour and twenty minutes, and I didn't pass a single foreign tourist the entire way, only two local photography enthusiasts with cameras slung over their shoulders who nodded as we crossed paths. Part of the trail has almost no phone signal, so download offline maps in advance, and carry your own water—there are no convenience stores or restrooms on the mountain, so take care of that at Kurama-dera before you set off. Cresting the ridge and starting downhill, I could hear the Kibune River long before I saw it, the sound faint at first, then sharpening, as if the valley were counting down my arrival. I quickened my pace, nearly jogging the last stretch, mud caking the soles of my shoes.

Noodles on the River: Lunch at the Kawadoko Platforms

By 12:30pm I finally emerged from the trail into the cluster of kawadoko restaurants built directly over the Kibune valley stream. I picked a small, slightly weathered-looking place that seemed to draw the most local regulars, its seating platform suspended barely half a meter above the rushing water—the cool mist off the current reached me even through the wooden boards, and the moment I sat down goosebumps ran up my arms, not from cold but from being wrapped in mountain water air. I ordered the house specialty, nagashi somen, for thirteen hundred yen: the staff had split a length of green bamboo into a flowing chute, and chilled noodles came sliding down on a stream of spring water diverted from upstream. I had to be quick with my chopsticks, and when I missed a batch, the Kyoto auntie at the next table burst out laughing, her laugh blending oddly well with the sound of the water. The noodles themselves were plain thin somen, but chilled in near-freezing spring water they had an almost crisp bite, dipped in a sauce fragrant with bonito and light soy sauce, savory with a faint sweetness trailing behind. I added a grilled ayu river fish, its skin charred crisp, the flesh tender with a faint bitter edge from the innards—a distinctly summer flavor. Eating it all to the sound of cicadas and rushing water, the chill traveled from the tip of my tongue clean down the back of my neck. An elderly couple at the next table struck up conversation; they said they drive up from Osaka every summer for exactly this meal. 'Kibune runs five degrees cooler than Arashiyama—that's Kyoto's summer secret,' the husband said, fanning himself, a note of quiet pride in his voice. The two of them, drinks included, spent about four thousand yen, and I remember thinking every single yen of mine had been well spent too.

Back Alleys of Gion and Jizo-bon: What an Old Woman Taught Me About Kyoto Manners

By six in the evening I'd taken the bus back into the city and deliberately wandered into the back lanes around Gion Shirakawa, the area tourists rush through only long enough to photograph Hanamikoji before moving on. In an alley barely wide enough for two people to walk side by side, I stumbled on a group of neighbors setting up a small stone Jizo statue, with lanterns, offerings, and a red cloth-covered table in front, while a few children crouched nearby folding paper. An old woman sitting in her doorway folding paper lanterns noticed me staring in curiosity and explained, unprompted, that this was Jizo-bon—every late August, residents of each small Kyoto neighborhood association hold a two- or three-day mini festival in front of their local Jizo statue, where nearby children come to chant sutras, play games, and collect sweets. It's entirely a neighborhood affair, something you'll almost never find mentioned in a guidebook. She told me she'd grown up on this very lane, and Jizo-bon was, in her memory, 'the real signal that summer vacation had begun,' more important to her as a child than any fireworks festival—what she looked forward to most was the bag of assorted rice crackers she'd always be given. As she folded lanterns she told me the unwritten rules of the lane: no loud talk after ten at night, because next door is where the okami of a tea house sleeps; garbage must go out only on the days assigned by the neighborhood association, or the neighbors will remember it against you for years. She added that on lanes like Hanamikoji, lined with exclusive tea houses, many shopfronts display no menu or signage at all, because they operate under ichigen-san okotowari—no service for first-time customers without an introduction from a trusted regular. It isn't about exclusion, she said, it's how Kyoto people maintain a web of mutual trust. These small, specific, unglamorous rules were the Kyoto I'd never read about in any guidebook—a life bound tightly to its alleys, its neighbors, and a full calendar of small festivals that never make it onto a tourist map.

Kyoto's deepest hidden gem isn't a location at all—it's an entire rhythm of daily life that only makes sense if you actually live in the alley.

A Night at the Sento: Closing the Day at Funaoka Onsen

By nine at night my legs felt like they'd been filled with lead, so I took a taxi up to Funaoka Onsen in the northern part of the city—a sento with over a hundred years of history, registered as a tangible cultural property, its wooden karahafu roof cutting an unexpectedly grand silhouette against the dark sky, nothing like the plain bathhouse I'd pictured. Sliding the door open, the proprietress greeted me with a soft 'irasshaimase,' the entry fee just four hundred ninety yen. Beside the counter sat a row of bamboo baskets worn pale from decades of use, and the bathing hall walls were tiled with colorful mosaics of Mount Fuji and an eagle. The water was hot enough to make me gasp, while the old man soaking in the next pool over looked completely unbothered, telling me the mineral quality of the water here is rare even within Kyoto—he comes three times a week without fail. 'Cheaper than a gym membership, and I get to chat with the neighbors,' he said, laughing at his own line before I could respond. Stepping out of the bath, my skin flushed red and carrying a faint mineral scent, the night breeze drifted through the quiet residential streets of the northern district, a vending machine humming somewhere in the distance and, once or twice, the ring of a passing bicycle bell. It was in that moment I understood what I was really bringing home from this trip—not anything I'd bought, but the fact that I'd finally walked into the places Kyoto people go after work, rather than only the stages they perform on for visitors. On the walk back to the inn I took the long way round on purpose, wanting to hold onto that mellow, satisfied feeling a little longer, the faint glow of a vending machine at the corner quietly closing the day like a full stop at the end of a long sentence.
