中文

清晨五點半,我被隔壁院子的雞啼和壁虎「嘖、嘖、嘖」的叫聲吵醒。峇里島烏布的空氣裡總是先聞到丁香菸的甜味,再聞到雞蛋花的香氣,才輪到咖啡。我住在Jalan Bisma巷弄深處一間家庭式民宿,老闆Made說,如果我想真正理解「烏布」這兩個字,就該趁天還沒亮,走到康普安橋(Jembatan Campuhan)去看兩條河交會的地方。我半信半疑地套上拖鞋出門,沒想到這一走,竟走進了一段一千兩百年前的傳說裡。

破曉前的康普安:一句咒語似的地名

從民宿走到康普安橋大約十五分鐘,天色還是靛藍色,路上只有賣粥的小攤已經升起爐火。橋下,西沃斯河(Wos Barat)與東沃斯河(Wos Timur)在一片濃霧裡交會,水聲比想像中大,嘩嘩地拍打著長滿蕨類的岩壁。橋邊立著一座小巧的Pura Gunung Lebah寺廟,香灰混著晨露的味道飄過來,一位老婦人正把剛編好的椰葉小盤(canang sari)放在石階上。

Made後來跟我解釋,西元八世紀左右,一位來自爪哇的印度教高僧Rsi Markandeya,率領信眾翻山越嶺來到峇里島,在阿貢火山下的貝沙基(Besakih)埋下象徵五種金屬的聖物,建立了峇里島最重要的母廟。他繼續往南方尋找適合定居與冥想的土地,走到兩條河流交會之處時,發現這裡的溪水與野生草藥特別能治病療傷,於是稱這片土地為「Ubad」——峇里語中「藥」的意思。時間久了,發音逐漸滑向「Ubud」,這座小鎮的名字,原來從一開始就是一帖藥方。

烏布的名字本身就是一句咒語:它從不承諾繁華,只承諾療癒。

站在橋上,我看著霧氣慢慢從河谷裡散去,遠處傳來第一聲寺廟銅鑼響,忽然覺得這座以瑜伽靜修聞名的小鎮,原來從一千兩百年前就已經在做同一件事——讓人把身心放進水聲裡洗一洗。

王宮深處:烏布王族、荷蘭殖民與畫筆的百年交會

沿著大馬路Jalan Raya Ubud往鎮中心走約二十分鐘,上午八點多,烏布皇宮(Puri Saren Agung)剛開門。石雕大門上爬滿青苔,守門的是兩尊被布巾纏繞腰身的石獅,門口那棵老榕樹據說已經有上百年樹齡,樹蔭幾乎遮住整個十字路口。

這座宮殿由十九世紀末的烏布領主Cokorda Putu Kandel所建。當時荷蘭殖民勢力已經逐步蠶食峇里島南部,1906年登巴薩王室選擇「普普坦」(puputan)——集體持刀衝向荷軍槍口、寧死不降的儀式性自殺,震驚整個西方世界。但烏布王室走了另一條路:選擇與荷蘭當局周旋合作,換取王室血脈與傳統文化得以保存。這個決定,意外地為烏布日後的命運埋下伏筆。

1927年,烏布王子Cokorda Gede Agung Sukawati邀請德國畫家Walter Spies定居烏布,後來又邀來荷蘭畫家Rudolf Bonnet。這兩位外來者被峇里島的光線、儀式與雕刻深深著迷,1936年與王室共同創立「Pita Maha」藝術家協會,把原本只служ於寺廟的傳統工藝,轉化成能被世界看見的美術創作。我站在宮殿內庭,聽見側廳傳來甘美朗(gamelan)練習的叮咚聲,忽然明白眼前這座至今仍住著王室後裔、夜夜上演雷貢舞(Legong)的宮殿,正是烏布從「聖地」蛻變成「藝術之都」的起點。1956年設立的普里魯基桑博物館(Museum Puri Lukisan)就在皇宮斜對面,至今仍完整保存那批Pita Maha時期的原作。

沿著溪谷步行:Campuhan Ridge Walk 實際體驗全記錄

第二天早上七點,我在民宿吃完一份印尼綜合飯(nasi campur)——白飯配天貝、水煮蛋、辣炒空心菜和一勺參巴醬,配一杯滴漏峇里咖啡,七點半就出發走Campuhan Ridge Walk。起點就在昨天看到的康普安橋旁,先經過畫家安東尼奧・布蘭可(Antonio Blanco)那棟外觀誇張如城堡的紀念館,接著爬上一段約五十階的水泥階梯,視野瞬間打開。

步道兩側是及腰高的象草(alang-alang),風一吹整片山脊像海浪一樣起伏,左右兩邊分別是Wos河的兩條支流切出的深谷,谷底隱約可見稻田和椰子樹梢。前半段是鋪好的水泥步道,好走;過了中段開始出現泥土路面,雨後會有點濕滑,建議穿有防滑紋的運動鞋,別學我第一天穿夾腳拖差點滑倒。整段步道單程約兩公里,我邊走邊拍照,花了四十分鐘走到終點附近的Karsa Kafe,來回加上休息大概一個半小時。全程不收門票,只有起點附近停機車要付五千印尼盾(約臺幣十元)的清潔費。

快到八點半時,太陽已經完全升起,氣溫瞬間飆高,建議一定要在七點半前出發,九點以後不但曬,還會遇到大批跟團遊客把步道擠得水洩不通。途中遇到一位牽著兩頭牛在山脊上放牧的農夫,笑著跟我打招呼,我用簡單的峇里語說「suksma」(謝謝),他咧嘴笑得更開。走到步道盡頭一間簡陋小攤,花兩萬印尼盾買了一顆現剖的椰子,坐在木椅上一口氣把椰子水喝完,看著山谷發呆了快二十分鐘才捨得起身往回走。

Ibu Oka烤乳豬攤前:排隊、油香與一口酥脆

中午十二點,我特地趕到皇宮旁Jalan Suweta上的Ibu Oka本店。這間烤乳豬(babi guling)老店從一九七零年代開到現在,據說創辦人是現任王妃的祖母,後來因為美國名廚安東尼・波登在節目裡稱讚它是「此生吃過最好吃的豬肉」而聲名大噪。十一點四十五分抵達時,隊伍已經排了十幾個人,空氣裡飄著木炭烤乳豬的油脂香混著薑黃、南薑和香茅的辛香味,聞了讓人肚子咕嚕叫。

我點了一份「特餐」(nasi babi guling spesial),七萬五千印尼盾(約臺幣一百五十元),配一杯甜冰茶(es teh manis)八千印尼盾。餐盤裡有白飯、撕成細絲的滷豬肉、一大片脆得像仙貝一樣的烤豬皮、暗紅色的lawar(用椰絲、香料和些許豬血拌成的沙拉)、一份椰漿蔬菜咖哩,還有一支用香茅梗串起來的沙嗲肉串(sate lilit)。豬皮咬下去先是「喀滋」一聲脆響,接著油脂在嘴裡化開,底下的肉浸透了薑黃和香料,鹹香帶點微辣的lawar解膩得剛剛好。我坐在塑膠矮凳上,跟旁邊一位澳洲背包客共用一張長桌,兩個人吃到最後都舔起了手指,誰也沒說話,只顧著扒飯。

寺廟慶典夜:與Made伯伯的對話,理解Odalan的哲學

傍晚六點多走回Jalan Bisma,還沒到民宿就先聽見甘美朗鑼鼓聲,巷口一戶人家的大門前架起兩根高聳彎曲、掛滿椰葉和稻穗的竹製「Penjor」,幾位穿著金色紗籠(kamen)的婦女頭頂著堆疊成塔狀的水果供品「gebogan」,排隊走向巷底的家族廟。我攔下Made問是什麼日子,他說今晚是他們家廟的「Odalan」——廟宇周年慶。

Made解釋,峇里島採用210天一輪的Pawukon曆法,每一座寺廟,不管是家族小廟、社區廟還是村廟,都依照自己啟用那天的曆法日期,每210天慶祝一次「生日」。因為全島廟宇數以萬計,啟用日期各不相同,所以幾乎天天都有某個角落在辦Odalan——這也是為什麼觀光客常常在路上撞見盛裝遊行卻毫無心理準備。他下午才帶著全家人坐在庭院裡,用椰葉條折出一個個方形小盤canang sari,鋪上白米、花瓣和線香,他說這是每天必做的功課,連小孩子從五六歲就開始學。

在烏布,時間不是用星期計算的,而是用一場又一場的儀式串起來的。

我獲邀進到廟埕外圍旁觀,一位祭司(pemangku)手持聖水,對著排隊的信眾額頭與喉頭黏上幾粒白米(bija),連我這個外來遊客也被輕輕點了一下,米粒帶著涼意黏在眉心。廟埕中央甘美朗樂手敲著銅鑼,孩子們在供品塔之間追逐奔跑,雞蛋花與線香的味道混在一起飄散在夜色裡。我忽然想起清晨在康普安河邊聽到的傳說——這片土地曾經因為藥草而被賦予「Ubad」之名,而眼前這場延續千年的儀式,不正是同一種對土地、對神靈的感謝方式嗎?從Rsi Markandeya到今晚額頭上的那幾粒米,烏布始終沒有停止過療癒與感恩這件事。臨走前,Made笑著說,下次若還想懂峇里島,別只看沙灘和梯田,「要蹲下來看阿嬤怎麼折那片棕櫚葉」。這句話,我一路帶回了機場。

ENGLISH

At half past five in the morning I woke to a rooster crowing next door and the clicking call of a gecko somewhere above my bed. In Ubud, Bali, the air always smells of clove cigarettes and frangipani before it smells of coffee. I was staying in a family-run homestay tucked down a lane off Jalan Bisma, and my host Made told me that if I really wanted to understand the word 'Ubud,' I should walk to the Campuhan bridge before sunrise, to the place where two rivers meet. I laced up my sandals half-doubting him. That short walk turned out to be a walk into a legend twelve hundred years old.

Before Dawn at Campuhan: A Name Like an Incantation

It took about fifteen minutes to reach Campuhan Bridge, the sky still indigo, only a porridge cart stirring its coals. Below the bridge, the Wos Barat and Wos Timur rivers converge in a bank of mist, the water louder than I expected, slapping against fern-covered rock walls. Beside the bridge stands the small Pura Gunung Lebah temple, the smell of incense ash mixing with morning dew, an old woman setting a freshly woven palm-leaf offering, a canang sari, on the stone steps.

Made later explained that around the eighth century, a Hindu high priest named Rsi Markandeya arrived from Java, leading followers over the mountains of Bali. At the foot of Mount Agung he buried sacred objects representing five metals, founding what would become Besakih, the island's most important mother temple. He continued south searching for a place to settle and meditate, and when he reached the point where two rivers met, he found the water and wild herbs there possessed unusual healing power. He named the place 'Ubad' — the Balinese word for medicine. Over centuries the pronunciation softened into 'Ubud.' The town's name, it turns out, has been a prescription from the very beginning.

Ubud's name has always been a kind of spell: it never promised glamour, only healing.

Standing on the bridge, watching the mist lift slowly off the ravine, I heard the first gong sound from a nearby temple and realized this town, now famous for yoga retreats and wellness spas, had been doing exactly the same thing for twelve centuries — inviting people to rinse their minds in the sound of running water.

Inside the Palace Walls: Royalty, Dutch Colonization, and a Century-Long Encounter with Art

Walking along the main road, Jalan Raya Ubud, took another twenty minutes into the town center, where by eight in the morning the Puri Saren Agung palace gates had just opened. Moss climbed over the carved stone entrance, two stone lions wrapped in checkered cloth guarded the doorway, and an ancient banyan tree, said to be over a century old, shaded almost the entire crossroads.

The palace was built in the late nineteenth century by the Ubud lord Cokorda Putu Kandel. At the time, Dutch colonial power was steadily tightening its grip on southern Bali. In 1906, the royal court of Denpasar chose puputan — a ritual mass suicide, marching into Dutch rifles rather than surrendering — an act that shocked the Western world. Ubud's royal family chose a different path: cooperation and negotiation with the colonial administration, a compromise that allowed their bloodline and cultural authority to survive intact. That decision would quietly shape everything that came after.

In 1927, Prince Cokorda Gede Agung Sukawati invited the German painter Walter Spies to settle in Ubud, later joined by the Dutch artist Rudolf Bonnet. Both men fell under the spell of Bali's light, its rituals, its carving traditions, and in 1936 they co-founded the Pita Maha artists' collective with the royal family, transforming craftsmanship once reserved for temples into art recognized on a world stage. Standing in the palace's inner courtyard, I heard gamelan practice tinkling from a side pavilion and understood that this very palace — still home to royal descendants, still staging Legong dance performances nightly — was where Ubud began its transformation from sacred ground into an art capital. The Museum Puri Lukisan, founded in 1956 just across the street, still holds original works from that Pita Maha era.

Walking the Ridge: A Full Account of the Campuhan Ridge Walk

The next morning I set out at seven-thirty after a breakfast of nasi campur — rice with tempeh, a boiled egg, stir-fried water spinach, and a spoonful of sambal — washed down with a cup of drip Balinese coffee. The trailhead sits right by Campuhan Bridge, past the flamboyant, castle-like museum of the painter Antonio Blanco, then up roughly fifty concrete steps before the view suddenly opens up.

Waist-high elephant grass lines both sides of the ridge, rippling like waves in the wind, with two river valleys carved deep on either side, coconut palms and rice paddies just visible at the bottom. The first half of the path is paved concrete, easy going; past the midpoint it turns to dirt, slippery after rain — wear proper trainers, not the flip-flops I nearly slid on. The full one-way trail runs about two kilometers; walking slowly, stopping constantly for photos, it took me forty minutes to reach Karsa Kafe near the far end, roughly ninety minutes round trip including rest stops. There's no entrance fee, only a five-thousand-rupiah parking fee near the start if you arrive by scooter.

By eight-thirty the sun was already fierce — I'd strongly recommend setting out before seven-thirty, since after nine the heat becomes brutal and tour groups pack the trail shoulder to shoulder. Along the way I passed a farmer grazing two cows on the ridge; I greeted him with a clumsy 'suksma' (thank you) and he grinned back wider than I expected. At a small stall near the trail's end I bought a fresh young coconut for twenty thousand rupiah, sat on a wooden bench, drank it in one long pull, and stared at the valley for a good twenty minutes before I could make myself walk back.

Queuing for Suckling Pig at Ibu Oka: Smoke, Fat, and a Perfect Crunch

At noon I made my way to the original Ibu Oka warung on Jalan Suweta, right beside the palace. This babi guling — Balinese roast suckling pig — institution has been running since the 1970s, reportedly founded by the grandmother of Ubud's current royal consort, and shot to global fame after chef Anthony Bourdain called it the best pork he'd ever eaten. By a quarter to noon the queue already stretched more than a dozen people deep, the air thick with charcoal smoke and the smell of turmeric, galangal, and lemongrass, enough to make my stomach growl audibly.

I ordered the special plate, nasi babi guling spesial, for seventy-five thousand rupiah, with a sweet iced tea, es teh manis, for eight thousand more. The plate arrived piled with rice, shredded roast pork, a slab of crackling so crisp it shattered like a wafer, dark red lawar — a mix of shredded coconut, spices, and a touch of pork blood — a coconut-milk vegetable curry, and a skewer of minced meat satay wrapped around a lemongrass stalk, sate lilit. The crackling gave a loud crunch before dissolving into rendered fat; underneath, the meat had soaked up turmeric and spice all the way through, the lawar cutting the richness with its sharp, herbal bite. I sat on a plastic stool sharing a long table with an Australian backpacker; by the end neither of us said a word, too busy licking our fingers clean.

A Temple Anniversary Night: What Made Taught Me About Odalan

Walking back to Jalan Bisma around six in the evening, I heard gamelan drums before I saw anything. At the mouth of a lane, a household had erected two tall, curved bamboo poles called penjor, draped with palm leaves and rice stalks, while several women in gold-threaded sarongs balanced towering fruit offerings, gebogan, on their heads, walking in procession toward the family temple. I stopped Made and asked what was happening. Tonight, he said, was his family temple's Odalan — its anniversary.

Made explained that Bali runs on a 210-day Pawukon calendar, and every temple — whether a small family shrine, a neighborhood temple, or a village temple — celebrates its own 'birthday' every 210 days, counted from the date it was first consecrated. With tens of thousands of temples across the island, each on its own cycle, some corner of Bali is celebrating almost every single day, which is why visitors so often stumble upon a full ceremonial procession with no warning at all. That afternoon, he told me, his whole family had sat in the courtyard weaving canang sari from strips of palm leaf, layering them with rice, flower petals, and incense — a daily task children learn from as young as five or six.

In Ubud, time isn't measured in weeks. It's strung together, ceremony by ceremony.

I was invited to watch from the edge of the temple courtyard. A priest, the pemangku, dipped his fingers in holy water and pressed a few grains of rice onto each worshipper's forehead and throat — even mine, an outsider, received the cool, sticky touch of bija between the eyebrows. In the center of the courtyard, gamelan musicians struck their gongs while children chased each other between the towering fruit offerings, the smell of frangipani and incense drifting through the dark. I thought back to the legend I'd heard at dawn by the Campuhan river — how this land had once been named for its healing herbs — and realized that this centuries-old ritual was simply another form of the same gratitude toward the land and its spirits. From Rsi Markandeya to the grains of rice still damp on my forehead that night, Ubud has never stopped practicing the twin arts of healing and thanks. Before I left, Made smiled and told me that if I really wanted to understand Bali, I shouldn't just look at the beaches and rice terraces — I should crouch down and watch how a grandmother folds a palm leaf. I carried that line with me all the way to the airport.
