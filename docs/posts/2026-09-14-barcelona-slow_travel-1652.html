中文

我在巴塞隆納待了九天,住在格拉西亞(Gràcia)一間頂樓公寓,窗外曬著鄰居的床單,紅色屋瓦一路延伸到看不見的地方。這篇筆記記錄的不是米拉之家門口排隊的清晨,也不是主教座堂前人擠人的正午,而是我在第五天,決定把相機收進背包、徹底放慢腳步過完的那一整天——從清晨麵包店飄出的奶油香,到深夜苦艾酒杯底那顆浸得發皺的橄欖,這是屬於在地人的巴塞隆納,一天足夠,卻也一天不夠。

清晨六點半,格拉西亞還沒醒

六點半,我被鐵捲門拉起的聲音吵醒,那是巷口五金行老闆準時開店的聲音,規律得像鬧鐘。我推開窗,街道還籠罩在藍灰色的光裡,清潔隊的水車緩緩沖洗石板路,水痕反著路燈的光。我沿著維爾第街(Carrer de Verdi)走下樓,轉進特拉維瑟拉街(Travessera de Gràcia),七點整走進 Fornet de la Rosa 麵包店,櫃檯後的師傅一邊揉著新一批麵團,一邊已經開始招呼熟客——一位提著購物網袋的老太太,師傅不用她開口就遞上她慣買的那份。我點了一份奶油可頌和一杯 cortado,總共三點六歐元,站在吧檯邊,學著旁邊西裝男子的樣子,幾口喝完咖啡就走。可頌的外皮在齒間碎裂的聲音,是我對格拉西亞最早的記憶之一。

怎麼玩:天亮前爬上卡梅爾碉堡

大部分的旅遊指南都建議傍晚去卡梅爾碉堡(Bunkers del Carmel)看日落,但我聽當地朋友說那個時段常常擠得像跨年,於是我決定反其道而行。七點四十分從公寓出發,沿著埃斯科里亞爾街(Carrer d'Escorial)一路往上坡走,再接上特拉維瑟拉德達特街(Travessera de Dalt),穿過還沒完全甦醒的卡梅爾(El Carmel)住宅區,最後爬上格洛里亞下坡道(Baixada de la Glòria)的階梯——我後來數了一下,大約一百五十階,兩旁是塗鴉牆與居民自種的都市小菜園,番茄藤攀在鐵絲網上。整趟路走了四十分鐘,八點二十分抵達山頂,除了兩位正在打太極拳的當地老先生,幾乎沒有其他人。眼前是薩格拉達家族大教堂的尖塔從屋海中探出頭,再往遠方是一條淡淡的海平線,晨光還帶著涼意,風有點大,我慶幸出門前在轉角雜貨店花一歐元買了一瓶一點五公升的水。我穿著慢跑鞋,因為碎石地形其實不太好走,尤其接近碉堡邊緣時要特別小心,那裡沒有護欄。我在舊防空炮台的水泥平台上坐了將近四十五分鐘,看著整座城市從灰藍轉成金黃,幾乎忘記時間。下山我刻意選了另一條路,經過穆爾伯格街(Carrer de Mühlberg),再步行到阿方索十世地鐵站(Alfons X)搭乘 L4 線回格拉西亞,來回總共花了兩個半小時,除了水和咖啡,幾乎沒有其他花費——這大概是整趟巴塞隆納行程裡,我花最少錢卻覺得最值得的兩個半小時。

旅行的意義,不是走過多少地方,而是願意在一個地方停下來,學會用當地人的步伐呼吸。

自由市場的日常採買與 La Pubilla 的家常美食

十點左右,我走進自由市場(Mercat de la Llibertat),這座一八八八年由建築師費朗塞斯克・貝倫格爾設計的現代主義鐵構市場,遊客比波格利亞市場(La Boqueria)少上許多,叫賣聲卻更真實。魚販的冰塊味混著柑橘攤傳來的香氣,一位名叫蒙特瑟(Montse)的起司攤老闆娘讓我試吃了三種曼徹格起司(manchego),分別熟成三個月、六個月與一年,最後我買了一塊熟成六個月、約三百克的,六點二歐元,還順手抓了一袋帕德龍青椒(pimientos de Padrón)兩歐元,以及當季新鮮草莓。中午肚子開始咕嚕作響,我沿著小坡走到革命廣場(Plaça de la Revolució de Setembre de 1868)旁的 La Pubilla,這是一間格拉西亞在地人才會排隊的家常餐館,午間套餐(menú del día)二十一歐元,包含前菜、主菜、甜點與一杯酒。我點了烤蔬菜佐鯷魚(escalivada amb anxoves),甜椒與茄子烤到微焦帶點煙燻味,鹹香的鯷魚在舌尖化開;主菜是黑墨魚汁燉飯(arròs negre),鍋底微微焦脆(socarrat),配上一大匙蒜味蛋黃醬(allioli),蒜味濃烈到我後來整個下午都還記得;甜點是加泰隆尼亞燉蛋(crema catalana),湯匙敲下去焦糖脆殼碎裂的聲音,是這頓飯最療癒的瞬間。鄰桌兩位老先生一邊吃飯一邊打骨牌,侍者用夾雜加泰隆尼亞語腔調的西班牙語跟我閒聊,問我是不是台灣人,說他表哥在馬德里開過一間亞洲超市。這頓飯我坐了將近九十分鐘,沒有人催我離開。

格拉西亞教會我的第一件事是:真正的節慶不是給遊客看的表演,而是鄰居之間一年一度的約定。

午後靜默,巷弄甦醒

下午三點到五點,格拉西亞像被按下靜音鍵。店家一間接一間拉下鐵門,貼著「Tancat per vacances」或單純寫「obrim a les 17h」的紙條,連平常喧鬧的太陽廣場(Plaça del Sol)也只剩幾隻貓趴在大理石階梯上曬太陽。我坐在噴水池邊發呆,樹蔭把光切成一格一格灑在地上,唯一的聲音是某戶人家開著的電視,傳出西班牙語新聞主播的聲音,斷斷續續飄下樓。五點半左右,巷弄像是被喚醒,曬在陽台的床單一件件被收進屋內,雜貨店老闆重新拉起鐵捲門,金屬摩擦聲此起彼落。格拉西亞村莊廣場(Plaça de la Vila de Gràcia)前,幾位老太太搬出輕便的折疊椅坐在自家門口,手裡拿著扇子聊天,這是我在別的城市很少見到的畫面——不是刻意營造的觀光風情,而是日復一日的生活節奏。

節慶前夕:與努莉亞在維爾第街掛彩帶,以及夜幕裡的苦艾酒時光

六點半,我沿維爾第街往下走,看見路中央架起了梯子與鷹架,一位名叫努莉亞(Núria)的婦人正踩在梯子上綁著一串用回收寶特瓶剪成魚鱗狀的裝飾,她在這條街住了三十年,是維爾第街節慶委員會(comissió de festes)的成員。她告訴我,每年八月中旬的格拉西亞大節(Festa Major de Gràcia)前,每戶人家會自願出資約二十到三十歐元,加上大家犧牲下班後的時間一起佈置,今年的主題是「海底世界」,那些用寶特瓶裁成的魚鱗要撐過整整一週的活動,包括夜間的火焰奔跑(correfoc)——她特別提醒我,如果晚上要去看,務必穿舊衣服、戴上帽子蓋住頭髮,因為煙火的火星真的會噴到觀眾身上;還有疊人塔(castellers)與巨人偶遊行(gegants),整條街會封街舉辦通宵舞會(verbena)。她遞給我一段膠帶,示意我幫忙固定裝飾,我的手指黏得發亮,聽她說「這不是做給觀光客看的,是鄰居之間每年重新許下的約定」。天色暗下來後,我在附近一間小酒館 Bodega點了一杯黑苦艾酒加蘇打水(vermut negre amb sifó),兩點五歐元,配上一小罐淡菜罐頭四歐元,黃昏的金色光線斜斜打在陽台鐵欄杆上,街角有個街頭吉他手彈著佛朗明哥的旋律。走回公寓的路上已是十點,我這才明白,巴塞隆納最迷人的,從來不是那些必須排隊拍照的地標,而是這種願意慢下來、才能被看見的日常肌理。

回程班機上我翻著手機裡的照片,發現最喜歡的幾張,竟然沒有一張是聖家堂或奎爾公園,而是努莉亞手裡那串魚鱗彩帶,還有那杯泛著琥珀色光的苦艾酒。慢旅行教會我的,或許就是這件事:把行程留白,把時間留給一條巷子、一個市場、一場屬於鄰居的節慶,你反而會帶走更多。

ENGLISH

I spent nine days in Barcelona, staying in a top-floor apartment in Gràcia, where my neighbors' bedsheets flapped on the line outside my window and terracotta rooftops rolled out toward a horizon I couldn't quite see. This isn't a story about queuing outside Casa Milà at dawn or being swallowed by crowds in front of the cathedral at noon. It's about the fifth day, when I zipped my camera into my backpack and let myself slow all the way down — from the smell of butter drifting out of a bakery at sunrise to the wrinkled olive at the bottom of a vermouth glass late at night. This is the Barcelona that belongs to the people who live there. One day was enough to understand it, and nowhere near enough to leave it behind.

6:30 AM: Gràcia Hasn't Woken Up Yet

I woke to the clatter of a metal shutter being cranked open — the hardware store owner on the corner, punctual as an alarm clock. I pushed open the window and the street was still steeped in blue-grey light, a municipal water truck slowly hosing down the cobblestones, the wet trails catching the last of the streetlamps. I walked down Carrer de Verdi and turned onto Travessera de Gràcia, arriving at Fornet de la Rosa bakery right at seven. The baker behind the counter was already kneading a fresh batch of dough while greeting an elderly woman with a shopping net bag — he didn't even need to ask what she wanted. I ordered a butter croissant and a cortado, 3.60 euros total, and stood at the counter the way the man in the suit beside me did, drinking my coffee in a few quick sips before heading out. The sound of the croissant shattering slightly between my teeth is one of my earliest memories of Gràcia.

How to Do It: Climbing Bunkers del Carmel Before the Crowds

Most guidebooks tell you to go to Bunkers del Carmel at sunset, but a local friend warned me it gets as crowded as a countdown party. So I did the opposite. I left my apartment at 7:40 AM, walking uphill along Carrer d'Escorial, then onto Travessera de Dalt, cutting through the still-sleeping residential streets of El Carmel, before climbing the stairs of Baixada de la Glòria — roughly 150 steps by my count, flanked by graffiti walls and residents' urban vegetable plots where tomato vines climbed chain-link fences. The whole walk took forty minutes, and I reached the summit at 8:20 AM to find almost no one there except two elderly men practicing tai chi. Ahead of me, the spires of the Sagrada Família rose out of a sea of rooftops, and beyond that, a faint line of sea on the horizon. The morning air still had a chill to it, the wind picking up, and I was grateful I'd spent one euro at a corner convenience store on a 1.5-liter bottle of water before the climb. I wore trainers because the loose gravel near the summit makes footing tricky, and there's no railing at the edge of the old bunker platform, so it pays to be careful. I sat on the concrete slab of the old anti-aircraft battery for nearly forty-five minutes, watching the city shift from grey-blue to gold, losing all track of time. Coming down, I deliberately chose a different route, passing through Carrer de Mühlberg before catching the L4 metro at Alfons X back to Gràcia. The round trip took two and a half hours and, aside from water and coffee, cost me almost nothing — probably the cheapest two and a half hours of the entire trip, and the one I'd repeat without hesitation.

Travel isn't measured by how many places you've seen, but by how willing you are to stop and breathe at someone else's pace.

Mercat de la Llibertat and Lunch at La Pubilla

Around ten, I wandered into Mercat de la Llibertat, a wrought-iron Modernista market designed by architect Francesc Berenguer in 1888, far less touristed than La Boqueria but every bit as alive. The smell of fish-stall ice mixed with the citrus tang from a fruit vendor nearby, and a cheesemonger named Montse let me sample three ages of manchego — three months, six months, and a year. I bought a 300-gram wedge of the six-month for 6.20 euros, grabbed a bag of Padrón peppers for two euros, and picked up a punnet of strawberries in season. By noon my stomach was growling, so I walked up the slope to La Pubilla, a neighborhood restaurant on Plaça de la Revolució de Setembre de 1868 where locals queue for the 21-euro menú del día — starter, main, dessert, and a glass of wine included. I ordered escalivada amb anxoves, roasted vegetables charred just enough to taste smoky, the salted anchovy melting on my tongue; then arròs negre, black squid-ink rice with a crisp socarrat crust at the bottom of the pan, served with a heavy spoonful of garlicky allioli so pungent I could still taste it hours later; and to finish, crema catalana, the sound of my spoon cracking the caramelized sugar shell the most satisfying moment of the meal. At the next table, two old men played dominoes over their own lunch, and the waiter, speaking Spanish with a distinct Catalan lilt, asked if I was from Taiwan and mentioned a cousin who once ran an Asian grocery in Madrid. I sat there for nearly ninety minutes and nobody once made me feel like I should hurry up and leave.

The first lesson Gràcia taught me: a real festival isn't a performance for tourists — it's a promise neighbors renew every year.

The Quiet Afternoon, and the Alley Waking Up Again

Between three and five in the afternoon, Gràcia goes on mute. Shops pull down their shutters one after another, taping up handwritten notes reading 'Tancat per vacances' or simply 'obrim a les 17h' — we open again at five. Even Plaça del Sol, usually buzzing, was down to a few cats sprawled on the marble steps in the sun. I sat by the fountain doing nothing much, watching sunlight cut through the plane trees into neat little squares on the pavement, the only sound a television leaking out of an open window somewhere above, a Spanish news anchor's voice drifting in and out. By half past five, the alley stirred back to life — laundry pulled in from balconies piece by piece, the grocer cranking his shutter back up, metal scraping against metal from every direction. In front of Plaça de la Vila de Gràcia, a few elderly women dragged out folding chairs to sit right in their own doorways, fanning themselves as they chatted — a scene I rarely see in other cities, not staged for visitors, just the plain rhythm of a life lived the same way, day after day.

The Eve of a Festival: Hanging Ribbons with Núria, and Vermouth at Dusk

At half past six I walked back down Carrer de Verdi and found ladders and scaffolding set up in the middle of the street. A woman named Núria stood on one of them, fastening strings of fish-scale decorations cut from recycled plastic bottles. She's lived on this street for thirty years and sits on the neighborhood's festival committee. She explained that every August, ahead of the Festa Major de Gràcia, households voluntarily chip in twenty or thirty euros each and give up their evenings to help decorate, and this year's theme was 'under the sea.' Those plastic-bottle scales have to survive an entire week of festivities, including the correfoc, the night fire-run — she made a point of warning me that if I planned to watch, I should wear old clothes and cover my hair, because sparks really do land on the crowd. There would also be castellers building human towers and gegants, giant papier-mâché figures, parading through streets closed off for all-night open-air dances called verbenas. She handed me a strip of tape and had me help hold a garland in place; my fingers were sticky by the time she said, 'this isn't done for tourists — it's a promise the neighbors renew with each other every year.' As the sky dimmed, I stopped at a small neighborhood bodega for a glass of vermut negre amb sifó, black vermouth with soda, 2.50 euros, alongside a small tin of mussels for four. The golden evening light slanted across wrought-iron balconies, and somewhere around the corner a street guitarist was working through a flamenco melody. By the time I walked home it was ten at night, and I finally understood that Barcelona's real charm was never in the landmarks you queue to photograph, but in this kind of texture — the kind you only notice once you've slowed down enough to see it.

On the flight home, scrolling through my photos, I realized my favorites weren't of the Sagrada Família or Park Güell at all — they were of the fish-scale garland in Núria's hands, and that amber glass of vermouth catching the last of the light. That might be exactly what slow travel teaches you: leave space in your itinerary, give your time to a single alley, a single market, a festival that belongs to someone else's neighbors — and you'll come home with more than you ever planned to take.
