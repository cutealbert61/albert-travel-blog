中文

飛機降落馬拉喀什的那個傍晚,機場外的計程車司機把行李往後車廂一扔,沿途的圍牆全是同一種顏色——曬乾泥土般的赭紅,在夕陽裡像被點燃一樣發亮。我後來才知道,這個顏色有個名字叫「馬拉喀什紅」,而這座城市規定所有建築外牆都必須維持這個色調。那一刻我還沒意識到,接下來六天,我會一路被這座城市的顏色、線條與手工痕跡牽著走,像上了一堂沒有課本的美學課。入住麥地那深處一間老宅改建的riad,厚重的雪松木門推開時發出低沉的吱呀聲,天井裡的noria水聲滴滴答答,我在時差裡半夢半醒,心裡只有一個念頭:明天一定要趕在遊客大隊之前,先看一次巴迪皇宮的晨光。

破曉巴迪皇宮:一堂日光與泥牆的建築課

清晨六點我走出旅館,麥地那的巷弄還沒睡醒,只有麵包師傅推著木板車經過,烤爐飄出的麥香混著清晨微涼的空氣,貓群蹲在門檻上打盹。巴迪皇宮九點才開門,我刻意提早抵達,八點四十五分排在鐵門外,才第五個人。門票七十迪拉姆(約新台幣兩百三十元),換來的是一整座幾乎沒有屋頂的宮殿廢墟——十六世紀薩阿德王朝的薩阿迪蘇丹阿默德·曼蘇爾為了炫耀從蘇丹得來的黃金,蓋了這座當年號稱「無與倫比」的宮殿,如今只剩下夯土牆(pisé)骨架,和四座下沉式庭園裡的橘子樹。陽光斜斜切進來的角度剛剛好,把泥牆上風化的紋理照得像等高線地圖,牆頭上一群白鸛築巢,偶爾振翅發出乾澀的拍打聲,整座廢墟安靜得只剩下風聲跟鳥叫。我站在中央水池邊,想著這座曾經鑲滿義大利大理石與黃金天花板的宮殿,如今只剩骨架,卻比完整的建築更誠實地告訴我摩洛哥建築的邏輯:牆不是重點,光線灑落的方式才是。

廢墟教會我的事:真正的奢華從來不是黃金,而是光線願意停留多久。

馬約雷花園與貝爾貝爾博物館:當藍色成為信仰

離開巴迪皇宮,我搭了一輛小紅計程車往新城區Gueliz走,車資議價到三十迪拉姆。九點半抵達馬約雷花園門口時,隊伍已經排到街角,現場買票要等將近四十分鐘,我後悔沒有提前線上訂票。花園門票加上裡面的貝爾貝爾博物館合計約兩百迪拉姆。一踏進園子,那種鈷藍色瞬間把我震住——法國畫家雅克·馬約雷1930年代混出的這個藍,後來被命名為「馬約雷藍」,飽和到近乎螢光,卻和竹林、仙人掌的墨綠形成一種奇異的和諧。1980年伊夫·聖羅蘭買下這座花園,把它從荒廢邊緣救回來,園區深處還有一座小小的紀念柱,寫著他的名字。貝爾貝爾博物館裡收藏的柏柏爾人銀飾、皮革包、彩色羊毛披肩,每一件都在訴說撒哈拉邊緣部落如何用有限的染料——番紅花黃、胭脂蟲紅、靛藍——創造出繁複到令人屏息的幾何圖案。我在博物館裡待了將近一小時,盯著一件十九世紀的柏柏爾新娘頭飾看,銀片與琥珀交疊出的重量感,讓我理解摩洛哥的美學從來不是輕盈的,而是層層疊疊、寧可過度也不留白。

怎麼玩:巴伊亞宮與染坊巷弄的迷路實記

中午十二點,我從德吉瑪廣場出發,沿Rue Riad Zitoun el Jdid往南走大約十五分鐘,兩旁全是賣皮拖鞋跟燈具的小店,巷子越走越窄,終於在一群人潮後方看見巴伊亞宮低調的木門。門票七十迪拉姆,現場買票排隊約十分鐘,建議避開下午兩點到四點的旅行團尖峰時段。這座十九世紀大維齊爾巴伊·艾哈邁德為自己的四位妻子與二十四位妾室蓋的宮殿,最讓我駐足的是主庭院的zellige馬賽克磁磚地板,幾何切割的藍白綠拼貼在陽光下幾乎在震動,抬頭一看,雪松木天花板上滿滿是手工雕刻加彩繪的muqarnas鐘乳石紋,師傅告訴我光是一間房的天花板就要耗費工匠團隊四到六個月。我在宮殿裡繞了將近一個半小時,刻意避開主動線,鑽進側邊較安靜的側翼小房間,光影透過雕花木窗灑在磁磚上,幾乎沒有其他遊客。走出宮殿後,我憑著手機地圖往北走了二十分鐘,誤打誤撞繞進了Souk Sidi Abdelaziz染坊巷弄,還沒看到店面先聞到味道——濃烈的氨水混著植物染料的酸味撲面而來,巷子頭頂上晾滿了一整排剛染好的羊毛,靛藍色、番紅花黃、石榴皮的赭紅,像一整條會滴水的彩虹。有個染坊老闆看我盯著看,直接遞了一把濕答答的羊毛要我摸,還笑著說要拍照可以,象徵性給個十迪拉姆銅板就好。這趟從德吉瑪廣場出發到染坊巷弄,前後大約花了三個半小時,門票加小費總共不到一百五十迪拉姆,卻是整趟旅程裡我覺得最物超所值的一段路。

美食實記:屋頂餐廳與廣場蝸牛湯的兩種摩洛哥

傍晚六點,我爬上位在Souk Cherifia巷弄深處的Nomad餐廳頂樓,穿過堆滿地毯與燈具的市集才找到那道不起眼的樓梯。頂樓露台被夕陽染成蜜糖色,我點了七蔬庫斯庫斯(約一百二十迪拉姆)跟一份改良版的鴿肉巴斯提亞(pastilla,約一百四十迪拉姆),庫斯庫斯的顆粒蒸得極輕盈,底下的燉蔬菜甜中帶點肉桂香,巴斯提亞外皮酥到一咬就掉滿桌的酥皮屑,裡頭卻是鹹甜交織的肉餡撒著糖粉跟肉桂粉,那種鹹甜同台的衝突感,一開始有點不適應,吃到第三口卻上癮。餐後我沿著原路走回德吉瑪廣場,天色已暗,整個廣場被烤肉攤的白煙籠罩成一片朦朧,我在編號14號攤位坐下,點了一碗babbouche蝸牛湯,老闆用長針把蝸牛肉一顆顆挑出來遞給我,湯頭是茴香、肉桂、丁香熬出的深色湯底,喝起來帶點藥膳的苦甘,一碗只要十迪拉姆,旁邊還配了一份現烤msemen千層薄餅沾蜂蜜,油香跟蜂蜜的甜膩混在一起,是那種吃完會滿手油卻捨不得擦的滿足。同一天吃到的兩頓飯,一頓精緻設計、一頓路邊直火,卻同樣誠實地代表著這座城市的味覺邏輯——不管貴賤,香料永遠不手軟。

在馬拉喀什,美味從不分貴賤,只分香料下得夠不夠狠。

風俗民情:地毯工坊裡的手作信仰

隔天上午,我在染坊巷弄附近一間不到十坪的地毯工坊裡,遇到正在紡羊毛線的優素福。他一邊踩著老式織布機,一邊用半生不熟的法文跟我解釋,他家三代都是柏柏爾織毯匠人,每一種顏色背後都有意義——番紅花黃象徵陽光與富足,靛藍代表天空與保護,胭脂蟲紅則是婚禮專用色。他遞給我一杯薄荷茶,倒茶時特意舉高茶壺讓茶水拉出一條細長弧線,他說這是待客的禮貌,茶要倒得夠高,泡沫才會夠多,而傳統上客人要喝滿三杯——「第一杯為了生命,第二杯為了愛,第三杯為了死亡」,他半開玩笑地說完自己也笑了。聊到興頭上,他提到再過三週就是宰牲節Eid al-Adha,街角的臨時羊市已經開始擺出待售的公羊,家家戶戶要提前準備,那是他一年裡最忙也最期待的日子,全家人會聚在一起分食羊肉,連隔壁鄰居都會互相分送。他也提醒我,遞東西給人一定要用右手,左手在這裡被視為不潔;還說每逢週五中午,幾乎所有家庭都會停下手邊工作,聚在一起吃庫斯庫斯,那是比週末更神聖的儀式感。我原本只是想買一條地毯當紀念品,最後卻在他的織布機前坐了將近兩小時,聽他講完一整套關於顏色與信仰的家族史,最後用八百迪拉姆買下一條手染的柏柏爾小地毯,議價的過程裡他反而顯得比我更享受。

夜幕:德吉瑪廣場與新城塗鴉的兩種當代

晚上九點,德吉瑪廣場進入最喧鬧的時刻,說書人halqa圍成一圈圈聽眾,手鼓聲跟嗩吶聲此起彼落,染指甲花的女人們拉著遊客的手腕現場畫henna,一晚喊價五十到一百迪拉姆不等,價格全靠當場眼神攻防。我站在人群外緣看了將近半小時,燈籠攤的手工鍛鐵燈具在夜色裡投出滿地星芒狀的光斑,那種老派的手工美學,跟我隔天下午在新城Gueliz區看到的完全是另一個世界——33 Rue Majorelle概念店裡陳列的當代摩洛哥設計師作品,幾何線條的燈具、極簡的陶器,把傳統zellige圖騰重新解構成現代語彙;附近牆面上還有幾幅色彩鮮豔的當代塗鴉壁畫,把柏柏爾圖騰跟街頭藝術風格混搭在一起。站在這兩個時空之間,我忽然明白馬拉喀什的美學從來不是靜止的博物館收藏,而是一場仍在進行式裡的對話——老匠人用手指延續五百年前的圖案,年輕設計師則拿噴漆重新詮釋同一套視覺語言。臨走前最後一晚,我又繞回廣場吃了一碗哈里拉湯當宵夜,湯匙碰到碗底的聲音混著遠處手鼓的節奏,我想這大概就是這座紅色城市最誠實的閉幕式。

ENGLISH

The taxi driver tossed my bag into the trunk the moment I landed in Marrakech, and as we drove toward the medina every wall along the road wore the same color — a sun-baked ochre red that seemed to catch fire in the late afternoon light. I later learned this hue has an actual name, 'Marrakech red,' and by municipal law every building in the city must be finished in some shade of it. I didn't yet know that for the next six days I'd be led around by color, line, and the fingerprints of handwork, as if enrolled in an architecture class with no textbook. My riad, an old merchant house tucked deep in the medina, had a cedar door so heavy it groaned when pushed open, and in the courtyard a noria fountain dripped quietly while I drifted in and out of jet-lagged sleep. I had one thought before falling asleep: get to Badi Palace before the light gets crowded out by tour groups.

Dawn at Badi Palace: A Lesson in Light and Rammed Earth

I stepped out of the hotel at six in the morning while the medina was still half-asleep. A baker pushed a wooden cart past me, the smell of fresh dough mixing with the cool morning air, and cats dozed on doorsteps as if they owned the street. Badi Palace doesn't open until nine, so I arrived deliberately early, standing at the gate by eight forty-five as only the fifth person in line. The entrance fee was 70 dirhams (roughly USD 7), and what it bought me was an almost roofless ruin — a sixteenth-century palace built by the Saadian sultan Ahmad al-Mansur to flaunt gold he'd won from Sudan, once famously boasted to be 'without equal.' Today only the pisé rammed-earth skeleton remains, framing four sunken orange orchards. The light cut across the eroded walls at exactly the right angle, turning the weathered texture into something like a topographic map. A colony of storks nested along the parapets, occasionally beating their wings with a dry, papery sound, and otherwise the ruin held nothing but wind and birdsong. Standing at the central reflecting pool, I thought about how this palace once had Italian marble and gilded ceilings, and yet stripped down to its bones it tells the truth about Moroccan architecture more honestly than any intact building could: the wall was never the point. What mattered was how long the light chose to stay.

What the ruin taught me: true luxury was never gold — it was how long the light was willing to linger.

Jardin Majorelle and the Berber Museum: When Blue Becomes a Creed

Leaving Badi Palace, I hailed a small red taxi toward the Gueliz district, haggling the fare down to 30 dirhams. By nine-thirty, the line at Jardin Majorelle already wrapped around the corner, and buying tickets on-site cost me nearly forty minutes I hadn't planned for — I regretted not booking online in advance. Combined entry to the garden and the Berber Museum ran about 200 dirhams. The moment I stepped inside, that cobalt blue hit me — a color French painter Jacques Majorelle mixed in the 1930s, later christened 'Majorelle Blue,' saturated almost to the point of glowing, yet strangely harmonious against the bamboo and cactus greens. Yves Saint Laurent bought the garden in 1980 to rescue it from ruin, and deep in the grounds a small memorial column bears his name. Inside the Berber Museum, silver jewelry, leather bags, and hand-loomed wool shawls tell the story of how Amazigh tribes on the edge of the Sahara built astonishingly intricate geometric patterns out of a limited palette — saffron yellow, cochineal red, indigo blue. I spent nearly an hour in front of a nineteenth-century Berber bridal headdress, transfixed by the layered weight of silver plates and amber beads, and it clicked for me: Moroccan aesthetics were never about restraint. They'd always rather pile on too much than leave anything bare.

How I Actually Did It: Getting Lost Between Bahia Palace and the Dyers' Souk

At noon I set out from Jemaa el-Fnaa, walking about fifteen minutes south along Rue Riad Zitoun el Jdid, past shops selling leather babouches and pierced-metal lamps, the alley narrowing with every block until Bahia Palace's understated wooden gate appeared behind a knot of tourists. Admission was 70 dirhams, with roughly a ten-minute line — I'd recommend avoiding the two-to-four-p.m. tour-bus rush. Built in the nineteenth century by the grand vizier Bou Ahmed for his four wives and twenty-four concubines, the palace's main courtyard floor of zellige mosaic tile — geometric shards of blue, white, and green — practically vibrated under the sun. Looking up, the cedar ceilings were carved and painted with muqarnas honeycomb patterns; a guide told me a single room's ceiling can take a team of craftsmen four to six months. I lingered nearly ninety minutes, deliberately veering off the main tourist route into quieter side chambers where light filtered through carved wooden screens onto empty tile floors. Leaving the palace, I followed my phone map north for another twenty minutes and stumbled, half by accident, into Souk Sidi Abdelaziz, the dyers' quarter — I smelled it before I saw it, a sharp mix of ammonia and plant dye hanging in the air, with freshly dyed wool draped overhead in a dripping rainbow of indigo blue, saffron yellow, and pomegranate-skin red. A dyer noticed me staring and handed me a wet fistful of wool to touch, laughing as he waved off my hesitation about photos — just a 10-dirham coin would do. From the square to the dyers' alley, the whole excursion took about three and a half hours and cost under 150 dirhams in tickets and tips combined, and it turned out to be the single best value stretch of the entire trip.

What I Actually Ate: A Rooftop Feast and a Bowl of Snail Soup

By six that evening I'd climbed to the rooftop of Nomad, tucked deep inside Souk Cherifia above stalls piled with rugs and lanterns, reachable only via an unmarked staircase easy to walk past twice. The terrace glowed honey-gold in the sunset, and I ordered seven-vegetable couscous (about 120 dirhams) and a reworked pigeon pastilla (about 140 dirhams). The couscous grains were steamed impossibly light, the stewed vegetables underneath sweet with a whisper of cinnamon, while the pastilla's pastry shattered at the first bite, scattering flakes across the table, revealing a filling that was sweet and savory at once, dusted with powdered sugar and cinnamon — a combination that felt wrong for exactly two bites before becoming addictive. Afterward I walked back to Jemaa el-Fnaa, now shrouded in the white smoke of grill stalls, and sat down at stall number 14 for a bowl of babbouche snail soup. The vendor fished the snails out one by one with a long pin and handed them to me, the broth a dark, medicinal-tasting blend of fennel, cinnamon, and clove — bittersweet and strangely comforting, and just 10 dirhams a bowl, served alongside a fresh msemen flatbread drizzled with honey, its oily richness sticking to my fingers in the best way. Two meals in one day, one refined and design-forward, one cooked over open flame at street level, both equally honest about this city's culinary logic: rich or poor, the spices never hold back.

In Marrakech, flavor never bows to price — only to how fearlessly the spices are used.

Local Life: Faith Woven Into a Rug Workshop

The next morning, in a workshop barely ten square meters near the dyers' alley, I met Youssef, spinning wool thread while working an old wooden loom. In broken French he explained his family had woven Amazigh rugs for three generations, and that every color carried meaning — saffron yellow for sunlight and abundance, indigo blue for sky and protection, cochineal red reserved for weddings. He poured me a glass of mint tea, lifting the pot high so the stream arced down and foamed at the top — a deliberate gesture of hospitality, he said, because the higher the pour, the better the foam. Tradition demands three glasses: 'the first for life, the second for love, the third for death,' he said, laughing at his own line. Warming up, he mentioned Eid al-Adha was only three weeks away — the temporary sheep market down the street had already begun setting up rams for sale, and this, he said, was his busiest and most anticipated time of year, when the whole family gathers to share the meat and even neighbors receive portions. He reminded me to always hand things with the right hand, since the left is considered unclean here, and that nearly every family pauses work each Friday at noon to share couscous together — a ritual he considers more sacred than any weekend. I'd only meant to buy a small rug as a souvenir; instead I sat at his loom for nearly two hours listening to his family's history of color and faith, eventually paying 800 dirhams for a small hand-dyed Berber rug, and he seemed to enjoy the haggling more than I did.

Nightfall: Two Kinds of Contemporary, Jemaa el-Fnaa and the Graffiti of Gueliz

By nine that night, Jemaa el-Fnaa had reached its loudest hour — storytellers' circles ringed with listeners, hand drums and oboe-like ghaita horns overlapping, henna painters grabbing tourists' wrists to sketch designs on the spot for anywhere from 50 to 100 dirhams, the price entirely a matter of eye-contact negotiation. I stood at the edge of the crowd for half an hour, watching hand-forged lantern stalls scatter star-shaped shadows across the pavement, an old-world craftsmanship entirely different from what I'd see the following afternoon in Gueliz — the new town. At the 33 Rue Majorelle concept store, contemporary Moroccan designers displayed geometric lamps and minimalist ceramics that deconstructed traditional zellige patterns into a modern visual language, while nearby walls carried vivid contemporary murals blending Amazigh motifs with street-art style. Standing between these two worlds, it hit me that Marrakech's aesthetic was never a static museum piece but an ongoing conversation — old artisans still tracing patterns five hundred years old with their fingers, while young designers reinterpret the same visual vocabulary with a spray can. On my last night, I circled back to the square for a bowl of harira soup as a late-night snack, the clink of my spoon against the bowl mixing with the distant rhythm of hand drums, and I thought this was probably the most honest closing scene this red city could offer.
