中文

清晨五點四十分,我從京都車站八条口的深夜巴士站牌走下來,腿還是麻的。從東京搭夜行巴士過來省了一晚住宿費,代價是脖子僵硬和滿嘴的巴士站零食味。京都車站前的空氣很涼,帶著一點潮濕的鐵軌味道,遠處已經有穿著西裝的上班族踩著細碎的腳步聲走過,月台廣播用那種輕輕柔柔卻字正腔圓的關西腔報著車次。我把背包重新揹好,心裡只有一個念頭:趕在遊覽車和旅行團湧入之前,先把伏見稻荷大社的千本鳥居走完。

清晨六點,獨自征服伏見稻荷的千本鳥居

從京都車站搭JR奈良線到稻荷駅只要五分鐘,一趟150日圓,班次不算密集,清晨這班車上除了我以外只有兩三個扛著腳架的攝影客。走出車站,朱紅色的樓門幾乎是貼著馬路矗立,還沒有半個攤販開張,連平常賣狐狸煎餅的小攤都還拉著鐵捲門。六點的伏見稻荷安靜得有點不真實,只有烏鴉的叫聲和我自己的腳步聲在鳥居間迴盪。我沿著參道往上走,第一段千本鳥居密度最高,朱紅色的木柱一根接一根,晨光從縫隙斜切進來,在地上切出一條一條的光影,踩上去的觸感是石板路的微微濕滑,昨夜似乎下過雨。

走到四辻(よつつじ)展望台大約花了四十分鐘,這裡視野一開,可以俯瞰整個京都市區,遠方的東寺五重塔在薄霧裡若隱若現。很多人以為伏見稻荷只有前段的千本鳥居值得拍照,其實真正安靜、原始的參道是過了四辻之後,遊客會銳減九成。我繼續往上,山路開始出現碎石與樹根,鳥居的數量變少,取而代之的是大大小小的小祠、狐狸石像和信眾供奉的迷你鳥居,整條山徑瀰漫著潮濕泥土與線香混合的氣味。攻頂來回大約花了我兩個半小時,中途在半山腰一間叫「にしむら亭」的茶屋喝了一杯冰涼麥茶,老闆娘笑說我是今天第一個客人。

下山時已經快九點,山腳下的參道完全變了樣,旅行團的旗子、狐狸仙貝的香氣、還有此起彼落的快門聲全部湧了進來。我慶幸自己選擇清晨出發——這條路線幾乎零花費(只有電車來回300日圓加一杯茶150日圓),卻換來整座山頭獨享的安靜。提醒後來者:山路有階梯落差,建議穿有抓地力的鞋子,夏天務必帶水,登山口的公廁過了四辻之後就沒有了。

京都最貴重的東西不是門票,是清晨六點那份沒有人跟你搶的安靜。

錦市場與深夜拉麵:京都人吃的日常

中午時分我搭公車回到市區,鑽進「京都的廚房」錦市場。這條長約400公尺的拱廊商店街,空氣裡混著醬油烤章魚的焦香、醃漬物的酸氣、還有炭火烤海鰻的煙。我在「三木雞卵」買了一支現烤玉子燒串,180日圓,老闆現點現煎,蛋液在鐵板上滋滋作響,外層微焦帶點醬油甜香,裡面卻還是滑嫩得像布丁,燙口但捨不得等它涼。旁邊「井上蒲鉾店」的炸魚餅一份也才150日圓,咬下去外皮酥脆,魚漿紮實帶點魚腥的鮮甜,是我吃過最划算的錦市場小吃。

傍晚我沒有選擇觀光客愛去的先斗町高級料亭,而是走進京都車站八条口附近、當地人排隊的「本家 第一旭 たかばし本店」。晚上七點的店裡幾乎座無虛席,穿著工作服的上班族和騎腳踏車來的歐吉桑擠在吧檯前呼嚕呼嚕吃麵。我點了招牌中華そば,850日圓,湯頭是豚骨加醬油的濃厚系,表面浮著一層油光,喝一口鹹香直衝喉頭,叉燒厚實但不軟爛,帶點嚼勁,蔥花切得極細,撒得毫不手軟。老闆娘一邊煮麵一邊用京都腔跟熟客閒聊今天的天氣,那種市井氣味,比任何米其林餐廳都讓我覺得真實。吃完一碗麵配一顆免費滷蛋,總共不到千円,是我這趟旅程裡最滿足的一餐。

灑水掃街的清晨儀式:京都人的規矩與四季感

住在祇園附近的町家民宿第三天,我起了個大早想拍空無一人的花見小路,卻意外撞見一幕讓我印象深刻的日常:七點不到,好幾戶老舖的女將已經穿著碎花圍裙,拿著長柄勺子在自家門前的石板路灑水,這叫「打ち水」,是京都人夏天用來降溫、也象徵「歡迎客人到來」的古老習俗。民宿的老闆娘阿姨看我好奇地站在旁邊拍照,笑著跟我解釋:灑水要灑在門口而不是路中央,水花濺起的方向也有講究,象徵把塵埃與暑氣一起帶走。她還提醒我,若在花見小路遇到匆匆走過的舞妓或藝妓,千萬不要伸手拉扯她們的和服衣袖拍照,那是近年因觀光客失禮行為而被立牌明文禁止的行為,違者甚至會被當地商店會罰款一萬日圓。

我後來刻意放慢腳步觀察,發現京都人對「界線」這件事極為講究:巷弄裡的町家住宅,格子窗內透出的暖黃燈光是私領域,鏡頭最好別直接對著人家窗戶;神社裡的手水舍洗手順序有先後,先左手、再右手、再漱口,最後把柄立起沖洗,是對神明的禮貌而非表演動作。若剛好遇上夏季的祇園祭(七月),整條四条通會封街搭起山鉾,晚上宵山時分提燈籠、穿浴衣的居民會傾巢而出,那種全城共同呼吸的節慶感,是任何攻略文字都很難完全轉述的體驗——你只能站在人群裡,聞著烤玉米與甜酒的香氣,才會真正懂。

在京都,禮貌不是規矩,是這座城市呼吸的方式。

背包客實戰帳本:住宿、交通與那些踩過的雷

先說住宿。我這趟住的是位在京都車站步行十五分鐘、五条附近的「K's House Kyoto」青年旅館,六人混宿床位一晚含早餐約3200日圓,公共廚房、交誼廳都很齊全,晚上還有旅客自發的清酒交流會。若想體驗傳統町家,大原、鞍馬口一帶有不少改建的Guesthouse,如「Guesthouse Waraku」,一泊約3800日圓起,榻榻米房型有淡淡的藺草香,睡起來意外舒服,但要注意多數町家民宿隔音較差,晚歸請放輕腳步。

交通部分,若行程集中在市區(清水寺、金閣寺、二条城一帶),巴士一日券700日圓非常划算,一天搭超過三趟就回本;但若像我一樣熱衷早起跑遠一點的伏見稻荷、嵐山,JR與地鐵反而更快更準時,建議下載Google地圖離線版隨時查詢接駁。我在嵐山踩了一個雷:中午十二點抵達竹林小徑,結果人潮多到幾乎無法好好拍照,後來跟當地攤販阿伯聊天才知道,竹林最美的時段其實是清晨七點半到八點半,遊覽車還沒到,晨光穿過竹葉灑落一地碎光,那才是明信片上那張照片的真實樣貌。

花費方面,我七天六夜的京都自由行,住宿約22000日圓、交通約9000日圓(含往返關西機場的HARUKA特急券)、餐飲平均一天2500日圓左右(早餐便利商店飯糰、午餐市場小吃、晚餐一碗拉麵或定食),再加上零星的神社參拜費用(多數神社免費,清水寺門票400日圓、金閣寺500日圓),總花費控制在五萬日圓有找,折合台幣約一萬出頭,對一個背包客來說算是相當實在的數字。真正該提醒的避雷點是:別在京都車站的置物櫃區猶豫太久,尖峰時段小型置物櫃常常一位難求,建議一早先去卡位;另外多數寺院五點左右就會關閉售票口,別學我曾經六點才衝到南禪寺撲了個空。

離開京都那天,我在車站月台等新幹線,回頭望了一眼那座熟悉的巨大車站中庭,忽然覺得這座城市最迷人的地方,從來不是那些出現在教科書上的世界遺產,而是清晨掃街的水聲、拉麵店裡此起彼落的呼嚕聲,還有一條走過就懂的、屬於在地人的節奏。

ENGLISH

It was 5:40 in the morning when I stepped off the overnight bus at the Hachijo exit of Kyoto Station, my legs still numb from the ride. Taking the night bus from Tokyo saved me a night's accommodation, at the cost of a stiff neck and a mouth that tasted like convenience-store snacks. The air outside was cool and faintly metallic, smelling of damp railway tracks. A few salarymen in suits were already clicking past on the platform, and the announcements rolled out in that soft, precise Kansai cadence. I hitched my backpack up and had one thought: get to Fushimi Inari's thousand torii gates before the tour buses did.

6 AM Alone Among the Thousand Torii Gates

The JR Nara Line from Kyoto Station to Inari Station takes just five minutes and costs 150 yen. The trains aren't frequent at that hour, and besides me, only two photographers with tripods slung over their shoulders rode along. Stepping out, the vermillion Romon Gate practically touches the road, and not a single stall was open yet — even the fox-shaped rice cracker cart was still shuttered. At six in the morning, Fushimi Inari felt almost unreal in its silence, just crows calling and my own footsteps echoing between the gates. The first stretch of Senbon Torii is the densest, red pillar after red pillar, morning light slicing through the gaps and cutting stripes across the ground. The stone path underfoot was slightly slick — it must have rained overnight.

It took about forty minutes to reach the Yotsutsuji lookout, where the view opens up over the city, and Toji Temple's five-story pagoda floated faintly in the haze in the distance. Most visitors assume the lower Senbon Torii section is the highlight, but the truly quiet, untouched part of the trail is beyond Yotsutsuji, where the crowds drop by ninety percent. I kept climbing; the path turned to gravel and roots, the gates thinned out, replaced by small shrines, fox statues, and miniature torii left by worshippers. The whole trail smelled of wet soil and incense. The round trip to the summit took about two and a half hours, and halfway up I stopped at a small teahouse called Nishimura-tei for a cold glass of mugicha — the owner laughed and said I was her first customer of the day.

By the time I came down it was nearly nine, and the base of the trail had completely transformed — tour flags, the smell of grilled senbei, shutters clicking non-stop. I was glad I'd left at dawn: the whole route cost almost nothing (300 yen round-trip on the train plus 150 yen for tea) yet gave me a mountain nearly to myself. A word of warning for anyone following this route: the stairs have uneven drops, wear shoes with real grip, bring water in summer, and there are no public restrooms past Yotsutsuji.

Kyoto's most precious commodity isn't the admission fee — it's the silence at six in the morning that nobody else is fighting you for.

Nishiki Market and Midnight Ramen: How Kyoto Actually Eats

By midday I'd taken the bus back into the city center and ducked into Nishiki Market, known as 'Kyoto's Kitchen.' Along this roughly 400-meter covered arcade, the air mixed the caramelized smell of soy-glazed grilled octopus, the tang of pickles, and smoke from charcoal-grilled conger eel. At Miki Keiran I bought a freshly grilled tamagoyaki skewer for 180 yen — the owner cooked it to order right there, the egg sizzling on the griddle, the outside lightly caramelized with a soy-sweet edge while the inside stayed as soft as pudding. It was too hot to eat slowly, but I couldn't wait for it to cool either. Next door, Inoue Kamaboko's fried fish cake was only 150 yen a piece — crispy skin, dense and faintly sweet fish paste inside. It might be the best value bite in the whole market.

That evening, instead of the polished ryotei restaurants of Pontocho that tourists gravitate toward, I walked into Honke Daiichi Asahi Takabashi Honten, a ramen shop near the Hachijo exit locals actually queue for. At seven, the place was nearly full — office workers still in uniform, an old man who'd biked over, all hunched over the counter slurping loudly. I ordered the signature chuka soba for 850 yen. The broth was a rich tonkotsu-shoyu blend, a sheen of oil floating on top; one sip sent a salty punch straight up the back of my throat. The chashu was thick but not falling apart, with real chew left in it, and the scallions were sliced almost paper-thin, piled on generously. The owner's wife stirred the pot while chatting with regulars in unmistakable Kyoto dialect about the day's weather. That kind of unpretentious atmosphere felt more honest than any Michelin-starred meal. With a free soft-boiled egg on the side, the whole dinner came to under 1,000 yen — the most satisfying meal of the whole trip.

The Morning Ritual of Sweeping and Sprinkling: Kyoto's Unspoken Rules

On my third morning, staying in a machiya guesthouse near Gion, I got up early hoping to photograph an empty Hanamikoji Street, and stumbled instead onto something that stuck with me. Just before seven, several shopkeepers' wives, in floral aprons, were already out front with long-handled ladles, sprinkling water onto the stone pavement in front of their shops. This is called uchimizu — an old custom meant to cool the street in summer and, symbolically, to welcome guests. My guesthouse's obasan, catching me staring, laughed and explained: you sprinkle at the entrance, not the middle of the road, and even the direction the water splashes matters — it's meant to carry away dust and heat together. She also warned me that if I happened to pass a geiko or maiko hurrying down Hanamikoji, I should never grab their kimono sleeves for a photo — a rule now posted explicitly after too many tourists behaved badly, with fines up to 10,000 yen enforced by the local shopping association.

After that, I slowed down and started noticing how carefully Kyoto people guard boundaries. The warm yellow light glowing through the latticed windows of a machiya is private — better not to point a lens straight at someone's window. At shrine purification basins, there's a proper order: left hand first, then right, then rinse your mouth, then tip the ladle upright to rinse the handle — a gesture of respect toward the kami, not a performance for cameras. If you happen to be there in July for Gion Matsuri, all of Shijo Street shuts down for the towering yamahoko floats, and on Yoiyama evening, residents in yukata pour out with paper lanterns glowing. That sense of an entire city breathing together for one night is something no guidebook can fully translate — you have to stand in the crowd, breathing in grilled corn and sweet amazake, to actually understand it.

In Kyoto, politeness isn't a rule — it's the rhythm the whole city breathes by.

The Backpacker's Real Ledger: Stays, Transport, and the Mistakes I Made

Let's start with lodging. I stayed at K's House Kyoto, a fifteen-minute walk from Kyoto Station near Gojo, where a bed in a six-person dorm with breakfast ran about 3,200 yen a night — full shared kitchen, a proper common room, and a spontaneous sake-tasting circle among travelers most nights. If you want the machiya experience, the Ohara and Kuramaguchi areas have several converted guesthouses, like Guesthouse Waraku, from about 3,800 yen a night; the tatami rooms carry a faint scent of rush grass and were surprisingly comfortable, though soundproofing in most machiya is thin, so tread lightly coming in late.

For transport, if your itinerary stays central — Kiyomizu-dera, Kinkaku-ji, Nijo Castle — the 700-yen all-day bus pass pays for itself after three rides. But for early runs out to Fushimi Inari or Arashiyama, like I did, JR and the subway are faster and more reliable; I'd recommend downloading offline Google Maps to check connections on the go. I made one real mistake in Arashiyama: I arrived at the bamboo grove at noon and the crowd was so dense I could barely get a clear shot. A vendor later told me the real window is 7:30 to 8:30 AM, before the tour buses arrive, when morning light slants through the bamboo leaves and scatters into that postcard-perfect pattern on the ground.

As for total spend: over seven days and six nights, my accommodation ran about 22,000 yen, transport about 9,000 yen (including the Haruka Limited Express to and from Kansai Airport), and food averaged roughly 2,500 yen a day — a convenience-store rice ball for breakfast, market snacks for lunch, a bowl of ramen or a set meal for dinner. Add in scattered shrine and temple admissions (most shrines are free; Kiyomizu-dera is 400 yen, Kinkaku-ji is 500 yen) and my total came in just under 50,000 yen — a genuinely modest number for a backpacker. Two warnings worth repeating: don't dawdle over the coin lockers at Kyoto Station, since the smaller ones vanish fast during peak hours — grab one first thing in the morning. And most temples close their ticket counters around five, so don't repeat my mistake of sprinting to Nanzen-ji at six only to find the gates shut.

On my last day, waiting on the platform for the shinkansen, I looked back one more time at that familiar, cavernous station atrium and realized Kyoto's real charm was never the sites printed in textbooks. It was the sound of water being swept across stone at dawn, the overlapping slurps in a crowded ramen shop, and a rhythm you only understand once you've walked it yourself.
