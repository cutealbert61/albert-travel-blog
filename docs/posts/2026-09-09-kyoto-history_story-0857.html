中文

抵達京都的第二天晚上,我在御池通旁的老旅館裡翻著地圖,忽然覺得那些「必去景點清單」很無聊。京都不是一座用來打勾的城市,它是一層一層被燒過、重建過、換過主人的土地。於是我決定放下清單,用一天的時間,順著一場改變日本命運的政變,把這座城走一遍。

黎明的寺町通:本能寺之變的餘燼

清晨五點五十分,鬧鐘響的時候天還沒亮。京都的六月早晨帶著一點潮濕的涼意,街道上只有便利商店的燈還醒著。我沿著寺町通往北走,路面濕濕的,像是剛下過一場沒人看見的雨。六點二十分,我站在本能寺的山門前——這裡不是1582年那場政變發生的原址,豐臣秀吉重建京都街道時,把寺院整體遷到了現在的御池下御門這一帶,但名字和信仰都被完整地搬了過來。

1582年6月21日凌晨,家臣明智光秀突然率兵包圍了織田信長暫居的本能寺,一句「敵在本能寺」成了日本戰國史上最著名的背叛台詞。信長自知無法脫身,點火自焚,一代梟雄就這樣消失在火光裡,連遺體都沒能確認。我站在清晨無人的寺內庭院,聞到值堂僧人剛點的香,聽見他用竹掃帚清理石階上的落葉,沙沙的聲音在寂靜裡特別清楚。本堂九點才開放,寶物殿裡陳列著一小片據說是信長遺物殘片的展示,五百日圓的門票,說實話我看完只覺得那塊焦黑的金屬碎片,承載的重量遠比它的尺寸大得多。

京都燒過太多次了,但每一次燒完,人們選擇的不是遺忘,而是把灰燼重新蓋成一座新的廟。

二條城的鶯張地板:德川三百年的開場與收場

八點四十分,我沿著御池通往西走了大約十五分鐘,抵達二條城東大手門。早上還沒到開放時間,已經有一小群人在門口排隊,大多是舉著相機的外國遊客和幾位穿制服的高中生。九點準時開門,一般參觀券八百日圓,想進二之丸御殿內部要另外購買通行券,總共大約一千三百日圓,入口處有可租借的語音導覽機,五百日圓,有中文選項。

走進二之丸御殿,最讓我印象深刻的不是金碧輝煌的障壁畫,而是腳下的地板——每走一步都會發出「唧、唧」的鳥鳴般聲響,導覽員解釋這是「鶯張地板」,木板與釘子之間刻意留出摩擦空隙,任何人想偷偷靠近都會被地板出賣。我刻意放慢腳步試著踩得很輕,結果聲音反而更明顯,忍不住笑了出來,同行的一位京都阿伯也笑著說:「這地板比忍者還厲害。」二條城建於1603年,是德川家康在京都的據點,象徵幕府權力凌駕天皇所在的京都御所;但諷刺的是,264年後,末代將軍德川慶喜正是在這座城裡的大廣間宣布「大政奉還」,把統治權還給明治天皇。同一座建築,開場與收場都在這裡發生,權力像潮水一樣進來又退去。庭園裡的枯山水安靜得只剩水流聲,我坐在迴廊邊看了將近二十分鐘,才捨得離開。整段參觀大約花了一個半小時。

錦市場的煙火氣:京都人的廚房

離開二條城,我搭乘東西線一站到烏丸御池轉搭巴士,十一點十五分左右擠進了錦市場的拱頂長廊。空氣裡混著炭烤醬汁的焦香、高湯的鮮甜味,還有醃漬物散發出的酸香,人聲和店家吆喝聲擠在窄窄的通道裡,比我想像中還要熱鬧擁擠。第一站是三木雞卵,買了一支現烤玉子燒串,三百五十日圓,外層微焦帶脆,內裡卻是滑嫩到快要塌陷的口感,甜鹹比例剛好,還冒著熱氣。

接著轉進うね乃的「だし場」,花三百日圓買了一杯現煮鰹魚昆布高湯,店員直接遞給我一個小陶杯,湯色清澄,喝下去先是鹹鮮的海味,接著是甘甜的餘韻在舌根停留,那一刻我終於懂為什麼京都料理總說「一湯定味」。再往前走到麩嘉,買了一顆生麩饅頭,兩百日圓,外皮是麻糬般的Q彈麵麩,咬開後紅豆餡綿密不甜到膻,和外皮的清淡形成很舒服的對比。中途在打田漬物試吃了幾種漬物,芥末風味的柴漁漬讓我整個鼻腔都嗆了一下,老闆娘在旁邊笑著遞水給我。最後在中村藤吉錦小路店買了一支濃茶霜淇淋收尾,四百八十日圓,苦味紮實不澀口。整條市場逛下來大約花了一個半小時,吃吃喝喝加起來不到一千五百日圓,卻比任何一間餐廳都更貼近京都人真實的日常餐桌。

錦市場不是觀光紀念品店,它是京都人買晚餐配菜的地方,只是恰好連遊客也一起餵飽了。

祇園巷弄的三味線:茶屋文化與一位老婦人的故事

下午兩點半,我從錦市場搭公車到祇園,走進花見小路。石板路兩側是掛著紅殼格子窗(弁柄格子)的茶屋建築,午後陽光斜斜地照進窄巷,安靜得幾乎聽不到腳步聲以外的聲音。在八坂神社門口附近,我遇到一位坐在小攤前包粽形護符的老婦人,她賣的是「粽」(chimaki)——不是食物,而是掛在自家門口驅邪的稻草護符,一個八百日圓。我好奇問起典故,她放下手邊的稻草,笑著跟我說起祇園祭的傳說:相傳素戔嗚尊化身旅人夜宿人間,只有貧窮的蘇民將來願意收留他,神明離去前教導蘇民將來的子孫在門口掛上茅草護符,便能躲過瘟疫。八坂神社的祇園祭正是源自869年京都爆發瘟疫時,人們抬著神轅巡遊全城祈求消災,一路演變成今日七月壯觀的山鉾巡行。

她說著說著,忽然抬頭指向巷子深處,壓低聲音提醒我:「那邊私人小巷不能拍照喔,拍舞妓的話,罰一萬日圓。」我這才知道自2019年起,祇園部分私人巷弄已立法禁止未經同意拍攝藝妓與舞妓,因為太多遊客會伸手去拉她們的和服袖子,甚至追著拍照。傍晚五點半左右,我站在一間茶屋外,聽見二樓窗戶裡飄出斷斷續續的三味線練習聲,琴音生澀卻認真,不久後木門滑開,一位舞妓踩著木屐匆匆而過,白粉香氣混著木屐敲打石板的清脆聲響,一晃眼便消失在巷口轉角,像是這座城市故意留給我的一個殘影。

伏見稻荷的千本鳥居:暮色中向歷史道別

傍晚五點,我從祇園四条搭京阪本線南下,大約十分鐘後抵達伏見稻荷站。走出車站,夕陽已經把大鳥居染成濃烈的橘紅色,這個時段遊客明顯變少,只剩下三三兩兩想拍晚霞的人。穿過本殿,我一頭鑽進千本鳥居的隧道,朱紅色的木柱一根接一根地把天光切成細碎的光斑,伸手摸上去,木頭還帶著白天曬過太陽的溫度,每根柱子側面都刻著捐贈企業或個人的名字與年份,有些已經超過百年,油漆剝落露出斑駁木紋。空氣裡是潮濕的杉木與青苔混合的氣味,蟬鳴一陣一陣地從山林裡傳來,愈往上走愈稀薱。

途中經過幾隻口中咬著稻穗鑰匙的狐狸石像,那是稻荷神的使者,鑰匙象徵掌管米倉的權柄,難怪京都商家至今仍會定期來此參拜求生意興隆。晚上六點十五分,我爬到四辻展望台,整個京都盆地在腳下鋪展開來,建築群漸漸亮起燈火,遠處比叡山的輪廓被夕陽鑲了一道金邊。展望台旁有間小茶屋還在營業,我點了一碗狐狸烏龍麵,六百日圓,湯頭清甜,豆皮吸滿湯汁後鹹甜交融,一邊吃一邊看太陽緩緩沉入嵐山方向的山脊線,竟有種奇妙的儀式感——彷彿這一天追著京都的權力更迭走了一整圈,最後在這裡跟歷史道了聲別。

晚上七點十五分下山時,鳥居兩側的石燈籠已經亮起微黃的光,山道恢復了黑夜該有的安靜。我想起明治天皇1868年遷都東京後,京都失去了首都的名分,卻沒有失去它作為靈魂居所的地位。這座城市燒過、換過主人、被政治拋下過,但它從沒真正死去,只是把每一次的灰燼,都認真地重新燒成了瓦、蓋成了廟、寫成了一個個等著旅人親自走進去的故事。

ENGLISH

On my second night in Kyoto, sitting in a small ryokan just off Oike-dori with a map spread across the tatami, I realized the standard checklist of temples bored me. Kyoto isn't a city you tick off. It's a landscape that has been burned, rebuilt, and handed between rulers over and over again. So I put the guidebook away and decided to spend one full day tracing a single coup that changed the course of Japanese history, letting it lead me through the city instead.

Dawn on Teramachi Street: Ashes of the Honno-ji Incident

My alarm went off at 5:50 a.m., well before sunrise. Kyoto's June mornings carry a damp chill, and the only lights still on belonged to a lone convenience store. I walked north along Teramachi Street, the pavement slick as if rain had fallen while no one was watching. By 6:20 a.m. I was standing at the gate of Honno-ji Temple — not the original site of the 1582 coup, since Toyotomi Hideyoshi relocated the entire temple when he redrew the city grid, but the name and the faith survived the move intact.

In the early hours of June 21, 1582, the general Akechi Mitsuhide's troops suddenly surrounded the temple where his lord Oda Nobunaga was staying. 'The enemy is at Honno-ji' became one of the most infamous lines in Japanese history. Cornered with no escape, Nobunaga set the building alight and took his own life; his body was never recovered. Standing alone in the quiet courtyard that morning, I caught the scent of incense a monk had just lit and heard the rhythmic scrape of his bamboo broom against the stone steps. The treasure hall doesn't open until 9 a.m., and inside, for a 500-yen entry fee, sits a small, charred metal fragment said to be a relic from Nobunaga's death. It looked unremarkable, yet the weight of what it represented felt far heavier than its size.

Kyoto has burned more times than anyone can count, but every time the ashes cooled, its people didn't choose to forget — they chose to build something new on top.

The Nightingale Floors of Nijo Castle: Where the Tokugawa Era Opened and Closed

At 8:40 a.m. I walked west along Oike-dori for about fifteen minutes and arrived at the Higashi Ote-mon gate of Nijo Castle. A small line had already formed, mostly tourists with cameras and a few high school students in uniform. The gates opened right at 9 a.m. General admission costs 800 yen, and a combined ticket for the Ninomaru Palace interior brings the total to roughly 1,300 yen. An audio guide device can be rented at the entrance for 500 yen, with an English track available.

Inside the Ninomaru Palace, what struck me wasn't the gilded sliding-door paintings but the floor itself — every step produced a chirping, bird-like squeak. The guide explained these are 'nightingale floors,' built with deliberate gaps between the boards and nails so that no intruder could approach silently. I tried walking softly to test it, and the squeaking only got louder; an elderly local visitor beside me laughed and said the floor was better than any ninja at catching trespassers. The castle was built in 1603 as Tokugawa Ieyasu's Kyoto stronghold, a deliberate symbol of shogunate power looming over the emperor's nearby palace. The irony is that 264 years later, the last shogun, Tokugawa Yoshinobu, stood in this very castle's Great Hall to announce the 'Restoration of Imperial Rule,' handing authority back to Emperor Meiji. The same building opened and closed an entire era. In the garden, the dry rock landscape was silent except for the faint trickle of hidden water; I sat by the corridor for nearly twenty minutes before I could bring myself to leave. The whole visit took about ninety minutes.

The Sizzle of Nishiki Market: Kyoto's Everyday Kitchen

From the castle, I took the Tozai subway line one stop to Karasuma-Oike, then a short bus ride, and by 11:15 a.m. I was squeezed into the covered arcade of Nishiki Market. The air was thick with the char of grilling sauce, the sweetness of simmering dashi, and the sharp tang of pickles, all crowded into a narrow lane far busier than I'd expected. My first stop was Miki Keiran, where I bought a freshly grilled tamagoyaki skewer for 350 yen — the edges lightly charred and crisp, the center so soft it nearly collapsed, the sweet-savory balance exactly right, still steaming when I bit in.

Next I ducked into Une-no's 'Dashi-ba' counter and paid 300 yen for a small cup of freshly brewed bonito-kelp broth. The server handed it over in a tiny ceramic cup; the flavor hit salty and oceanic first, then settled into a lingering sweetness at the back of the tongue. Only then did I understand why Kyoto cuisine insists that 'one soup defines the meal.' Further down, at Fuka, I bought a steamed nama-fu bun for 200 yen — the chewy, mochi-like wheat-gluten skin wrapped around a smooth, lightly sweet red bean paste that never overwhelmed the mild wrapper. I sampled a few pickles at Uchida Tsukemono, and the mustard-laced shibazuke made my sinuses sting instantly, prompting the shopkeeper to hand me water with a knowing smile. I finished with a rich matcha soft-serve at the Nishiki branch of Nakamura Tokichi, 480 yen, its bitterness firm without turning astringent. The whole crawl took about ninety minutes and cost under 1,500 yen total, yet it felt closer to Kyoto's real dinner table than any formal restaurant could.

Nishiki Market isn't a souvenir shop for tourists — it's where Kyoto locals still buy tonight's side dishes. Visitors just happen to get fed along the way.

Shamisen Notes in the Gion Alleys: Teahouse Culture and an Old Woman's Story

By 2:30 p.m. I'd taken a bus from Nishiki to Gion and was walking down Hanamikoji Street. Stone pavement lined both sides with teahouses behind red-lacquered lattice windows, the afternoon sun slanting into the narrow lane so quietly I could hear little beyond my own footsteps. Near the entrance to Yasaka Shrine, I met an elderly woman folding straw into chimaki charms at a small stall. These aren't food — they're straw amulets hung on doorways to ward off illness, priced at 800 yen each. When I asked about the custom, she set down her work and told me the legend behind Gion Matsuri: the god Susanoo-no-Mikoto once traveled disguised as a stranger, and only a poor man named Somin Shorai offered him shelter. Before leaving, the god taught Somin's descendants to hang straw charms at their doors to escape plague forever after. The festival itself traces back to the year 869, when Kyoto was ravaged by epidemic and residents paraded portable shrines through the city to pray for deliverance — a ritual that eventually grew into today's spectacular Yamaboko float procession every July.

Mid-conversation, she suddenly gestured toward a narrower lane behind her and lowered her voice: photography in that private alley is banned, she warned — snapping a photo of a maiko there can bring a 10,000-yen fine. That's when I learned that since 2019, parts of Gion have restricted photography specifically because too many tourists were grabbing at the sleeves of passing geisha and maiko for photos. Around 5:30 p.m., standing outside a teahouse, I heard the halting, earnest practice of a shamisen drifting down from an upstairs window. Not long after, a wooden door slid open and a maiko hurried past in her geta sandals, the scent of white rice powder mixing with the sharp clack of wood on stone, before she vanished around the corner — leaving behind an afterimage the city seemed to have arranged just for me.

A Thousand Torii at Fushimi Inari: Saying Goodbye to History at Dusk

At 5 p.m. I caught the Keihan Main Line south from Gion-Shijo, arriving at Fushimi Inari Station about ten minutes later. Stepping outside, the great vermilion torii was already glowing deep orange in the fading sun, and the crowds had thinned considerably at this hour. Past the main hall, I plunged into the tunnel of Senbon Torii, thousands of red-lacquered pillars slicing the evening light into scattered fragments. Touching one, the wood still held the day's warmth; each pillar bore the name and date of the company or individual who had donated it, some over a century old, their paint peeling to reveal grain beneath. The air smelled of damp cedar and moss, cicadas calling in waves that grew sparser the higher I climbed.

Along the way I passed several fox statues clutching keys in their mouths — the messengers of Inari, the deity of rice and, by extension, commerce, the key symbolizing mastery over the granary. It's no surprise that Kyoto merchants still make pilgrimages here to pray for prosperity. By 6:15 p.m. I reached the Yotsutsuji viewing platform, where the entire Kyoto basin unfolded below, city lights just beginning to flicker on as the sun edged behind the silhouette of the Arashiyama mountains, rimming them in gold. A small teahouse near the lookout was still open; I ordered a bowl of kitsune udon for 600 yen, the broth sweet and clean, the fried tofu soaking up just enough of it to turn each bite savory-sweet, while the sun slid fully behind the ridgeline. It felt oddly ceremonial — as if the whole day, chasing Kyoto's shifting seats of power from dawn to dusk, had led me here to say a proper goodbye to the story.

Descending at 7:15 p.m., the stone lanterns along both sides of the path had already lit up in a faint amber glow, and the mountain path settled into the kind of silence only nightfall brings. I thought about how, after Emperor Meiji moved the capital to Tokyo in 1868, Kyoto lost its official title but never lost its role as the country's spiritual home. This city has burned, changed hands, and been abandoned by politics more than once — yet it has never truly died. It simply takes each new pile of ashes and, with quiet persistence, turns it back into tile, into temple, into another story waiting for a traveler willing to walk straight into it.
