中文

飛機降落馬拉喀什的那個傍晚,計程車司機搖下車窗,一股混合著炭火、孜然與塵土的氣味灌進車廂,他笑著說:「歡迎來到紅城,這裡的一切都用鼻子先認識。」我當時還不太懂這句話,直到接下來五天,我幾乎是用嗅覺和味覺重新畫了一張馬拉喀什的地圖。這不是一篇教你「必去景點」的攻略,而是我跟著市集攤販、麵包師傅、皮革工人和一位邀我吃週五午餐的婦人,一起走過的飲食路徑。

破曉前的德吉瑪廣場,城市還沒醒

清晨五點四十分,宣禮聲從庫圖比亞清真寺的宣禮塔傳來,把我從里亞德(riad)老宅的睡夢中拉起。我披了件外套走出巷弄,老城麥地那(Medina)的石板路還帶著夜裡的涼意,幾隻貓蜷在門檻上,對我這個不速之客連眼皮都懶得抬。轉過三個彎,穿過氣味還殘留著昨夜柳橙皮香的Riad Zitoun Lakdim巷,我在六點零五分走進了德吉瑪廣場(Jemaa el-Fna)。

白天喧鬧到近乎失控的廣場,此刻安靜得不可思議。只有一位老人拿著棕櫚葉紮成的掃把,一下一下清掃著昨夜留下的甘蔗渣與瓜子殼,動作緩慢卻規律,像是某種儀式。橘子汁攤位還沒開張,推車一字排開,塑膠布上凝著露水。這時我看見一位麵包師傅推著木板車經過,車上擺滿了印著不同花紋的圓形生麵團——那是我第一次認識「公共烤爐」(ferrane)文化:當地家庭在家揉麵、用木戳或手指捏出屬於自家的記號,再送到街角的公共烤爐,付上一兩塊迪拉姆的柴火費,由烤爐師傅統一烘烤。沒有人會拿錯麵包,因為每一團麵團都有自己的「簽名」。這個畫面比任何導覽手冊都更讓我理解:在馬拉喀什,食物從一開始就是社群共有的事。

在這座城市,連一條麵包都有屬於自己的家族印記。

香料巷弄與市場迷宮:哈桑的番紅花

早上八點多,我在里亞德的天台吃了摺得像信封的薄煎餅姆斯曼(msemen),沾著杏仁堅果醬阿慕(amlou)和蜂蜜,配一杯甜到發膩的薄荷茶,才有力氣鑽進市集。從Souk Semmarine入口一走進去,頭頂的蘆葦編織頂棚把陽光切成一條一條的光帶,灑在堆疊如山的皮拖鞋、銅燈與地毯上。空氣裡是皮革鞣製劑、孜然、機車廢氣和薄荷混在一起的味道,叫賣聲此起彼落,一開始我幾乎是被推著走的。

走到市集深處的Rahba Kedima廣場,我在一個掛滿麻布袋的香料攤前停下腳步,攤主哈桑用中文跟我打招呼「你好」,把我逗笑了。他攤位上擺著至少三十種顏色的香料粉,他抓起一撮番紅花絲放在我掌心,要我聞聞看真假——真的番紅花帶著微微的乾草與蜂蜜氣息,而不是死氣沉沉的塵土味。他家三代都做香料生意,爺爺是柏柏爾人,從阿特拉斯山區把野生香草帶進城裡賣。他一邊秤重一邊跟我解釋「摩洛哥綜合香料」拉斯哈努特(ras el hanout)裡最多可以有三十五種成分,包括肉桂、薑黃、乾玫瑰花瓣,甚至微量的天堂椒。我原本開價要50克番紅花120迪拉姆,他開價350,我們一來一往喝了兩杯他請的薄荷茶,最後以180迪拉姆成交——哈桑笑著說這才是「應該有的價格」,殺價在這裡從來不是敵對,而是一種認識彼此的社交過程,少了它反而顯得無禮。

親身走一趟:巴伯達巴革皮革染坊的清晨路線

離開香料攤,我決定去看看那個Instagram上常出現的彩色染缸——巴伯達巴革皮革染坊(Bab Debbagh Tanneries)。從德吉瑪廣場叫了一輛小計程車(petit taxi),司機沒跳表,我們議定15迪拉姆,車程約十分鐘,穿過越來越窄、越來越安靜的巷弄,最後在城牆邊一座古老城門前停下。也可以選擇步行,約二十五分鐘,會先穿過賣生皮與皮革工具的Souk Btana,那條路線氣味更濃烈,但能看到更多真實的日常工作場景。

早上九點四十分抵達時,一位自稱免費導覽的年輕人尤瑟夫主動靠近,我事先做過功課,知道這類「免費」通常是按皮革店銷售抽成,便直接問清楚「不買東西也沒關係嗎」,他爽快答應。跟著他爬上一段又窄又滑的樓梯,到達一處俯瞰染坊的屋頂平台,他遞給我幾片薄荷葉,示意壓在鼻孔下——因為染坊使用鴿糞與石灰軟化獸皮,氣味嗆鼻到讓我瞬間理解薄荷葉的存在意義。眼前是幾十個圓形石缸,像巨大的調色盤:白色缸是軟化階段,咖啡色、赭紅、鵝黃各自對應番紅花、罌粟與靛藍等天然染料。幾名工人赤腳站在及膝的染液裡踩踏皮革,沒有任何防護裝備,尤瑟夫說這門手藝從十一世紀延續至今,是家族世代相傳的重活。我在屋頂待了約四十五分鐘拍照、提問,下樓後象徵性給了尤瑟夫50迪拉姆小費(即使他堅稱免費,禮貌上還是該給),接著被順勢帶進一間皮革店,店主開價300迪拉姆的手工皮夾,我殺到120迪拉姆買下,算是紀念品也算是對這趟體驗的回饋。整趟行程含來回車資、小費與購物大約花費200迪拉姆、兩個小時,建議選擇上午較涼爽時段前往,正午高溫會讓氣味加倍濃烈,鞋子最好穿著不介意弄髒的款式。

廣場上的深夜盛宴:第31號攤位與蝸牛湯

傍晚六點半,我又回到德吉瑪廣場,這次它已經完全換了一張臉。夕陽把清真寺尖塔染成蜜糖色,上百個烤肉攤同時升起白煙,鼓聲、笛聲和叫賣聲混成一片,耍蛇人正在收拾道具準備收工,畫亨娜的婦人拉著遊客的手不放。我沒有讓拴著鏈子的猴子拍照,牠們疲憊的眼神讓我不太忍心參與這種表演。穿過人群,我找到編號攤位區裡的第31號攤「Chez Aicha」——當地朋友再三推薦,說這家的烤肉串三十年沒換過老闆。

我點了一份綜合烤肉拼盤,60迪拉姆,有孜然味濃厚的肯夫塔肉丸(kefta)和微辣的梅爾蓋茲香腸(merguez),用剛出爐還燙手的圓餅khobz撕下來包著吃,搭配一小碟哈里薩辣醬。肉的焦香混著炭火味,咬下去先是酥脆的表皮,接著是多汁帶點粗獷顆粒感的肉餡,比我在里亞德吃過的任何一頓精緻晚餐都更讓我滿足。吃完主餐,我又走到專賣蝸牛湯(babbouche)的推車前坐下,10迪拉姆一碗,湯裡飄著茴香、孜然與百里香的香氣,用牙籤一顆一顆挑出蝸牛肉,最後把剩下的湯直接端起來喝掉——隔壁一位常客歐馬爾告訴我,馬拉喀什人相信這碗湯有助消化,常在飽餐一頓後來上一碗。他還說,這片廣場的美食攤已經被聯合國教科文組織列為非物質文化遺產,每個攤位都是家族企業,誰家的烤肉比較道地,是本地人永遠吵不完的話題,就像那不勒斯人吵誰家披薩最正宗一樣認真。

這片廣場入夜後不是市集,是一整座城市的露天餐桌。

里亞德晚餐與週五的庫斯庫斯:慢燉出來的時間感

隔天是星期五,里亞德的女主人法蒂瑪一早就在忙碌,她邀我留下來吃「星期五午餐」——這是摩洛哥家庭幾乎雷打不動的傳統。中午的清真寺主麻聚禮結束後,一家人陸續回到餐桌,法蒂瑪端出一大盤鋪滿七種蔬菜與燉羊肉的庫斯庫斯,全家人圍著同一個盤子,只用右手抓食,小孩會被溫柔糾正手勢——這不只是吃飯,更像是一週一次的家庭校準時刻。法蒂瑪的母親說,週五的庫斯庫斯是「真主的禮物日」,家家戶戶幾乎都在這天煮,街上麵包店和肉舖從清晨就特別忙碌。

飯後她端上薄荷茶,示範了我看過最講究的倒茶方式:茶壺舉高至少三十公分,茶水拉出一條細長的弧線注入玻璃杯,表面浮起一層細緻泡沫。她說當地有句柏柏爾諺語形容這三杯茶——第一杯像人生一樣苦澀,第二杯像愛情一樣甜蜜,第三杯像死亡一樣溫柔平靜。那天晚上,我又特地去了一趟慕阿辛清真寺(Mouassine)附近一家家庭經營的小餐館,點了馬拉喀什最具代表性的塔吉亞(tanjia)——一種裝在陶罐裡的燉肉,傳統上不是在家裡的爐火上煮,而是由男人們在去公共澡堂(hammam)前把陶罐送到澡堂的鍋爐間,埋進燒了一整天的餘燼裡,靠著緩慢的餘溫燉上四到六個小時。肉燉到用手一撥就化開,混著醃檸檬、大蒜、孜然與番紅花的油脂香氣滲進每一絲纖維,90迪拉姆一份,配著薄餅直接用手撕著吃,是我整趟旅程吃過最溫柔、也最耗時間的一道菜。

那晚走回里亞德的路上,麥地那的巷子只剩零星燈籠的光,遠處還傳來收攤的推車輪聲。我忽然明白司機第一天說的那句話——馬拉喀什真的是要用鼻子和舌頭去認識的城市,市集裡的番紅花、染坊裡的薄荷葉、廣場上的炭火香、法蒂瑪家餐桌上的薄荷茶泡沫,拼起來才是這座紅城真正的地圖,一張永遠不會印在任何旅遊手冊上的地圖。

ENGLISH

The evening I landed in Marrakech, the taxi driver rolled down his window and let in a gust of charcoal smoke, cumin, and dust. 'Welcome to the Red City,' he grinned, 'here you smell everything before you understand it.' I didn't fully get what he meant until five days later, when I realized I'd mapped this city almost entirely through my nose and tongue. This isn't a checklist of must-see sights. It's the trail I followed through spice stalls, tannery pits, and a Friday family table where a woman named Fatima taught me how tea should really be poured.

Before Dawn at Jemaa el-Fna, While the City Still Sleeps

At 5:40am the call to prayer from the Koutoubia minaret pulled me out of bed at the riad. I stepped into the medina's alleys still cool with night air, cats curled on doorsteps too unbothered to open an eye at me. Cutting through a lane still faintly smelling of last night's orange peels, I reached Jemaa el-Fna at 6:05am. The square that roars into chaos by afternoon was, at this hour, almost unrecognizable. An old man swept sunflower shells and sugarcane pulp with a palm-frond broom, slow and rhythmic, like a private ritual. Juice carts sat empty, dew still beaded on their plastic sheets.

Then a baker rolled past pushing a wooden cart stacked with unbaked dough rounds, each pressed with a different family mark. That's how I first learned about the ferrane, the neighborhood communal oven: families knead dough at home, stamp it with their own thumbprint or wooden tool, and send it to be baked for a small fee. No loaf ever gets mixed up, because every one carries a signature. It struck me then that in Marrakech, bread was never a private matter — it was communal from the very first knead.

In this city, even a loaf of bread carries its family's signature.

Into the Souk Maze: Hassan and His Saffron

Breakfast on the riad's rooftop was msemen, a folded flatbread dipped in amlou — an almond and argan oil paste — with honey and syrupy mint tea, fuel for what came next. Walking into Souk Semmarine around 8am, the reed-mat ceiling sliced sunlight into thin gold stripes across mountains of leather slippers, brass lamps, and rugs. The air was thick with tanning chemicals, cumin, motorbike exhaust, and mint, and the calls of vendors came from every direction — I was practically swept along by the current of the crowd.

Deeper in, at Rahba Kedima square, I stopped at a spice stall hung with dozens of burlap sacks. The vendor, Hassan, greeted me with a cheeky 'ni hao' that made me laugh. He pressed a pinch of saffron into my palm and told me to smell it — real saffron carries a faint hay-and-honey note, not the flat mustiness of dyed imitations. His family had sold spices for three generations, tracing back to his grandfather who carried wild herbs down from the Atlas Mountains. Weighing out fifty grams, he explained that a proper ras el hanout blend can contain up to thirty-five ingredients — cinnamon, turmeric, dried rose petals, even a trace of grains of paradise. I opened at 120 dirhams; he countered with 350. We settled, over two glasses of tea he insisted on pouring, at 180 dirhams. Hassan laughed and said that was 'the right price' — bargaining here isn't hostility, it's how strangers get acquainted, and skipping it would almost be rude.

How I Actually Got There: A Morning at the Bab Debbagh Tanneries

After the spice stall, I set off for the tanneries near Bab Debbagh, the ones you've probably seen in photos as circles of colored dye pits. From Jemaa el-Fna I flagged a petit taxi — no meter, we agreed on 15 dirhams — and rode about ten minutes through progressively narrower, quieter alleys until we reached an old city gate. Walking is also an option, about twenty-five minutes, passing through Souk Btana where raw hides and tanning tools are sold; the smell only intensifies along that route.

I arrived at 9:40am, and a young man named Youssef immediately offered a 'free' tour. Having read up beforehand, I knew these tours usually run on leather-shop commissions, so I asked directly whether declining to buy anything afterward would be a problem. He agreed without hesitation. We climbed a narrow, slick staircase to a rooftop terrace overlooking the pits, and he handed me sprigs of mint to hold under my nose — the tanneries use pigeon droppings and lime to soften hides, and the smell hit hard enough that I finally understood the mint's purpose. Below, dozens of round stone pits looked like an enormous palette: white pits for softening, then browns, reds, and yellows corresponding to saffron, poppy, and indigo dyes. Workers stood knee-deep in dye without any protective gear, treading hides by foot. Youssef told me the craft dates to the eleventh century and has passed down through families ever since. I stayed about forty-five minutes, photographing and asking questions, then tipped him 50 dirhams on the way down despite his 'free tour' claim — customary courtesy. He led me, as expected, into a leather shop where a wallet marked at 300 dirhams was talked down to 120. All in, the taxi, tip, and wallet cost roughly 200 dirhams over two hours — worth doing in the cool of the morning, since midday heat makes the smell considerably worse, and worth wearing shoes you don't mind getting dirty.

Feasting After Dark: Stall 31 and a Bowl of Snail Soup

By 6:30pm I was back at Jemaa el-Fna, and the square had completely changed character. The setting sun turned the mosque minaret honey-gold, smoke rose from a hundred grills at once, and drums, flutes, and vendor calls blurred into one continuous hum. Snake charmers packed up their baskets while henna women grabbed at passing wrists. I skipped photographing the chained monkeys — their tired eyes made it hard to feel good about participating. Weaving through the crowd, I found Stall 31, 'Chez Aicha,' recommended by a local friend who swore the owners hadn't changed in thirty years.

I ordered a mixed grill for 60 dirhams — cumin-heavy kefta meatballs and slightly spicy merguez sausage, scooped up with hot khobz flatbread and a smear of harissa. The meat was smoky and charred outside, coarse and juicy within, more satisfying than any refined dinner I'd had at the riad. Afterward I sat at a cart selling babbouche, snail soup, for 10 dirhams a bowl — a fragrant broth of anise, cumin, and thyme, with tiny snails picked out one by one with a toothpick, the broth drunk straight from the bowl at the end. A regular named Omar, sitting beside me, told me locals believe this soup aids digestion and often order it after a heavy meal. He also mentioned that the square's food stalls are recognized by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage — each one a family business passed down for generations, and whose grill is truly the best remains an argument locals never tire of, not unlike Neapolitans debating pizza.

After dark, this square isn't a market anymore — it's an entire city's open-air dinner table.

A Riad Dinner and Friday Couscous: Cooking Measured in Hours

The next day was Friday, and Fatima, who ran the riad, was busy from early morning. She invited me to stay for the Friday lunch, an almost unbreakable tradition in Moroccan households. After midday prayers at the mosque, the family gathered around a single enormous platter of couscous topped with seven vegetables and braised lamb. Everyone ate with their right hand from the same dish, children gently corrected on their technique — less a meal than a weekly ritual of family recalibration. Fatima's mother called Friday couscous 'God's gift day'; bakeries and butcher shops around the medina had been busy since dawn preparing for it.

After lunch she poured mint tea in the most deliberate way I'd ever watched — lifting the pot at least thirty centimeters above the glass, the stream arcing down to raise a fine layer of foam. She told me a Berber saying about the three glasses one is always offered: the first bitter like life, the second sweet like love, the third gentle like death. That evening I went out again, this time to a small family-run restaurant near the Mouassine Mosque, and ordered Marrakech's signature dish, tanjia — meat slow-cooked in a clay urn. Traditionally, it isn't cooked at home at all; men drop the sealed urn off at the neighborhood hammam's furnace room before bathing, burying it in the embers that have been burning all day, letting it cook for four to six hours on residual heat. The meat fell apart at a touch, infused with preserved lemon, garlic, cumin, and saffron-scented fat, at 90 dirhams a plate, eaten by hand with torn flatbread. It was the gentlest, slowest dish of my entire trip.

Walking back to the riad that night, the medina's alleys were lit only by scattered lanterns, the wheels of a last food cart creaking somewhere in the distance. I finally understood what the taxi driver had told me on my first evening — Marrakech really is a city you have to know through your nose and your tongue. The saffron in the souk, the mint held against my face at the tannery, the charcoal smoke over the square, the foam on Fatima's tea: together they form the real map of this red city, the one no guidebook ever prints.
