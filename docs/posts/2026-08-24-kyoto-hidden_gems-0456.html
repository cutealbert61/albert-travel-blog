中文

清晨五點五十分,鬧鐘在京都御所北側一間老町家民宿裡震動。木頭地板的涼意透過襪子傳上來,窗外還是一片墨藍色。三年前我第一次來京都,在清水寺的參道上被人潮推著走,連好好看一眼三重塔的時間都沒有,那時候我就跟自己說,下次一定要用不一樣的方式認識這座城市。這一次,我把行程表丟掉,只留下幾個當地朋友私下告訴我的地名,決定用五天的時間,走進京都人真正生活的巷弄。

京都的秘密從不張揚。它不會出現在車站大廳的巨幅海報上,而是藏在某條你以為走錯路的小徑盡頭,或是某位掃落葉的阿嬤隨口一句「這附近很少觀光客啦」裡頭。

清晨六點半:上賀茂社家町,比神社更安靜的水聲

從民宿騎共享單車到上賀茂神社,大約二十五分鐘,沿著北山通一路向北,晨霧還沒散,路上幾乎只有送報的機車與早起遛狗的老先生。上賀茂神社本身是世界遺產,但六點多抵達時,連售票的攝末社都還沒開門,整座神社只有我跟一位正在打掃細殿前立砂的神職人員。那兩座圓錐形的白砂「立砂」在晨光裡泛著淡淡的青灰色,據說模仿的是神明降臨的神山形狀,我站在那裡看了快十分鐘,才意識到自己竟然完全沒被打擾。

真正讓我駐足的,是神社東側那條沿著明神川而建的社家町。這裡曾是專門服侍上賀茂神社的神職人員世代居住的地方,至今仍有二十多戶保留著江戶到明治時期的老宅,每一戶門前都有一座小木橋跨過清澈見底的水渠,把自家庭院與河水連在一起。水聲潺潺,偶爾有錦鯉游過橋下,幾株老楓樹的枝葉垂到水面。我沿著這條不到三百公尺的巷子來回走了兩趟,途中遇到一位提著竹籃出門買菜的婦人,她笑著跟我點頭,用京都腔說了句「早いですね」(你起得真早呢),那語氣裡沒有觀光地慣有的職業性親切,反而像鄰居間隨口一句招呼,讓我突然覺得自己不再是個外人,而是這條巷子清晨風景的一部分。

京都的美從不在你抬頭尋找的地方,而在你低頭繞過水溝蓋、放慢腳步的瞬間。

一乘寺深山尋幽:詩仙堂與圓光寺的健行實記

八點半,我從出町柳站搭上叡山電鐵,前往一乘寺站,單程只要十分鐘,票價270日圓,車廂裡幾乎都是穿制服的高中生,沒有一個拖著行李箱的觀光客。這條路線我事先做了不少功課,因為一乘寺一帶的巷弄非常曲折,Google地圖常常會把你導到住宅區死巷裡。出站後我沿著詩仙堂通往東走,經過一段兩旁種滿樹籬的斜坡,大約走了十五分鐘,坡度漸陡,兩側開始出現竹林與土牆,直到看見詩仙堂那扇低矮的茅草屋頂山門。

詩仙堂是江戶初期武將石川丈山晚年隱居之處,九點開門,我是當天第二位入場的訪客,票價500日圓。踏進玄關脫鞋的那一刻,整間屋子安靜到我能聽見自己呼吸的聲音,走廊木地板被歲月磨得油亮,坐在緣側望向枯山水庭園,一株百年皋月杜鵑修剪成緩緩起伏的雲形,園中的添水(鹿威し)每隔幾分鐘發出一聲清脆的「叩」聲,那是整個空間裡唯一的聲響。我在緣側坐了將近四十分鐘,沒有任何人催促我離開。

接著我步行約五分鐘到圓光寺,這裡票價600日圓,園內有一片竹林小徑和一座名為「十牛之庭」的枯山水庭園,秋天楓葉會染紅整片山坡,但我去的是六月初,滿眼新綠反而更顯清幽。爬上寺後方的小丘還能俯瞰京都市區,視野開闊卻幾乎沒有其他遊客,我遇到一位獨自寫生的老先生,他告訴我他每個月都會來這裡畫上一整個下午,「這裡比嵐山安靜多了,而且不用人擠人」。整段一乘寺健行,從出站到走完兩座寺院,大約花了兩個半小時,交通加門票總花費不到1500日圓,卻是整趟旅程中我覺得最奢侈的兩個半小時,因為奢侈的不是金錢,而是完全屬於自己的時間與寂靜。

午後暖胃:出町ふたば的豆餅與一乘寺拉麵街的高安

中午回到出町柳,我特地繞去排隊已久的出町ふたば買豆餅。這家創業超過一百年的和菓子店,下午一點左右排隊人潮已經繞過轉角,但仔細看會發現排隊的九成是拿著購物袋的當地媽媽與上班族,而不是舉著相機的觀光客。我排了約二十分鐘,買了兩顆招牌豆餅,一顆220日圓。豆餅外皮是帶著淡淡鹹味的艾草麻糬,咬下去先是黑豆的顆粒感,鹹香的豆子與內餡紅豆沙的甜味在嘴裡拉扯出微妙的平衡,麻糬本身還帶著現做的溫熱與彈牙口感,完全不像一般便利商店賣的那種偏硬的年糕。我站在鴨川三角洲的跳烏龜石頭上邊吃邊看,幾個大學生光著腳踩在淺水裡聊天,一隻黑鳶低空盤旋想搶走路人手上的食物,那一刻的鴨川,跟嵐山渡月橋前擠得水泄不通的畫面完全是兩個世界。

晚一點肚子真正餓的時候,我走回一乘寺車站附近的拉麵橫丁,選了在地人口耳相傳的「らーめん高安」。這裡的九条葱系拉麵是招牌,850日圓一碗,湯頭是濃厚的豚骨基底,表面鋪滿滿滿一大把切得極細的九条葱,一入口先是葱的辛香衝上鼻腔,接著豚骨湯的濃郁厚度包住舌尖,叉燒厚實但不油膩,麵條是偏硬的直麵,吸附湯汁恰到好處。我隔壁坐著一位下班的上班族,西裝外套隨手掛在椅背,吃得滿頭大汗還不停加桌上的辣油,老闆娘用關西腔跟熟客閒聊今天的天氣,那種市井小店的煙火氣,跟京都車站樓上拉麵小路裡整齊劃一的連鎖店完全不同。

真正的京都美食,不在排隊四小時的名店,而在一碗連鎖店永遠複製不出來的、屬於巷弄的湯頭裡。

東福寺塔頭光明院:枯山水前的獨處時光

下午兩點,我特地避開東福寺本院的通天橋人潮,直接前往寺內東南側一處鮮少被寫進旅遊書的塔頭——光明院。這裡入場採自由捐獻,寺方擺了一個小木箱,建議金額300日圓,沒有任何工作人員盯著訪客。院內的枯山水庭園「波心之庭」出自昭和時期造園大師重森三玲之手,以白砂波紋象徵大海,佇立其中的青石象徵浮於海上的仙島,午後的陽光斜斜穿過紅葉與松樹枝葉,在白砂上灑下細碎光影。我脫鞋坐在本堂緣側,整整半小時裡只有另外兩位訪客悄悄進來又悄悄離開,沒有人交談,只有風吹過松針的沙沙聲與遠處隱約傳來的東福寺鐘聲。這種被稱為「虹之苔寺」的地方,秋天楓紅時據說也會湧入人潮,但六月的午後,我幾乎獨佔了整座庭園,連呼吸都不自覺放輕放慢。

伏見酒藏町的黃昏與先斗町的地藏盆:節慶裡的人情味

傍晚五點,我搭京阪電車到中書島站,步行進入伏見的酒藏町。這裡完全沒有清水坂那種紀念品店的喧鬧,只有白牆黑瓦的酒藏一間接著一間,空氣中飄著淡淡的米麴發酵香,混著護城河運河邊青苔的濕氣。我走進月桂冠大倉記念館,參觀費用600日圓,結束後可以試喝三小杯不同年份的清酒,館員是位六十多歲的釀酒師傅,他跟我解釋伏見之所以自古就是釀酒重鎮,是因為地下水質特別柔軟,「京都御所喝的水跟伏見的水,是同一條地下水脈」,他說這句話時眼神裡有種說不出的驕傲。我沿著十石舟航行的濠川散步,夕陽把水面染成橘紅色,幾艘載著遊客的小船緩緩划過,船夫用悠長的歌謠調子介紹兩岸的酒藏歷史,聲音在傍晚的水面上迴盪,那畫面比任何明信片都更接近我心中「京都黃昏」的樣子。

晚上七點半,我搭電車回市區,穿過先斗町狹窄的石板小巷準備找晚餐,卻在木屋町一條不起眼的住宅巷口撞見一場地藏盆。八月下旬的京都,幾乎每個町內會都會為守護孩童的地藏菩薩石像舉辦這場只屬於當地人的小型祭典,巷口搭起紅白帳篷,地藏像被重新描上鮮豔的臉部彩繪,供桌上擺滿西瓜與素麵,一群孩子圍著玩傳統的「福笑い」遊戲。一位負責張羅的阿姨看我站在旁邊探頭探腦,笑著遞給我一碗冰鎮素麵,「吃吧,拜過地藏さん的,吃了會平安」。她告訴我這個祭典完全不對觀光客宣傳,「因為這是我們町內自己的事」,那種毫無保留的邀請,讓我第一次覺得自己不是被允許旁觀的過客,而是被暫時收留進這條巷子的一夜居民。夜深了,提燈的光暈在石板路上搖晃,遠處傳來三味線隱約的音色,我站在那碗還冒著涼氣的素麵前,忽然明白這趟旅程真正找到的,不是某個景點,而是一種被允許靠近的信任。

回程的路上,京都的夜風帶著河水與線香混合的氣味,我沒有再打開地圖,只是憑著白天走過的方向感慢慢走回民宿。這五天裡我沒有踏進清水寺,也沒去金閣寺排隊拍照,但我卻覺得自己比任何一次都更靠近京都——不是作為觀光客,而是作為一個被這座城市暫時收留的旅人。

ENGLISH

My alarm buzzed at 5:50 a.m. inside a century-old machiya guesthouse just north of the Kyoto Imperial Palace. Cold seeped up through the wooden floorboards into my socks, and outside the window the sky was still ink blue. Three years earlier, on my first trip to Kyoto, I was shoved along the approach to Kiyomizu-dera by a river of tourists, unable to even glance properly at the three-story pagoda. I promised myself then that next time would be different. This time I threw away my itinerary and kept only a handful of place names whispered to me by Kyoto friends, determined to spend five days walking through the alleys where Kyoto people actually live.

Kyoto never advertises its secrets. You won't find them on the giant posters in the station concourse. They wait at the end of a path you think you've taken wrong, or in an offhand remark from an elderly woman sweeping fallen leaves: 'Not many tourists come around here.'

6:30 a.m.: The Priests' Quarter of Kamigamo, Where the Water Is Louder Than the Shrine

I rented a shared bicycle and pedaled roughly twenty-five minutes north along Kitayama-dori toward Kamigamo Shrine. Morning mist hadn't yet lifted; the only company on the road was a newspaper delivery scooter and an old man walking his dog. Kamigamo Shrine is a UNESCO World Heritage site, but arriving just after six, even the subsidiary shrine selling amulets hadn't opened. I shared the entire complex with a single shrine priest sweeping around the tatezuna, the two cone-shaped mounds of white sand said to represent the sacred mountain where the gods descend. In the pale morning light they glowed a faint bluish grey. I stood watching for nearly ten minutes before realizing, with quiet surprise, that no one had disturbed me once.

What truly stopped me, though, was Shake-machi, the quarter east of the shrine where hereditary Shinto priest families have lived for generations along the Myoshin River. More than twenty houses here still stand from the Edo and Meiji periods, each with its own small wooden bridge crossing the clear, shallow canal that connects the courtyards to the flowing water. Koi drifted lazily beneath the bridges, and old maple branches dipped toward the surface. I walked the length of this three-hundred-meter lane twice, and along the way passed a woman heading out with a bamboo basket for groceries. She smiled, nodded, and said in a soft Kyoto accent, 'You're up early,' with none of the practiced warmth of a tourist-facing shopkeeper — just a neighbor's casual greeting. For a moment I stopped feeling like an outsider and felt, instead, like part of that lane's quiet morning scenery.

Kyoto's beauty never waits where you crane your neck to look for it — it waits in the moment you slow down to step around a drainage grate.

Deep in Ichijoji: A Real Hiking Log Through Shisen-do and Enko-ji

At 8:30 I boarded the Eizan Electric Railway from Demachiyanagi to Ichijoji Station — a ten-minute ride for 270 yen, the carriage full of uniformed high schoolers and not a single rolling suitcase. I'd done some homework beforehand, because the backstreets around Ichijoji twist unpredictably and Google Maps loves routing you into dead-end residential cul-de-sacs. From the station I walked east along the road toward Shisen-do, past a hedge-lined slope that grew steadily steeper. After about fifteen minutes, bamboo groves and earthen walls began appearing on either side, until finally the low thatched gate of Shisen-do came into view.

Shisen-do was the retirement retreat of the early Edo warrior-scholar Ishikawa Jozan. It opens at nine, and I was the second visitor that morning, paying the 500-yen entry fee. The moment I stepped in and removed my shoes, the house was so quiet I could hear my own breathing. The wooden corridor floors, polished smooth by centuries of use, led to a veranda overlooking a dry landscape garden where a hundred-year-old azalea bush had been trimmed into gentle, cloud-like curves. Every few minutes a shishi-odoshi bamboo water spout released a single sharp knock — the only sound in the entire space. I sat on that veranda for nearly forty minutes, and no one ever hinted it was time to move on.

A five-minute walk brought me to Enko-ji, entry 600 yen, home to a small bamboo path and a dry garden called the Garden of Ten Bulls. In autumn the surrounding hillside apparently turns a deep crimson, but I visited in early June, when fresh green leaves gave the place an even more tranquil hush. Climbing the small hill behind the temple offered a sweeping view of the city below, with almost no other visitors in sight. I met an elderly man sketching alone, who told me he comes here every month to draw for an entire afternoon: 'It's much quieter than Arashiyama, and you don't have to fight the crowds.' The whole Ichijoji hike, from stepping off the train to finishing both temples, took about two and a half hours, and transport plus admission cost less than 1,500 yen — yet it was the most luxurious stretch of the entire trip, because the luxury wasn't money, it was silence entirely my own.

Midday Comfort: Mame-mochi at Demachi Futaba and Ramen at Takayasu in Ichijoji

Back in Demachiyanagi around noon, I detoured to join the perpetual line at Demachi Futaba, a wagashi shop over a century old. By one o'clock the queue curled around the corner, but a closer look revealed that nine out of ten people waiting were local mothers with shopping bags and office workers, not tourists with cameras. After roughly twenty minutes I bought two of the signature mame-mochi, 220 yen each. The outer layer is mugwort mochi with a faint saltiness; the first bite delivers the texture of whole black beans, their savory saltiness pulling against the sweetness of the red bean paste inside, while the mochi itself was still warm and springy from being freshly made — nothing like the firm, refrigerated version sold at convenience stores. I ate mine sitting on the turtle-shaped stepping stones of the Kamogawa Delta, watching university students dangle their bare feet in the shallow water and a black kite circle low, hunting for an unattended snack. That version of the Kamo River felt like an entirely different world from the shoulder-to-shoulder crush at Arashiyama's Togetsukyo Bridge.

By evening, once real hunger set in, I walked back toward the ramen alley near Ichijoji Station and chose Ramen Takayasu, a spot passed along strictly by word of mouth among locals. Their signature Kujo-negi ramen costs 850 yen: a rich tonkotsu broth piled high with finely sliced Kujo green onion. The first sip hits your nose with sharp onion aroma before the thick pork-bone broth coats your tongue; the chashu was thick-cut but not greasy, and the firm straight noodles carried just the right amount of broth. The salaryman next to me had draped his suit jacket over his chair, sweating as he kept adding chili oil, while the owner chatted in Kansai dialect with regulars about the day's weather. That kind of unpretentious, lived-in warmth felt worlds apart from the tidy chain shops in Kyoto Station's Ramen Koji food court.

Kyoto's real food culture doesn't live in the four-hour queue for a famous name — it lives in a bowl of broth that no chain restaurant could ever replicate.

Komyo-in at Tofuku-ji: An Afternoon Alone Before the Dry Garden

At two in the afternoon I deliberately skipped the crowds on Tofuku-ji's famous Tsutenkyo Bridge and headed instead to Komyo-in, a sub-temple in the southeast corner that rarely appears in guidebooks. Admission runs on an honesty system — a small wooden donation box suggests 300 yen, with no staff watching over visitors. The dry landscape garden inside, Hashin-no-niwa, was designed by the Showa-era garden master Mirei Shigemori: rippling white sand represents the sea, while upright blue-grey stones stand in for islands floating upon it. Afternoon sunlight slanted through maple and pine branches, scattering fragments of light across the sand. I sat shoeless on the main hall's veranda for a full half hour, during which only two other visitors slipped quietly in and out without a word exchanged — just wind through pine needles and, faintly, a temple bell somewhere in the distance. Known locally as the 'moss temple of the rainbow,' this place apparently fills with visitors when the maples turn red in autumn, but on this June afternoon I had the entire garden nearly to myself, and found myself unconsciously slowing even my breath.

Dusk in Fushimi's Sake District and a Jizo Festival in Pontocho: The Warmth Behind a Local Ritual

At five in the evening I took the Keihan Railway to Chushojima Station and walked into Fushimi's historic sake brewing district. There was none of the souvenir-shop clamor of Kiyomizu-zaka here — just white-walled, black-tiled breweries lined up one after another, the air carrying a faint scent of fermenting rice koji mixed with the mossy dampness of the old canal. I stepped into the Gekkeikan Okura Sake Museum, paid the 600-yen admission, and afterward sampled three small cups of sake from different vintages. The staff member pouring them, a brewer in his sixties, explained that Fushimi has been a brewing center for centuries because of its exceptionally soft groundwater: 'The water the Imperial Palace drinks and the water here in Fushimi come from the same underground vein,' he said, a note of unmistakable pride in his voice. I strolled along the canal where the jikkoku-bune sightseeing boats drift, the setting sun turning the water orange, a boatman's long, drawn-out song narrating the breweries' history echoing across the surface. It was closer to the Kyoto twilight I'd always imagined than any postcard I'd seen.

By 7:30 I rode the train back into the city and cut through Pontocho's narrow stone-paved alley in search of dinner, when I stumbled, quite by accident, onto a Jizo-bon festival in a nondescript residential lane off Kiyamachi. In late August, nearly every neighborhood association in Kyoto holds this small, purely local festival to honor the stone Jizo statues that watch over children. A red-and-white tent had gone up at the corner; the Jizo statue's face had been freshly repainted in bright colors; a table was piled with watermelon and cold somen noodles, while a cluster of children played the traditional blindfolded face-drawing game, fukuwarai. A woman helping organize the event caught me hovering curiously nearby and, laughing, handed me a bowl of chilled somen. 'Eat up — anything offered to Jizo-san brings good luck,' she said. She told me the festival is never advertised to tourists: 'This belongs to our neighborhood alone.' That unguarded invitation made me feel, for the first time on the trip, not like a tolerated onlooker but like a temporary resident of that alley for one night. As it grew dark, paper lanterns swayed shadows across the stone path and the faint sound of a shamisen drifted from somewhere nearby. Standing there with a bowl of still-cool noodles in hand, I understood that what this trip had really given me wasn't a landmark — it was a fleeting kind of trust.

Walking back that night, the wind off the river carried a mix of incense and water. I didn't open my map once, just followed the direction I'd memorized during the day back to the guesthouse. In five days I never once set foot inside Kiyomizu-dera, and I never queued for a photo at Kinkaku-ji. And yet I felt closer to Kyoto than I ever had before — not as a tourist passing through, but as a traveler the city had quietly, briefly, taken in.
