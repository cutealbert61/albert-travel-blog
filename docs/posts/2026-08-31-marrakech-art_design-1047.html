中文

飛機降落在馬拉喀什的明納哈機場(Aéroport Marrakech Ménara)時是傍晚六點,機艙門一開,熱風裡混著柴油與塵土的氣味撲面而來,底下還藏著一絲若有似無的橙花香——後來我才知道,那是機場周邊橙樹林飄來的味道,當地人管它叫「城市的體香」。計程車司機用不太流利的法文跟我確認麥地那(Medina)裡的地址,車子一頭鑽進舊城窄巷,兩側牆面是統一的赭紅色,像整座城市剛從沙漠裡燒製出來,還帶著窯溫。那一刻我就知道,接下來這幾天,我要記錄的不會是一張行程清單,而是一座城市怎麼用顏色、線條與手工的痕跡,把自己活成一件立體的藝術品。

清晨的紅城與屋頂的幾何光影

清晨六點,我從利雅德(Riad)的房間爬起來,踩著冰涼的水磨石地板走上屋頂露台。宣禮聲從四面八方的喇叭裡疊起來,像一場沒有指揮的合唱,由遠而近再散開。天還沒全亮,屋頂連成一片起伏的土黃色地形,偶爾冒出一株棕櫚或一支衛星天線,提醒我這不是博物館裡的模型,而是幾十萬人真實生活的容器。我住的利雅德外牆樸素得幾乎像廢墟,推開厚重木門走進去,天井卻鋪滿手工切割的星形馬賽克磚(zellige),牆面是雕花石膏(gebs)與雪松木格窗(mashrabiya),光線從天井正中灑下,隨著太陽移動,幾何圖案的影子會整天在牆上緩慢旋轉。屋主告訴我,這種「外樸內華」的設計不是偶然,而是伊斯蘭建築裡對謙遜與私密的堅持——美應該留給家人與真主,不必炫耀給街道看。九點左右,我步行到巴希亞皇宮(Bahia Palace),十九世紀大臣為妻妾建造的宅邸,一百六十個房間圍繞著數個庭院展開,天花板用雪松木雕出蜂窩狀的穆卡納斯(muqarnas),層層堆疊卻找不到兩片完全相同的花紋。導覽員說,光是其中一間房間的彩繪天花板,就花了工匠六年才完成。站在庭院正中央抬頭看,那種近乎強迫症的重複與變化,讓我第一次真正理解「幾何即信仰」這句話的重量。

這座城市把最華麗的心思,都藏在最不起眼的牆後面。

迷宮深處的手藝課——本尤瑟夫學院與市集裡的設計小店

離開巴希亞皇宮後,我沿著里亞德濟頓街(Rue Riad Zitoun)往北走,鑽進賽瑪林市集(Souk Semmarine)的拱頂長廊,目標是本尤瑟夫古蘭經學院(Madrasa Ben Youssef)。老實說,路線圖在手機上完全派不上用場——巷弄窄到只容兩人錯身,攤販的銅盤、皮革、香料袋層層疊疊擠壓視線,我照著Google地圖走了兩次死巷,最後靠一位賣薄荷茶壺的老闆比手畫腳指路,才在大約走了二十五分鐘後找到那扇不起眼的木門。門票五十迪拉姆(約新台幣一百六十元),開放時間是早上九點到晚上六點,建議挑開門前十五分鐘到,因為十點過後會湧入整團整團的遊客,庭院裡拍照根本擠不出一個空景。進到中庭那一刻,我倒吸一口氣:整面牆從地面到天花板,是三種工藝的疊層——最下面是幾何馬賽克磚,中段是手刻石膏浮雕,最上層是雪松木格柵,三種材質、三種節奏的圖案疊在一起卻毫不衝突,工匠據說靠的是心算比例,沒有現代電腦輔助。地面是冰涼的大理石,館方會提醒訪客脫鞋進入部分區域,我後悔沒帶一雙薄襪,光腳踩在石材上,涼意一路竄到腦門,反而讓我看得更專注。中午時分,我沿著市集邊緣走到蘇克謝里菲亞(Souk Cherifia)——一棟不起眼的建築,樓梯又暗又窄,爬上三樓卻豁然開朗:一整層樓的天井被改造成設計選物店聚落,像Max&Jan、KifKif Boutique這樣的品牌,把柏柏爾傳統的布切爾維特地毯(boucherouite,用回收碎布手工編織)重新配色,做成靠墊與托特包;隔壁的皮匠鋪則用番紅花與石榴皮天然染出的皮革,縫成一雙雙軟拖鞋(babouche)。老闆哈金跟我說,他們這一代設計師的功課,就是把奶奶輩的手藝「翻譯」給不懂柏柏爾語的世界看。我在那裡買了一雙駝色皮拖鞋,折合台幣約六百元,店家用牛皮紙包好,還細心地在鞋底縫了一小塊防滑膠——這種對細節的堅持,讓我瞬間理解為什麼歐洲設計師這麼多年來一直往這座城市跑找靈感。

馬約爾花園與伊夫聖羅蘭博物館:一抹藍色如何定義一座城市的美學

隔天早上八點,我特地叫了一台小計程車(petit taxi,車資約三十迪拉姆)前往新城區蓋利茲(Gueliz),目標是馬約爾花園(Jardin Majorelle)。開門前排隊的人已經不少,但比起中午動輒繞花園半圈的人龍,清晨這班算是幸運。踏進花園大門,那種鈷藍色瞬間把我的注意力整個吸走——這是法國畫家雅克·馬約爾一九三七年親自調配的顏色,深得帶點紫,後人稱它「馬約爾藍」。牆面、噴水池邊緣、巨大的陶罐,全部漆成同一種藍,和滿園仙人掌、竹林、荷花池的綠形成近乎粗暴卻極其和諧的對比。一九八〇年代,這座花園差點被財團買下改建成飯店,是設計師伊夫·聖羅蘭與伴侶皮耶·貝爾傑買下才保存至今,園區深處後來也蓋了一座柏柏爾博物館,展出項鍊、地毯與陶器上重複出現的菱形、鋸齒紋樣。走出花園,隔壁就是二〇一七年開幕的伊夫聖羅蘭博物館(Musée Yves Saint Laurent Marrakech),門票聯票約一百五十迪拉姆。館內展出他上千張手稿與經典禮服,策展文字直白地寫著:聖羅蘭一九六六年第一次來到馬拉喀什,被市集裡番紅花黃、石榴紅、天空藍毫無忌諱疊在一起的用色方式震撼,從此那種「不怕衝突」的配色邏輯,滲進了他往後三十年的高訂系列。站在一件一九八九年的絲質長袍前,導覽牌指出領口刺繡的靈感直接來自傑馬夫納廣場賣香料婦女頭巾的織紋——那一刻我忽然覺得,整座城市其實一直在被巴黎時裝週偷偷致敬,只是很少人回頭來看它本人。

馬拉喀什教會我的第一件事:顏色不必和諧,只需要夠誠實。

屋頂上的塔吉與街角的哈里拉湯——馬拉喀什的滋味地圖

逛完蘇克謝里菲亞那天傍晚,我又爬回同一棟樓,這次是為了吃飯——三樓的Nomad餐廳有一片正對拉巴凱迪馬廣場(Rahba Kedima)屋頂群的露台座位,傍晚六點半入座剛好接上日落。我點了燉梅子杏仁的羊肉塔吉(tagine d'agneau aux pruneaux),陶鍋一端上桌先聞到肉桂與孜然混合的暖香,掀蓋那瞬間蒸氣裹著焦糖化的梅子甜味撲出來,羊肉燉到用叉子輕輕一撥就骨肉分離,搭配的庫斯庫斯吸飽了肉汁,吃起來一點都不乾澀,一份約一百四十迪拉姆(約新台幣四百五十元),配一杯薄荷茶,坐在那裡看夕陽把整片赭紅色屋頂染成粉橘色,老實說值回票價。隔天中午,我改在傑馬夫納廣場邊緣一個編號攤位吃平民版本——一位叫哈山的大叔顧的哈里拉湯(harira)攤子,湯底是番茄、扁豆、鷹嘴豆和一點點麵粉勾芡,喝起來濃稠帶酸,底層藏著肉桂和薑的暖意,一碗才十五迪拉姆(約新台幣五十元),配一份手撕薄餅姆斯曼(msemen)沾蜂蜜,油香混著麥香,吃完整個人從胃暖到指尖。廣場另一頭還有專賣蝸牛湯(babbouche)的攤子,湯底用十幾種香料熬煮,老闆會用牙籤幫你把蝸牛肉挑出殼,湯本身帶著八角與孜然的濃烈氣味,第一口有點嚇到我,但喝到第三口就上癮,一碗大約八迪拉姆。廣場邊的鮮榨柳橙汁攤位永遠排著人,一杯只要四迪拉姆(約新台幣十三元),酸甜到讓人瞬間清醒,我後來幾乎每天下午都會回去喝一杯,順便看攤販之間用達里賈方言(Darija)互相鬥嘴喊價,那種市井的生猛,比任何米其林餐廳都更接近這座城市真正的味道。

傑馬夫納廣場的夜——說書人、格納瓦樂手與地毯裡的柏柏爾密碼

晚上八點過後的傑馬夫納廣場(Jemaa el-Fnaa)是另一個世界。白天賣果汁與蝸牛湯的攤位收攏,取而代之的是上百盞裸露燈泡下冒著油煙的烤肉攤,煙霧混著孜然味瀰漫整個廣場,像一層會流動的濾鏡。我沿著人群邊緣走,看見一圈圈人席地圍坐,中間是說書人(halqa),用阿拉伯語比手畫腳講著什麼故事,聽眾時而哄笑時而屏息,雖然我一句話都聽不懂,卻能感覺到那種口述傳統延續了幾百年的節奏感——聯合國教科文組織二〇〇一年把這個廣場的口頭文化列為「人類口述與非物質遺產」,此刻終於明白為什麼。再往前是格納瓦(Gnaoua)樂手,穿著綴滿貝殼的長袍,手裡三弦琴(guembri)彈出低沉重複的音線,腳踝的鐵鈴隨節奏晃動,那種源自撒哈拉以南、混合了宗教儀式與奴隸歷史記憶的音樂,現在每年六月會在海濱城市索維拉辦成盛大的格納瓦世界音樂節,但廣場上這種即興版本,更貼近它原本作為驅邪儀式音樂的樣子。我在市集裡的一間地毯店坐下喝茶,老闆尤瑟夫攤開一張又一張手工地毯跟我解釋圖騰:菱形代表生育與女性子宮,鋸齒代表河流與水源,三角形疊起來是山脈,他說每一位柏柏爾織工其實都在毯子上寫一封只有自己部落看得懂的信。他也提醒我,進入傳統家庭前最好先問候「Salam alaikum」,拍攝穿傳統服飾的婦女前一定要先徵得同意,週五中午整座城市會為了主麻日聚禮而放慢下來,許多家庭那天中午固定吃庫斯庫斯,是一週裡最鄭重的一餐。離開店裡時已近午夜,廣場上的人潮不減反增,我站在原地看著整個廣場像一顆持續跳動的心臟,忽然覺得,馬拉喀什的美學從來不在博物館的玻璃櫃裡,而在這種喧鬧、混雜、毫不修飾的日常裡。

離開的前一晚,我又爬回利雅德的屋頂,城市的燈火比星星密。我想起這幾天走過的每一面牆、每一塊磁磚、每一碗湯,忽然懂了為什麼這座城市能讓聖羅蘭念念不忘三十年——它從不追求乾淨的美,而是把矛盾、重複與手工的瑕疵,通通留在表面,任人觸摸。

ENGLISH

My plane touched down at Marrakech Menara Airport at six in the evening, and the moment the cabin door opened, hot air rushed in carrying diesel fumes, dust, and underneath it all, a faint trace of orange blossom drifting from the groves that ring the airport — locals call it the city's own perfume. The taxi driver confirmed my riad's address in halting French, then plunged into the medina's narrow lanes, walls the same unbroken ochre red on every side, as if the whole city had just been fired in a kiln and hadn't cooled yet. I knew right away that what I'd be writing over the next few days wouldn't be an itinerary. It would be an account of how a city turns color, line, and the visible trace of human hands into a living, breathing work of art.

Dawn Over the Red City: Geometry in Morning Light

At six the next morning I climbed out of bed in my riad and up cold terrazzo stairs to the rooftop terrace. The call to prayer rose from loudspeakers scattered across the medina, overlapping into an unconducted chorus that swelled and receded. The rooftops stretched out below in undulating ochre, punctuated by the odd palm or satellite dish — a reminder that this wasn't a museum diorama but the actual container for hundreds of thousands of real lives. My riad's street facade was almost aggressively plain, nearly derelict-looking, but stepping through the heavy wooden door revealed a courtyard paved in hand-cut zellige mosaic stars, walls carved with gebs plasterwork, and cedar mashrabiya screens filtering light that crept slowly across the floor all day, rotating its geometric shadows like a clock. My host explained that this outside-plain, inside-lavish logic wasn't accidental — Islamic domestic architecture treats beauty as something reserved for family and God, not for the street to gawk at. Around nine I walked to the Bahia Palace, a nineteenth-century vizier's residence with one hundred and sixty rooms wrapped around a series of courtyards. The cedar ceilings are carved into honeycombed muqarnas, stacked in layers where no two clusters repeat exactly; my guide told me one painted ceiling alone took craftsmen six years to finish. Standing in the courtyard, neck craned upward at that almost obsessive repetition-with-variation, I finally understood, viscerally, what people mean when they say geometry is a form of devotion here.

This city hides its most extravagant thinking behind its plainest walls.

A Craft Lesson in the Maze: Ben Youssef Madrasa and the Design Shops of the Souk

After the palace I walked north along Rue Riad Zitoun, cutting into the vaulted alleys of Souk Semmarine, aiming for the Ben Youssef Madrasa. My phone map was useless — the lanes are barely wide enough for two people to pass, and stalls stacked with copper trays, leather goods, and spice sacks swallow up any clear sightline. I hit two dead ends before a mint-teapot vendor pointed me the right way with hand gestures alone; the whole walk took about twenty-five minutes. Entry was fifty dirhams (roughly five US dollars), open nine to six, and I'd recommend arriving right at opening, because by ten the tour groups arrive in waves and you can't get a clear shot of the courtyard. Stepping inside, I actually gasped: the walls run floor to ceiling through three layered crafts — geometric tilework at the base, hand-carved plaster relief above it, and cedar lattice screens on top, three materials and three rhythms of pattern stacked without ever clashing, all proportioned, according to the guide, by craftsmen working from memorized ratios rather than modern tools. The marble floor was freezing underfoot in the shoes-off sections; I regretted not bringing socks, though the chill kept me sharply focused. By midday I'd wandered to Souk Cherifia, an unassuming building with a dim, narrow staircase that opens onto a rooftop cluster of concept stores. Labels like Max & Jan and KifKif Boutique take traditional boucherouite rugs — woven from recycled fabric scraps by Berber women — and repurpose them into cushions and totes in fresh colorways; next door a leatherworker dyes hides with saffron and pomegranate skin, stitching them into soft babouche slippers. The shop owner, Hakim, told me his generation's job is to translate their grandmothers' handiwork for a world that doesn't speak Berber. I bought a pair of camel-colored slippers for about twenty US dollars, wrapped in brown paper, with a small non-slip patch stitched carefully into the sole — the kind of detail that explains why European designers keep coming back here for inspiration.

Majorelle Garden and the Yves Saint Laurent Museum: How One Blue Defined a City's Aesthetic

The next morning I took a petit taxi (about thirty dirhams) to the new town, Gueliz, aiming to arrive at Jardin Majorelle right at opening. The line was already forming, but nothing compared to the crowds that circle the garden by noon. Stepping through the gate, that cobalt blue hit me immediately — a deep, slightly purple shade mixed by the French painter Jacques Majorelle in 1937, later named Majorelle Blue. Walls, fountain edges, giant ceramic pots, all painted the same blue, set against a near-violent, oddly harmonious backdrop of cactus, bamboo, and lily ponds. In the 1980s the garden nearly fell to developers before designer Yves Saint Laurent and his partner Pierre Bergé bought and saved it; deeper in the grounds is now a small Berber museum displaying necklaces, rugs, and pottery patterned with repeating diamonds and zigzags. Next door, the Musée Yves Saint Laurent Marrakech (combined ticket around fifteen US dollars) holds a thousand-plus sketches and iconic gowns. The wall text states plainly that Saint Laurent's first visit to Marrakech in 1966 left him stunned by the way the souk mixed saffron yellow, pomegranate red, and sky blue without apology — and that fearless clash of color shaped his work for the next three decades. Standing before a 1989 silk robe, a placard noted the neckline embroidery was drawn directly from the headscarves worn by spice vendors in Jemaa el-Fnaa. I realized then that this whole city has quietly been paid homage to on Paris runways for years, while very few people ever came back to look at the source.

The first thing Marrakech taught me: color doesn't need to harmonize — it just needs to be honest.

Tagine on a Rooftop, Harira on the Corner: A Taste Map of Marrakech

The same evening I climbed back up to the third floor of Souk Cherifia, this time for dinner at Nomad, whose terrace overlooks the rooftops of Rahba Kedima spice square. I sat down at six thirty, timed just right to catch sunset, and ordered lamb tagine with prunes and almonds. The clay pot arrived releasing a warm haze of cinnamon and cumin, and lifting the lid sent up a wave of caramelized prune sweetness; the lamb fell apart at the touch of a fork, and the couscous beneath had soaked up enough pan juices to stay moist rather than dry. The dish ran about fifteen US dollars, paired with mint tea, and watching the sunset turn the ochre rooftops salmon-pink while I ate made it feel entirely worth it. The next day at noon I tried the everyday version at a numbered stall on the edge of Jemaa el-Fnaa, run by a man named Hassan — a bowl of harira, thickened with flour, tomato, lentils, and chickpeas, tasting rich and faintly sour with cinnamon and ginger hiding underneath, all for about a dollar fifty, served with msemen flatbread torn and dipped in honey, oil and wheat warming me from stomach to fingertips. Across the square, another stall specialized in babbouche, a snail soup simmered with over a dozen spices; the vendor picked the snails out of their shells for me with a toothpick, and the broth's heavy anise and cumin punch startled me on the first sip but hooked me by the third. Fresh orange juice stalls nearby always had a line — about forty cents a glass, sharp and sweet enough to jolt you awake — and I found myself going back most afternoons, watching vendors haggle in Darija dialect, a rawness far closer to the real texture of this city than any Michelin-starred room could offer.

Night in Jemaa el-Fnaa: Storytellers, Gnaoua Musicians, and the Code Hidden in Rugs

After eight in the evening, Jemaa el-Fnaa becomes an entirely different place. The daytime juice and snail-soup stalls fold up, replaced by hundreds of bare bulbs over smoking grills, the cumin-laced haze hanging over the square like a moving filter. I walked along the edge of the crowd and found circles of people sitting on the ground around storytellers, or halqa, gesturing dramatically in Arabic while the audience laughed or fell silent on cue. I didn't understand a word, but I could feel the rhythm of an oral tradition centuries deep — UNESCO listed this square's oral culture as Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2001, and standing there, it finally made sense why. Further along were Gnaoua musicians in shell-studded robes, plucking a low, repetitive line on a three-stringed guembri while iron anklets rattled with each step — music rooted in trance rituals carried north by enslaved West Africans generations ago, now also celebrated each June at the Gnaoua World Music Festival in Essaouira, though the improvised version here felt closer to its original purpose of driving out spirits. I sat for tea in a rug shop where the owner, Youssef, unrolled carpet after carpet to explain the symbols woven into them: diamonds for fertility and the womb, zigzags for rivers and water, stacked triangles for mountains — every weaver, he said, is writing a letter only her own tribe can fully read. He also mentioned some quiet local customs: greeting someone with 'Salam alaikum' before entering a traditional home, always asking before photographing women in traditional dress, and how the whole city slows down for Friday prayers, with many families sharing couscous together at midday, the week's most formal meal. It was nearly midnight when I left the shop, and the square, if anything, had grown busier, pulsing like something alive. Marrakech's aesthetic, I realized then, was never meant for a museum case — it lives in exactly this kind of loud, mixed, unpolished ordinary night.

On my last evening I climbed back up to the riad's rooftop one more time, the city's lights denser than the stars above it. Thinking back over every wall, every tile, every bowl of soup from the past few days, I finally understood why this city held Yves Saint Laurent's imagination for thirty years — it never chases a clean kind of beauty. It leaves the contradictions, the repetitions, and the visible flaws of human hands right there on the surface, for anyone willing to touch them.
