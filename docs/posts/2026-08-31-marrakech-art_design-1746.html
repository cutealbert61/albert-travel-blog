中文

飛機降落馬拉喀什門那拉機場的時候是深夜,但走出航廈那一刻,空氣裡有種乾燥的、混著塵土與茉莉花的氣味,像是這座城市在跟我打招呼。計程車司機用法語跟我確認riad的地址,車窗外的路燈把老城牆染成一種介於磚紅與土黃之間的顏色,當地人叫這種顏色「馬拉喀什紅」。我後來才知道,這不是浪漫的形容詞,而是1912年法國殖民時期就立法規定的城市色彩準則——全馬拉喀什的建築外牆,至今都得漆成這種赭紅色調。一座城市用法律規定自己的顏色,這件事讓我在抵達的第一晚就明白,美學在這裡從來不是裝飾,而是身份。

清晨六點:班尤素福古蘭經學院的光

我特意把鬧鐘設在清晨五點半,為的就是搶在旅行團湧入之前,獨自站在Ben Youssef Madrasa(班尤素福古蘭經學院)的中庭裡。六點整,大門剛開,售票處只有一個睡眼惺忪的老先生,門票七十迪拉姆(約新台幣220元)。走進去的瞬間,我停下腳步,因為陽光正好斜斜打進中庭,把雪松木雕、灰泥浮雕(gebs)與zellige彩色磁磚拼貼的幾何圖案,投影成一整片會呼吸的光影地毯。這座十六世紀的伊斯蘭學院曾經住過八百多名學生,窄小的宿舍房間至今保留著,但真正讓我駐足超過一個小時的,是中庭牆面上沒有一處重複的圖案——工匠們相信,唯有真主的創造是完美無限的,人類的手藝必須留下一絲不對稱,作為謙卑的記號。我蹲下來用手指觸摸那些磁磚接縫,粗糙帶著涼意,想像五百年前的匠人如何用石膏刀一刀刀刻出這片天花板的鐘乳石般垂花飾(muqarnas)。那一刻很安靜,只有鴿子在天井上方撲翅的聲音。

在這裡,對稱是一種信仰,而故意留下的不完美,才是真正的完成。

上午九點半:馬約爾花園與聖羅蘭博物館——實地怎麼玩

離開古蘭經學院,我沿著Rue Amsefah步行到大馬路上攔了一輛小黃計程車,跟司機喊了目的地「Jardin Majorelle」,車資談好35迪拉姆(未跳表車資需事先議定,這是我學到的第一課)。九點半抵達時,售票口外已經排了將近一百人——旺季建議提前在官網訂票,可以避開現場排隊至少四十分鐘。花園門票70迪拉姆,若想連同旁邊的Yves Saint Laurent Museum一起參觀,建議買套票,總價約230迪拉姆(換算約台幣750元)。走進花園的第一步,我整個人愣住:法國畫家Jacques Majorelle在1930年代設計的建築,漆成一種近乎螢光的鈷藍色,後人稱之為「馬約爾藍」,竹林、仙人掌與九重葛在藍色建築前形成強烈色塊對比,像是走進一幅立體派畫作。我花了將近兩小時在園區裡繞,竹林區有種潮濕的青草氣味,水池邊的睡蓮安安靜靜漂浮,偶爾有蜥蜴從石縫竄出。隔壁的聖羅蘭博物館是2017年才落成的新建築,清水模與陶土色磚牆交錯,裡面展出的高級訂製服手稿讓我駐足良久——原來聖羅蘭1966年設計的那件經典燕尾服「Le Smoking」,靈感正是來自他在馬拉喀什街頭看見的柏柏爾人袍子輪廓。整趟行程,包含來回計程車、雙館門票與在花園咖啡廳喝的薄荷茶,大約花費台幣1200元,時間抓上午九點到下午一點最剛好,人潮少、光線也最適合拍照。

正午:哈奇夫人的蝸牛湯與麥吉巷的烤羊

中午十二點半,我的肚子已經在抗議。我沒有直接殺去傑馬夫納廣場的大排檔,而是先繞到廣場邊緣一個不起眼的小攤,攤主是一位包著頭巾的老婦人,大家都叫她「哈奇媽媽」。她的攤子只賣一種東西:babbouche,摩洛哥蝸牛湯。湯頭是用超過十五種香料——茴香籽、薄荷、丁香、甘草根——熬煮超過六小時的湯底,顏色深褐近黑,喝一口先是溫潤的甘草甜,接著是薄荷的涼,最後喉頭泛起淡淡的胡椒辣。她遞給我一根牙籤,教我怎麼把蝸牛肉從殼裡挑出來,口感介於花枝與蘑菇之間,帶點嚼勁。一碗大約8迪拉姆(不到台幣30元),我一連喝了兩碗,她笑著又多舀了一勺湯給我,沒有多收錢。喝完湯,我走進老城西側著名的「Mechoui Alley」(麥吉巷),整條巷子瀰漫著炭烤羊脂的濃烈煙燻味,幾家攤販從清晨就開始用地窯慢烤整隻羊,師傅直接徒手撕下羊肩肉放在秤上秤重賣,一份大約100迪拉姆(約台幣330元),配上現烤的圓餅khobz、孜然鹽與一碗清湯。羊肉入口即化,油脂香混著炭火氣息,我坐在塑膠矮凳上,跟鄰座一位摩洛哥卡車司機用破法文聊了半小時,他堅持要我嚐嚐他杯裡的薄荷茶,那種被陌生人請客的溫暖,比羊肉本身更讓我記得這座城市。

午後:染坊、皮革與磁磚工坊的顏色政治學

飯後我特意繞去老城東北角的Chouara Tanneries看看,雖然這座皮革染坊比非斯的同名景點規模小很多,但那種混合了鴿糞、石灰與植物染料的刺鼻氣味依然衝進鼻腔——工人遞給我一把新鮮薄荷葉,教我怎麼捂著鼻子觀察染缸,染缸裡番紅花黃、石榴紅、靛藍層層疊疊,皮革師傅赤腳踩在染液裡翻動皮料,他們的手掌因為長年浸泡染料,已經染成一種洗不掉的深褐色。往南走進老城巷弄深處的一間小型zellige磁磚工坊,老師傅用一把類似鑿子的工具,徒手把方形瓷磚敲成星形、菱形碎片,再一片片拼進幾何圖案裡,他告訴我,這門手藝在他家已經傳了四代,學徒至少要磨七年才能出師。我買了一片巴掌大的拼貼樣品當紀念品,60迪拉姆,老師傅用報紙包起來時,還隨手在角落簽了自己的名字——這座城市的每個工匠,似乎都對自己的手藝有種近乎驕傲的堅持。

他們染的不只是皮革,是一整座城市對顏色近乎固執的記憶。

傍晚到深夜:傑馬夫納廣場的聲音劇場與一碗哈里拉

傍晚六點半,太陽開始西斜,傑馬夫納廣場的變化像變魔術。白天賣柳橙汁的推車還在,但說書人、耍蛇人、坤布里琴(gnaoua樂器)樂手陸續佔據廣場各角落,鼓聲、笛聲與人群的喧嘩混成一片,聯合國教科文組織把這種「口述文化空間」列為非物質文化遺產,理由是這廣場本身就是一座活的劇場。我碰巧遇上齋戒月尾聲,天色完全暗下來前,整條廣場邊的攤販開始擺出成排的椰棗、無花果乾與一鍋鍋熱騰騰的哈里拉湯(harira),那是用番茄、扁豆與香菜熬煮的濃湯,通常是穆斯林開齋(iftar)的第一口食物。一位賣椰棗的攤主看我站在旁邊張望,直接遞給我三顆椰棗跟一小碗湯,用手勢示意「免費、朋友」。我後來才知道,在齋戒月裡請陌生人吃第一口開齋食物,被視為一種福報,不能拒絕,也不該詢問價錢——這是我在旅遊書上從沒讀過的細節。廣場另一頭,一群圍觀的孩子跟著坤布里琴手拍手打節奏,那種源自西非黑奴後裔的Gnaoua音樂,節奏低沉反覆,像是一種集體催眠,老一輩摩洛哥人相信這種音樂具有驅邪治病的力量,每年六月在濱海小城索維拉(Essaouira)舉辦的Gnaoua World Music Festival,吸引全世界的爵士與世界音樂樂手前來朝聖。夜色徹底降臨後,我爬上廣場邊一間茶館的天台,點了一杯薄荷茶,俯瞰整片廣場的燈火與炊煙,清真寺喚拜聲從Koutoubia宣禮塔傳來,悠長迴盪。那一刻我突然理解,馬拉喀什的美學從來不是掛在牆上等人欣賞的藝術品,而是活在氣味、聲音與陌生人遞給你的那一碗湯裡。

隔天上午我特地去了老城外新城區的MACAAL(阿爾馬阿丹非洲當代美術館),這座由私人基金會創辦的美術館,展出大量非洲當代藝術家作品,策展主題聚焦在殖民歷史與身份認同,跟老城裡那些古老工藝形成強烈的時間對話——一邊是五百年前的幾何謙卑,一邊是當代藝術家用畫布質問未來。離開美術館時已近黃昏,計程車司機放的是Gnaoua混搭電子樂,窗外掠過的紅色城牆在夕陽下幾乎要燃燒起來。我在riad的天台上坐到日落,遠處宣禮塔的剪影漸漸融進暮色,那杯薄荷茶已經涼了,但我捨不得喝完,像是捨不得結束這一整天被顏色與聲音餵養的旅程。

ENGLISH

My flight touched down at Marrakech Menara Airport well after midnight, but the moment I stepped outside, the air hit me first — dry heat laced with dust and something faintly floral, jasmine perhaps, drifting from somewhere I couldn't see. The taxi driver confirmed my riad's address in French, and through the window the old city walls glowed under streetlamps in a color somewhere between brick and ochre. Locals call it 'Marrakech red.' I later learned this isn't just a poetic nickname — it's an actual municipal code, a building regulation dating back to the French protectorate era in 1912, requiring every façade in the city to be painted this specific reddish ochre. A city that legislates its own color. That single fact told me, on my very first night, that aesthetics here isn't decoration. It's identity.

6 AM: The Geometry of Light at Ben Youssef Madrasa

I set my alarm for 5:30, determined to beat the tour groups into Ben Youssef Madrasa. At six sharp, the gates had just opened, the ticket booth manned by a single half-asleep attendant. Entry cost 70 dirhams, roughly seven US dollars. The instant I stepped into the courtyard I stopped in my tracks — the sun was cutting sideways across the cedar wood carvings, the carved plaster (gebs), and the zellige tilework, casting a breathing carpet of light and shadow across the stone floor. This sixteenth-century Islamic college once housed over eight hundred students, and the cramped dormitory cells still remain, but what held me for nearly an hour was the fact that no pattern on the courtyard walls repeats exactly. Craftsmen believed only God's creation could be perfect and infinite; human hands were obligated to leave a trace of asymmetry as a mark of humility. I crouched down and ran my fingers along the tile seams — rough, cool to the touch — and tried to imagine artisans five centuries ago carving these stalactite-like muqarnas ceiling ornaments one chisel stroke at a time. It was silent except for pigeons flapping somewhere above the light well.

Here, symmetry is a form of faith — and the deliberate imperfection is what completes the work.

9:30 AM: Jardin Majorelle and the Yves Saint Laurent Museum — How I Actually Did It

Leaving the madrasa, I walked down Rue Amsefah to the main road and flagged a petit taxi, negotiating a fare of 35 dirhams to Jardin Majorelle before getting in — fares here aren't metered, and settling the price upfront is the first lesson every visitor learns. By the time I arrived at 9:30, the ticket line already snaked nearly a hundred people deep; booking online in advance would have saved me a good forty minutes. Garden admission runs 70 dirhams, and if you want the Yves Saint Laurent Museum next door as well, the combined ticket comes to about 230 dirhams total, roughly 24 US dollars. The moment I walked in, I froze. The building French painter Jacques Majorelle designed in the 1930s is painted an almost fluorescent cobalt blue — now known simply as 'Majorelle blue' — and the bamboo groves, cacti, and bougainvillea in front of it create such violent color contrast that it feels like walking into a Cubist painting. I spent nearly two hours wandering the grounds; the bamboo section smelled damp and grassy, water lilies floated silently in the ponds, and every so often a lizard darted out from between the stones. Next door, the YSL Museum, completed only in 2017, alternates raw concrete with terracotta brick, and inside, the haute couture sketches on display stopped me cold — I learned that the designer's iconic 1966 tuxedo, 'Le Smoking,' was directly inspired by the silhouette of Berber robes he'd seen on Marrakech streets. All told — round-trip taxi fares, the combined museum ticket, and a mint tea at the garden café — I spent about 40 US dollars, and the window between nine and one in the afternoon proved ideal: thinner crowds and the best light for photographs.

Noon: Hajja's Snail Soup and the Roast Lamb of Mechoui Alley

By 12:30, my stomach was staging a protest. Rather than heading straight for the big food stalls in Jemaa el-Fna, I detoured to a modest cart at the square's edge, run by an elderly woman everyone simply calls 'Mama Hajja.' She sells exactly one thing: babbouche, Moroccan snail soup. The broth, simmered over six hours with more than fifteen spices — fennel seed, mint, cloves, licorice root — comes out dark brown, nearly black. The first sip was warm licorice sweetness, then a cooling wave of mint, then a faint peppery heat rising at the back of the throat. She handed me a toothpick and showed me how to pry the snails from their shells; the texture landed somewhere between squid and mushroom, pleasantly chewy. A bowl costs 8 dirhams — less than a dollar — and I drank two, prompting her to ladle in extra broth without charging me a dirham more. Afterward I walked into the famous Mechoui Alley on the medina's western edge, where the whole lane reeks of charred lamb fat; vendors have been slow-roasting whole lambs in underground pit ovens since dawn, and the men there simply tear the shoulder meat off by hand and weigh it on old brass scales. A portion runs about 100 dirhams — roughly 10 dollars — served with fresh khobz flatbread, cumin salt, and a light broth on the side. The lamb melted on contact, smoke and fat mingling on the tongue, and I sat on a plastic stool beside a Moroccan truck driver, trading broken French for half an hour. He insisted I try his mint tea. That small act of a stranger buying me tea stayed with me longer than the lamb itself.

Afternoon: The Color Politics of Dye Pits, Leather, and Zellige Workshops

After lunch I detoured to the Chouara-style tanneries on the medina's northeastern edge. Though smaller than the famous ones in Fes, the sharp reek of pigeon droppings, lime, and plant dye still hit me the moment I approached — a worker handed me a sprig of fresh mint to hold under my nose while I watched the dye vats, layered in saffron yellow, pomegranate red, and indigo, with barefoot tanners wading through them, their palms permanently stained a deep brown from years of contact. Further south, tucked deep in the medina's alleys, I found a small zellige tile workshop where an old master used a chisel-like tool to hand-carve square tiles into stars and diamonds, fitting each fragment into a geometric pattern. He told me the craft has passed through four generations of his family, and an apprentice needs at least seven years before being considered skilled enough to work alone. I bought a palm-sized tile mosaic sample for 60 dirhams; as he wrapped it in newspaper, he signed his name in the corner without being asked. Every artisan I met in this city seemed to carry a quiet, almost stubborn pride in their craft.

What they dye isn't just leather — it's an entire city's obstinate memory of color.

Evening Into Night: The Sound Theater of Jemaa el-Fna and a Bowl of Harira

By 6:30, as the sun dipped low, Jemaa el-Fna transformed like a magic trick. The daytime orange juice carts remained, but storytellers, snake charmers, and gnaoua musicians with their sintir lutes began claiming corners of the square one by one, drums and flutes and the roar of the crowd blending into a single wall of sound. UNESCO lists this as an intangible oral heritage site precisely because the square itself functions as a living theater. I happened to arrive near the end of Ramadan, and just before full darkness fell, the vendors lining the square set out rows of dates, dried figs, and steaming pots of harira, the tomato-lentil-coriander soup traditionally eaten as the very first bite at iftar. A date vendor noticed me hovering nearby and simply handed me three dates and a small bowl of soup, gesturing 'free, friend.' I later learned that offering a stranger the first bite of the iftar meal during Ramadan is considered a source of blessing, and it's considered rude to refuse it or to ask the price — a detail no guidebook had ever told me. Nearby, a cluster of children clapped along to the low, repetitive rhythm of a gnaoua musician; older Moroccans believe this music, rooted in the traditions of West African slave descendants, carries the power to exorcise and heal, and every June the coastal town of Essaouira hosts the Gnaoua World Music Festival, drawing jazz and world-music pilgrims from across the globe. Once night fully settled, I climbed to a rooftop café overlooking the square, ordered mint tea, and watched the smoke and lantern light spread beneath me as the call to prayer rolled out from the Koutoubia minaret, long and resonant. In that moment I understood that Marrakech's aesthetics were never meant to hang on a wall for admiration — they live in scent, in sound, and in a bowl of soup handed to you by a stranger.

The next morning I made my way out to MACAAL, the Museum of African Contemporary Art Al Maaden, in the newer part of town beyond the medina. Founded by a private foundation, it showcases a striking body of contemporary African art, its curatorial focus circling colonial history and questions of identity — a conversation across time with the ancient craftsmanship inside the old city walls: one side five centuries of geometric humility, the other, contemporary artists interrogating the future on canvas. By the time I left, dusk was settling in; the taxi radio played a gnaoua-electronic fusion track, and the red city walls outside seemed to catch fire in the fading light. I sat on my riad's rooftop until the sun went down completely, watching the minaret's silhouette dissolve into dusk. My mint tea had gone cold, but I couldn't bring myself to finish it — as if finishing it meant admitting the day, fed entirely on color and sound, was truly over.
