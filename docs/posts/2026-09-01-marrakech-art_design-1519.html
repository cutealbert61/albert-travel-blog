中文

飛機降落的前一晚,我幾乎沒睡。不是興奮,是機艙裡反覆播放的落地畫面在腦中揮之不去——紅褐色的城牆、遠方阿特拉斯山的雪頂、還有那句我從旅遊書上抄下來的形容詞:「赭紅之城」。但真正踏進馬拉喀什的第一個清晨,凌晨四點半,喚拜聲把我從時差的淺眠裡拉起來時,我才明白,這座城市從來不是用「看」的,它是用全身的感官去讀的一本立體書。

我住的利雅德(Riad)藏在麥地那深處一條連導航都會迷路的巷子裡,厚重的雪松木門一關上,外面市集的喧鬧瞬間被吸走,只剩中庭噴泉滴水的聲音。那一刻我就決定,接下來這一整天,我要把行程徹底交給光線——跟著太陽的角度,走一趟屬於建築、色彩與手工藝的馬拉喀什。

清晨六點:庫圖比亞宣禮塔下的第一道光

推開利雅德的木門,巷子裡幾乎沒有人,只有幾隻瘦削的橘貓窩在麵包窯的餘溫旁邊取暖,烤爐飄出的麥香混著隔夜殘留的孜然味,是我對馬拉喀什嗅覺記憶的起點。我沿著逐漸亮起來的天色往庫圖比亞清真寺(Koutoubia Mosque)的方向走,大約二十分鐘的路程,腳下的石板路還帶著夜裡的濕氣。

庫圖比亞的宣禮塔建於十二世紀阿爾摩哈德王朝時期,高達七十七公尺,是塞維亞大清真寺鐘樓與拉巴特哈桑塔的姊妹建築。清晨六點的天光還帶著一點靛藍,砂岩色的塔身被染成極淺的粉橘色,四面牆上的幾何鏤空窗花在漸強的光線裡由暗轉亮,像是慢慢被打開的相機光圈。非穆斯林不能入內,但站在外圍花園的長椅上,看著喚拜聲從擴音器傳出、驚起一群鴿子繞塔盤旋,我第一次意識到,伊斯蘭建築裡的「留白」與「比例」,本身就是一種極簡的設計語言——沒有具象雕塑,所有的美都建立在數學與韻律之上。

上午九點半:班尤瑟夫伊斯蘭學校的幾何課

離開庫圖比亞,我在附近小攤買了一杯現榨柳橙汁,四塊摩洛哥迪拉姆(約合新台幣十二元),酸中帶甜,一口氣灌下去当作早餐。接著徒步穿過賽馬林市集(Souk Semmarine)往北,巷子越走越窄,銅器敲打聲、皮革味、還有攤販此起彼落的招呼聲把我團團圍住,老實說我迷路了兩次,靠著手機定位加上一位賣香料的大叔比手畫腳的指路,才在大約四十分鐘後找到班尤瑟夫伊斯蘭學校(Medersa Ben Youssef)的入口。

門票七十迪拉姆,約合新台幣兩百三十元,開放時間是早上九點到下午六點。我刻意選在九點半抵達,比多數旅行團早了一個半小時,整個中庭幾乎只有我和一位打掃庭院的老先生。踏進中庭的瞬間,我下意識停下腳步——四面牆壁佈滿了三種工藝的疊加:下段是彩色瓷磚拼貼(zellige),幾何星形圖案精準得像用電腦切割;中段是雕刻灰泥(stucco),整片牆刻滿古蘭經經文的庫法體書法;上段則是雪松木雕花,深褐色木紋在陽光下泛著溫潤的光。中庭中央有一方淺淺的水池,把整棟建築的立面倒映在水面上,微風一吹,倒影跟著碎成一片幾何拼圖。

我在這裡待了將近一個半小時,大部分時間只是坐在迴廊的陰影裡,看著陽光隨時間移動,在牆面雕花上投下不斷變化的鏤空光影。樓上是舊時古蘭經學院的學生宿舍,一百三十二間狹小的石室沿著迴廊排列,窗戶正對中庭的美景,而房間本身卻樸素得近乎苦行——這種「公共空間極致華麗,私人空間極致簡樸」的對比,後來成了我理解摩洛哥室內設計哲學的一把鑰匙。附近還有阿爾摩拉維德穹頂(Almoravid Koubba),是馬拉喀什現存最古老的伊斯蘭建築遺跡,門票通常包含在同一張聯票裡,值得順道一看,大約十五分鐘就能走完。提醒想去的人:務必趕在十一點旅行團大軍抵達前進場,不然中庭的水池倒影會被此起彼落的手機自拍打斷。

在這裡,幾何圖案從來不是裝飾,而是一種關於無限與信仰的語言——每一片瓷磚都在說,人的手藝終究是模仿神的秩序。

正午:馬約爾花園與聖羅蘭美術館的鈷藍實驗室

中午時分,陽光開始變得直射刺眼,我攔了一輛小黃計程車前往新城區格利茲(Gueliz),車資談定二十五迪拉姆,大約十分鐘車程,司機是個健談的中年人,一路跟我抱怨麥地那的觀光客太多,反倒是格利茲才有「真正的馬拉喀什人生活」。到了馬約爾花園(Jardin Majorelle),入口已經排起一條蜿蜒的隊伍,現場買票要等將近四十分鐘,我後來才知道線上預約可以直接刷碼進場,省下不少時間,票價一百四十迪拉姆。

這座花園是法國畫家雅克·馬約爾在一九三〇年代親手打造的私人天堂,他調配出的那種飽和到近乎螢光的深藍色,後來被命名為「馬約爾藍」。一九八〇年,伊夫·聖羅蘭與伴侶皮爾·貝爾傑買下這座荒廢的花園並修復,鈷藍色的別墅、明黃色的窗框,配上竹林與仙人掌陣列,色彩對比大膽到近乎挑釁,卻在摩洛哥的烈日下顯得無比合理——原來高彩度不是任性,而是對抗這裡刺眼陽光的生存智慧。

花園旁新建的伊夫·聖羅蘭美術館由建築事務所Studio KO操刀,外牆用手工燒製的陶土磚拼出類似織物經緯的紋理,遠看像一塊放大的粗花呢布料。館內展出聖羅蘭從一九六二到二〇〇二年間的高訂手稿與服裝,策展特別強調馬拉喀什的市集色彩、柏柏爾人的珠寶紋樣如何滲透進他的設計語彙——看著那些印著幾何拼接與飽和撞色的晚禮服草圖,我才真正理解,原來這座城市的市集色彩,早在半世紀前就已經悄悄走上了巴黎的伸展台。

午後兩點:一頓意外找到的塔吉鍋午餐

逛完美術館已近下午兩點,飢腸轆轆的我決定不叫車,徒步走回麥地那,結果在拉赫芭卡迪瑪廣場(Rahba Kedima)附近又迷了路,反而意外撞見一間叫「Café des Épices」的香料廣場咖啡館,二樓露台正對著滿是藤籃與皮革攤的小廣場。我先點了一杯薄荷茶,十五迪拉姆,茶壺倒下去的瞬間茶葉香混著大量現摘薄荷葉的清涼氣息撲面而來,店員特意把茶壺舉得老高倒茶,說這樣能讓茶湯「呼吸」,泡出更多氣泡與香氣。

吃飽了茶水墊胃,我繼續往南走了約十五分鐘,到了在地人推薦、以露台聞名的「Nomad」餐廳,地址就在賽馬林市集裡的阿爾詹巷弄(Derb Aarjan),需要先穿過堆滿羊毛與絲巾的攤位,再爬上一段不太顯眼的樓梯才能找到入口。我點了一份燉梅乾杏仁羊肉塔吉鍋,一百一十迪拉姆,約新台幣三百六十元;鍋蓋掀開的瞬間,肉桂與番紅花混著羊肉油脂的濃郁香氣整個湧出來,羊肉燉得幾乎脫骨,甜梅乾的果酸中和了油脂的厚重,撒在表面的烤杏仁片提供了脆口的層次。我用手撕開一小塊摩洛哥圓餅(khobz)沾著醬汁吃,鹹甜交織的滋味讓我一口接一口停不下來。另外點了一份七蔬庫斯庫斯,七十迪拉姆,蒸得極其鬆軟的粗麥粒吸飽了燉菜湯汁,胡蘿蔔與南瓜燉得綿密,配菜的辣醬(harissa)一點就足夠嗆辣提味。露台上一群鸛鳥就築巢在對面屋頂,不時傳來牠們敲喙的咔咔聲,底下市集的討價還價聲隱隱傳上來,那一餐我坐了將近一個半小時捨不得走,結完帳含果汁總共花了將近兩百迪拉姆,卻覺得是整趟旅程裡最物超所值的一餐。

黃昏:德吉瑪廣場上一場沒有劇本的街頭劇場

傍晚六點左右,天光轉成蜂蜜色,我又走回德吉瑪廣場(Jemaa el-Fna),白天賣柳橙汁與雜貨的攤位這時候正快速拆換成一整排冒著煙的烤肉攤,孜然、烤肉油脂與炭火的氣味瞬間籠罩整個廣場。我在廣場邊緣停下腳步,看見一群人圍成一圈聽說書人(halqa)講故事,雖然聽不懂阿拉伯語,但說書人手勢誇張、聲音忽高忽低,周圍聽眾時而大笑時而屏息,那種現場感染力完全不需要語言翻譯。

一位坐在矮凳上畫指甲花圖案(henna)的女士朝我招手,她叫法蒂瑪(Fatima),用簡單的英文加法語跟我聊了起來。她說指甲花在柏柏爾文化裡不只是裝飾,更是保護的象徵,新娘出嫁前一晚,家族女性會聚在一起替她畫上繁複的花紋,圖案畫得越密,象徵祝福越多。她一邊在我手背上畫了一小朵簡單的花,一邊說了一句柏柏爾諺語給我聽:「第一杯茶苦如生命,第二杯茶甜如愛情,第三杯茶淡如死亡」,原來這裡待客的三杯薄荷茶,本身就是一套完整的人生隱喻。廣場另一頭傳來剛瓦音樂(Gnawa)手鼓與鐵片響板的節奏,低沉的鼓聲跟著人群的腳步一起晃動,我站在原地聽了快二十分鐘捨不得離開。她也提醒我,如果碰上齋戒月造訪,整個廣場會在日落那一刻瞬間安靜下來,所有人專心等待開齋的號角,那種集體屏息的氣氛,比任何白天的喧鬧都更讓人動容。

馬拉喀什不是一座用來看的城市,而是一座用來聞、用來摸、用來在氣味與顏色裡迷路的城市。

夜色:格利茲設計街區的最後巡禮

晚上八點多,我搭計程車回到格利茲,想在離開前補上這座城市「當代設計」的那一面。33 Rue Majorelle是一間集結摩洛哥新銳設計師的複合選物店,裡面陳列著把傳統瓷磚圖案轉印上皮革包款的作品,還有把柏柏爾地毯紋樣簡化成幾何印花的絲巾,價格從幾百到上千迪拉姆不等。隔壁MACMA非洲當代美術館規模不大,一小時就能逛完,展出的雕塑與裝置作品大膽挪用傳統圖騰,和白天在班尤瑟夫看到的古典幾何形成有趣的對話——原來這座城市的圖案語言,從十二世紀到二十一世紀,一直沒有真正斷過。

最後我在Kosybar的頂樓露台坐下,點了最後一杯薄荷茶,遠處打了燈的庫圖比亞宣禮塔在夜色裡泛著暖黃色的光,像是把清晨看到的那道粉橘晨光,又還給了我一次。那一刻我才真正明白,馬拉喀什的設計美學從來不是分裂的——瓷磚上的幾何、花園裡的鈷藍、市集裡的番紅花黃、指甲花的褐紅,全部都是同一套色彩語法的不同章節,而我這一整天,不過是把這本書從頭到尾翻了一遍。

回旅館的路上,巷子裡的燈籠一盞一盞亮起,把石牆染成溫暖的琥珀色。我摸著手背上那朵還沒褪色的指甲花圖案,忽然覺得,這一天走過的路,遠比任何一本設計雜誌能教會我的都還要多。

ENGLISH

I barely slept the night before my flight landed. It wasn't excitement so much as the looping image in my head of what I was about to see — ochre ramparts, the snow-capped Atlas Mountains in the distance, and that tired old descriptor I'd copied out of a guidebook: 'the Red City.' But it wasn't until my actual first morning in Marrakech, jolted awake at 4:30am by the call to prayer cutting through my jet-lagged half-sleep, that I understood this city was never meant to be looked at. It's meant to be read with your whole body.

My riad was tucked into a lane so deep in the medina that even my map app gave up. Once the heavy cedar door closed behind me, the market noise outside was swallowed whole, leaving only the drip of the courtyard fountain. That was the moment I decided how I'd spend the day: I would follow the light, hour by hour, and let it lead me through Marrakech's architecture, color, and craft.

6am: First Light on the Koutoubia Minaret

I pushed open the riad's wooden door onto an almost empty lane, just a few skinny orange cats curled against the warm side of a bread oven, the smell of baking flour mixing with last night's leftover cumin — my first olfactory memory of this city. I walked toward the Koutoubia Mosque as the sky slowly lightened, about twenty minutes on foot, the cobblestones still damp with overnight dew.

The Koutoubia's minaret was built in the twelfth century under the Almohad dynasty and stands seventy-seven meters tall, a sister structure to Seville's Giralda and Rabat's Hassan Tower. At 6am the sky still held a trace of indigo, and the sandstone tower blushed the faintest pink-orange as the light climbed. The geometric latticework carved into each face of the tower seemed to shift from dark to luminous as the light strengthened, like a camera aperture slowly opening. Non-Muslims can't enter, but standing on a bench in the surrounding gardens, listening to the call to prayer crackle out of the loudspeakers and watching a flock of pigeons spiral around the tower, I understood for the first time that in Islamic architecture, restraint and proportion are themselves a minimalist design language — no figurative sculpture at all, just beauty built entirely on mathematics and rhythm.

9:30am: A Geometry Lesson at the Ben Youssef Madrasa

Leaving the Koutoubia, I grabbed a fresh orange juice from a street cart for four dirhams, roughly twelve New Taiwan dollars, sweet and tart enough to count as breakfast on the go. I then walked north through Souk Semmarine, the alleys narrowing with every turn, the clang of coppersmiths and the smell of leather closing in around me. I got lost twice — once relying on GPS, once on the wildly gesturing directions of a spice vendor — before finally finding the entrance to the Ben Youssef Madrasa about forty minutes later.

Admission was seventy dirhams, about 230 New Taiwan dollars, open from 9am to 6pm. I'd deliberately arrived at 9:30, ninety minutes ahead of most tour groups, so the courtyard held only me and an old man sweeping the tiles. The moment I stepped inside I stopped in my tracks: the walls were built up in three layered crafts — a lower band of zellige tilework, star-shaped geometric mosaics cut with almost machine-like precision; a middle band of carved stucco, entire panels covered in Quranic verses rendered in Kufic script; and an upper band of cedar wood carving, its deep brown grain glowing softly in the light. A shallow reflecting pool sat at the courtyard's center, mirroring the whole façade, and when a breeze passed, the reflection shattered into a moving geometric puzzle.

I stayed nearly ninety minutes, mostly just sitting in the shaded arcade watching the sun travel and cast constantly shifting pierced-light patterns across the carved walls. Upstairs, 132 tiny student cells from the building's days as a Quranic college line the corridors, each window framing that same courtyard view while the rooms themselves remain almost ascetically plain. That contrast — lavish public space, austere private space — later became my key to understanding Moroccan interior design philosophy. Nearby stands the Almoravid Koubba, the oldest surviving Islamic monument in Marrakech, usually included on the same combined ticket and worth a fifteen-minute detour. One tip for anyone planning this: get there before 11am, before the tour buses arrive, or the pool's reflection will be interrupted by a constant parade of selfie sticks.

Here, geometric pattern was never decoration — it's a language about infinity and faith, every tile insisting that human craft is, at best, an imitation of divine order.

Noon: The Cobalt Laboratory of Jardin Majorelle

By midday the sun had gone harsh and direct, so I flagged a petit taxi to Gueliz, the new town, negotiating a fare of twenty-five dirhams for a ten-minute ride. My driver spent the whole trip complaining that the medina was overrun with tourists these days, insisting Gueliz was where 'real Marrakchis' actually lived. At Jardin Majorelle, the line already snaked well past the entrance — a nearly forty-minute wait for walk-up tickets, though I later learned advance online booking lets you skip straight in. Admission was 140 dirhams.

The garden was built by hand in the 1930s by French painter Jacques Majorelle, who mixed a saturated, almost fluorescent blue that now bears his name — Majorelle Blue. In 1980, Yves Saint Laurent and his partner Pierre Bergé bought the abandoned garden and restored it, pairing the cobalt villa with mustard-yellow window frames against thickets of bamboo and rows of cacti. The color contrast is bold to the point of provocation, yet under Marrakech's blazing sun it makes perfect sense — high saturation here isn't whimsy, it's a survival strategy against glare.

Next door, the Yves Saint Laurent Museum, designed by Studio KO, wears an exterior of hand-fired terracotta brick arranged to mimic the warp and weft of woven fabric, reading from a distance like an enlarged swatch of tweed. Inside, the collection spans his couture sketches and garments from 1962 to 2002, with curatorial notes tracing how Marrakech's market colors and Berber jewelry motifs seeped into his design vocabulary. Looking at those sketches of evening gowns built from bold geometric color-blocking, I finally understood: this city's market palette had already walked the runways of Paris half a century ago, quietly, without anyone announcing it.

2pm: A Tagine Lunch I Found by Accident

By the time I left the museum it was nearly 2pm and I was starving. I decided to walk back to the medina instead of taking a cab, promptly got lost again near Rahba Kedima square, and stumbled onto a café called Café des Épices, its second-floor terrace overlooking a small plaza crowded with basket-weavers and leather stalls. I ordered mint tea first, fifteen dirhams, and the moment the pot was poured, the scent of fresh mint leaves and tea hit me all at once — the waiter lifted the teapot dramatically high as he poured, explaining that this lets the tea 'breathe' and releases more aroma and foam.

Tea settled my stomach, and I walked another fifteen minutes to a restaurant called Nomad, known locally for its rooftop terrace, tucked down Derb Aarjan inside Souk Semmarine — you have to cut through stalls piled with wool and silk scarves and climb an easily-missed staircase to find the entrance. I ordered a lamb tagine with prunes and almonds, 110 dirhams, roughly 360 New Taiwan dollars. When the lid came off, cinnamon and saffron rolled out with the rich smell of lamb fat; the meat had been braised until it fell off the bone, the sweet-tart prunes cutting through the richness, toasted almond slices adding crunch on top. I tore off pieces of round Moroccan bread, khobz, to scoop up the sauce, the salty-sweet combination pulling me back for bite after bite. I also ordered seven-vegetable couscous, seventy dirhams, the semolina steamed impossibly light and fluffy, soaked in the braising liquid, carrots and squash stewed to a silky softness, a spoonful of harissa adding exactly the right amount of heat. A pair of storks had nested on the rooftop across from the terrace, their bills clacking every so often, while the murmur of market haggling drifted up from below. I lingered there for nearly ninety minutes, unwilling to leave, and walked out having spent about 200 dirhams total including juice — easily the best value meal of the whole trip.

Dusk: An Unscripted Street Theater in Jemaa el-Fna

Around 6pm, the light turned honey-colored and I wandered back to Jemaa el-Fna. The stalls that had sold orange juice and household goods all day were being swiftly dismantled and replaced by a long row of smoking grill carts, the smell of cumin, charred lamb fat, and charcoal blanketing the square almost instantly. At the plaza's edge I stopped to watch a circle of people gathered around a storyteller — a halqa. I couldn't understand a word of Arabic, but his gestures were huge, his voice swinging wildly between whisper and shout, and the crowd around him laughed and gasped in perfect sync. The charisma needed no translation.

A woman doing henna work from a low stool waved me over. Her name was Fatima, and in a mix of broken English and French she explained that henna in Berber culture isn't just decoration — it's protective. The night before a wedding, the women of a family gather to paint the bride's hands and feet in increasingly elaborate patterns, the density of the design believed to carry proportionally more blessing. As she painted a small flower on the back of my hand, she recited a Berber proverb: 'The first glass of tea is bitter like life, the second is sweet like love, the third is gentle like death' — and suddenly the ritual of three glasses of mint tea revealed itself as a complete philosophy of life. Across the square, Gnawa musicians kept a low, hypnotic rhythm going on hand drums and iron castanets, the crowd swaying with it almost without realizing. Fatima also mentioned that if I ever visited during Ramadan, the entire square falls silent in the instant before sunset, everyone waiting together for the call that breaks the fast — a collective held breath far more moving, she said, than any daytime noise.

Marrakech isn't a city built to be looked at. It's a city built to be smelled, touched, and gotten lost in — through scent, through color, through the crowd.

Night: One Last Loop Through Gueliz's Design District

Past 8pm, I took a cab back to Gueliz to catch the contemporary side of the city's design story before I left. 33 Rue Majorelle is a concept store gathering emerging Moroccan designers, its shelves stocked with leather bags printed with reinterpreted tile patterns and silk scarves that simplify Berber rug motifs into modern geometric prints, prices ranging from a few hundred to over a thousand dirhams. Next door, the compact MACMA Museum of African Contemporary Art can be seen in under an hour; its sculptures and installations boldly reappropriate traditional motifs, creating an unexpected dialogue with the classical geometry I'd seen that morning at Ben Youssef — proof that this city's visual vocabulary, from the twelfth century to the twenty-first, has never actually stopped evolving.

I ended the night on the rooftop terrace at Kosybar with one final glass of mint tea, watching the floodlit Koutoubia minaret glow amber in the distance — the same pink-orange dawn light I'd watched that morning, returned to me one more time before I left. Only then did it truly click that Marrakech's design language was never fragmented at all: the tile geometry, the cobalt of the garden, the saffron yellow of the souks, the rust-red of henna — they're all chapters of the same color grammar, and my one long day here had simply been a chance to read the whole book cover to cover.

Walking back to the riad, the lanterns in the alley flickered on one by one, staining the stone walls a warm amber. I touched the not-yet-faded henna flower on the back of my hand and realized this single day had taught me more than any design magazine ever could.
