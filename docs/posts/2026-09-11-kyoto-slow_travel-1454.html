中文

清晨五點半,我站在旅館二樓的窗邊,京都的天空還是一種介於藍與灰之間的顏色,連鳥都還沒吵起來。我對自己說:今天不排行程,不衝景點,只想像一個京都人一樣過一天。這是我第三次來京都,前兩次分別為了清水寺的楓紅和嵐山的竹林,拍了很多照片,卻總覺得少了什麼——好像只是路過一座巨大的攝影棚。這一次,我把相機收進包包深處,用整整一天的時間,只做京都人平日會做的事:去市場、喝一杯咖啡、洗一次錢湯、遇見巷弄裡的小拜拜。

清晨五點半:鴨川醒來之前

清晨五點半,鴨川的水聲比車聲清楚。我從御池通附近的旅館出發,沿著河岸步道往北走,腳下的碎石路踩起來沙沙作響,河面飄著一層薄薄水氣,幾隻鷺鷥站在淺灘上一動也不動,像還沒睡醒的雕像。晨跑的京都人三三兩兩經過,步伐很輕,沒有人戴耳機,只有布鞋踏地的聲音和偶爾傳來的呼吸聲。我在賀茂大橋附近坐下,看著第一道陽光斜斜切過東山稜線,把河面染成淡淡的橘粉色,那種光線只維持不到十分鐘,卻讓我整個人清醒過來——原來京都的早晨,是用「安靜」堆疊出來的奢侈。六點半左右,我沿著河原町通往西走,前往還沒完全甦醒的錦市場,路上經過幾間町家老屋,木格子窗後透出微弱燈光,聽得見鍋鏟碰撞的聲音,那是店家在準備一天的生意。

早晨八點:寺町通的咖啡與職人的早晨

早晨八點,我走進錦市場,這條被稱為「京都人的廚房」的拱廊商店街,此時大約只開了三分之一的店家,遊客還沒湧入,反而看得清楚每間老鋪的真實樣貌。我在三木雞卵前面停下腳步,這家專賣蛋料理的老店從七點半就開始現煎出汁巻き玉子,一份切成六塊、插著竹籤,約三百五十日圓。老闆娘俐落地把蛋液一層層捲進四角銅鍋裡,蛋香混著柴魚高湯的氣味整條街都聞得到。我站在攤位前吃第一口,外層微焦帶鑊氣,內層卻嫩得像布丁,鹹甜的高湯在嘴裡化開,那股溫熱感從喉嚨一路暖到胃裡,是我吃過最好的路邊早餐之一。

吃完蛋捲,我沿著寺町通往南走十分鐘,到了創業於一九四〇年的イノダコーヒ本店。京都的喫茶店文化和咖啡本身一樣重要,這裡的常客多半是穿西裝或和服工作服的中老年人,每人固定坐同一張桌子,店員甚至不用問就知道他們要什麼。我點了招牌「アラビアの真珠」,一杯已依傳統比例加好牛奶與糖的咖啡,不需另外調整——這是イノダ堅持了超過七十年的喝法。咖啡順口帶點微苦尾韻,配上他們的奶油土司,吐司邊緣烤得酥脆,中間卻鬆軟,奶油一抹就化開。隔壁桌兩位老先生在討論昨晚的棒球比賽,語調悠閒得像這杯咖啡一樣,完全不趕時間。那一刻我才明白,京都人不是「去」喝咖啡,而是「住」在咖啡館裡度過早晨的一段時光。

京都人不是去喝咖啡,而是把清晨的一段時間,安放進咖啡館裡。

正午:御所深處的野餐時光

正午時分,我搭京阪電車到出町柳駅,這裡是鴨川與高野川交會形成的三角洲,當地人叫它「鴨川デルタ」,是京都人假日野餐、小孩玩水的地方。車站旁的出町ふたば,從一八九九年開始賣豆餅,是那種一靠近就能聞到紅豆香氣的老店。我排了大約二十分鐘的隊,前面幾位都是提著布袋、看起來像常客的婆婆,買完就直接轉身走進巷子裡,顯然不是為了觀光。我買了兩顆豆餅,一顆二百二十日圓,外皮是淡粉色帶顆粒感的糯米,咬下去先是鹹味豌豆顆粒,接著紅豆內餡的甜味才慢慢浮現,兩種味道在嘴裡拉扯又融合,吃到最後有點捨不得吞下去。

我提著豆餅走到鴨川デルタ,河水在這裡分岔成兩條,踏著露出水面的龜形石頭跳過去,找了塊平坦的石頭坐下。河風帶著青草和水的氣味,對岸有一群大學生在彈吉他,幾個小孩光著腳丫在淺水裡追逐,水花濺起來在陽光下閃著碎光。我一邊啃豆餅一邊看著這一切,忽然覺得自己好像沒有在「旅行」,而是在過一個很普通、卻很扎實的星期六下午。後來我沿著河岸走進京都御苑,腹地很大,古松與砂地小徑之間幾乎沒有遊客,只有幾位老人牽著狗散步,還有一位穿和服的婦人坐在樹蔭下打盹。我也學她找了張長椅,瞇了大概二十分鐘的午覺,醒來時嘴角還沾著豆餅的糯米屑。

午後三點:船岡溫泉,一場真正的在地入浴

午後三點,我搭巴士從烏丸今出川一路搖晃到千本鞍馬口,車程大約十五分鐘,單程二百三十日圓,下車後沿著紫式部通往西走五分鐘,轉進一條窄窄的住宅巷,遠遠就能看到船岡温泉那棟大正時代風格的木造建築,屋簷雕著唐破風,外牆磁磚是那種帶點褪色感的藍綠色。這是一間有超過百年歷史的錢湯,一九九七年被登錄為國家有形文化財,但外觀完全沒有觀光地的架勢,門口只掛著一塊寫著「元湯」的暖簾,和幾台老舊的投幣式置物櫃。

我在門口的自動售票機投入四百九十日圓,拿票交給坐在番台的阿伯,他抬頭看了我一眼,又低頭繼續看手上的體育報,沒有多問一句。更衣室裡飄著淡淡的樟腦丸味道,幾位常客熟門熟路地把衣服疊得整整齊齊放進竹籃,我學著他們的樣子脫到全裸——這裡沒有更衣巾遮掩的習慣,想融入就得放下害羞。浴場裡有一整排洗澡椅,牆上貼著磁磚拼成的富士山圖案,已經有點斑駁掉色,反而更有味道。我先坐在小凳子上把身體徹底沖洗乾淨,這是進浴池前的鐵則,沒有人會在沒洗澡的狀況下直接下水,若不遵守很容易招來白眼。

浴池分成好幾種:藥湯、電気風呂、露天風呂,還有一個貼滿彩色磁磚的深池。我第一次嘗試「電気風呂」,那是會通微弱電流的浴池,泡進去的瞬間肌肉會不自覺抽動,一位常客大叔看我一臉驚恐,笑著用簡單的英文說「マッサージ,マッサージ」,示意這是老人家舒緩筋骨用的,要我放輕鬆坐穩。露天風呂在後院,抬頭是一小片被屋簷切割出來的天空,傍晚的風吹來帶著涼意,和熱水的溫度形成一種很舒服的對比,我整個人幾乎要在裡面睡著。前後泡了將近五十分鐘,出來時皮膚泛紅、微微發燙,喝了一瓶置物櫃旁販賣機賣的咖啡牛奶,冰涼的甜味滑進喉嚨,是那種在健身房或溫泉旅館都複製不出來的滿足感。

在船岡溫泉泡湯的五十分鐘裡,我才真正卸下觀光客的身份,變成巷子裡一個普通的鄰居。

傍晚到深夜:西陣巷弄的地蔵盆與晚餐尾聲

洗完澡走出船岡温泉,已經接近傍晚五點半,天色開始染上一層淡紫色。我沿著西陣的巷弄漫無目的地走,這一帶是京都傳統織品「西陣織」的發源地,巷子兩側還留著不少町家式樣的老屋,格子窗、犬矢来、暖簾一應俱全。走到一條窄巷口時,我看到幾位婆婆媽媽正忙著在一座小小的地藏菩薩石像前搭起紅白帳篷,掛上一串串紙燈籠,地上鋪著涼蓆,還擺了一張矮桌,上面堆著糖果餅乾。

我好奇地站在巷口張望,一位穿著碎花圍裙的婆婆注意到我,笑著用不太流利的英文夾雜日文跟我解釋,這是「地蔵盆」,每年八月下旬京都各個町內都會為守護孩子的地藏菩薩舉行的小型祭典,附近的小孩會聚在這裡玩遊戲、吃點心,晚上還有簡單的誦經儀式,感謝地藏菩薩一整年守護巷弄裡的孩童平安。她說這個習俗只有在京都和附近的近畿地區還留得很完整,東京幾乎已經看不到了。她一邊說一邊遞給我一顆包裝好的水果糖,說「一緒に食べて」,要我也一起沾沾福氣。我蹲下來看那尊被歲月磨得圓潤的地藏石像,臉上還畫著淡淡的紅色唇彩,是附近居民每年重新上色的痕跡,忽然覺得,所謂「在地生活」,大概就是這種不需要翻譯、卻能被一顆糖果傳達清楚的溫度。

晚上七點多,肚子開始咕嚕作響,我走進附近一間只有六張吧檯座位的小店「西陣 花結び」,老闆娘一個人掌廚,黑板上用粉筆寫著當天的おばんざい家常菜:芋頭燉煮、小芋莖涼拌、鯖魚味噌煮,還有一道京都夏天常見的加茂茄子田楽。我點了一份おまかせ三品套餐,加一杯地元的伏見清酒,整套下來大約兩千兩百日圓。芋頭燉得軟爛入味,一口咬下幾乎不需要咀嚼;加茂茄子那道田楽味噌塗得厚實,帶著微微炙燒的焦香,配著微溫的清酒,喉頭一路暖到胃裡。老闆娘聽到我是自己一個人來京都慢慢晃,笑著說「京都不是用來趕的城市,是用來『住』的城市,就算只有一天也一樣」。這句話我一直記到現在。

晚餐後,我又走回鴨川邊,夜裡的河岸安靜下來,只剩下遠處納涼床上傳來的談笑聲和杯盤碰撞的細碎聲響。我在堤防上坐了很久,想著這一天:市場裡剛煎好的蛋捲、咖啡館裡老先生的棒球話題、河灘上啃豆餅的自己、船岡溫泉裡微微顫動的電気風呂,還有那顆地藏盆婆婆遞給我的水果糖。我沒去清水寺,也沒去嵐山,卻覺得自己這一次,才真正走進了京都。

ENGLISH

At five-thirty in the morning I stood by the window of my second-floor room, watching Kyoto's sky hover somewhere between blue and grey. Not even the birds had started their noise yet. I told myself that today would have no itinerary, no landmarks to chase — I simply wanted to live one day the way a Kyoto local might. This was my third visit to the city. The first two times I came for the maple leaves at Kiyomizu-dera and the bamboo groves of Arashiyama, and I took a lot of photographs, but something always felt missing, as if I had merely walked through an enormous film set. This time I buried my camera at the bottom of my bag and gave myself an entire day to do only what ordinary Kyoto people do: visit the market, drink a cup of coffee, soak in a neighborhood bathhouse, and stumble into a small ritual happening in someone's back alley.

Five-Thirty in the Morning: Before the Kamo River Wakes

At five-thirty, the sound of the Kamo River was clearer than any traffic. I left my hotel near Oike-dori and walked north along the riverside path, gravel crunching softly under my shoes. A thin mist hung over the water, and a few herons stood motionless in the shallows like statues that hadn't quite woken up yet. Locals jogged past in twos and threes, unhurried, none of them wearing headphones — just the sound of cloth shoes on stone and the occasional exhale of breath. I sat down near Kamo Ohashi bridge and watched the first light cut sideways across the Higashiyama ridge, staining the river a pale orange-pink for barely ten minutes before it faded. That brief light was enough to wake me completely. I realized then that a Kyoto morning is a luxury built entirely out of quiet. Around six-thirty I turned west along Kawaramachi-dori, heading toward a Nishiki Market that hadn't fully opened yet, passing old wooden machiya townhouses where dim light leaked through lattice windows and the clatter of spatulas against pans drifted out into the street — shopkeepers preparing for the day ahead.

Eight O'Clock: Coffee and the Rhythm of Kyoto's Craftsmen

By eight o'clock I had wandered into Nishiki Market, the covered arcade known as 'Kyoto's kitchen.' At this hour maybe a third of the stalls were open, the tourists hadn't arrived yet, and I could actually see each old shop for what it really was. I stopped in front of Miki Keiran, a shop that has sold nothing but egg dishes for generations. From seven-thirty they grill fresh dashimaki tamago, a rolled dashi omelet cut into six pieces and skewered on a stick, for about 350 yen. The owner folded the egg mixture layer by layer into a square copper pan with practiced speed, and the smell of egg and bonito dashi filled the entire street. My first bite was faintly caramelized on the outside and as tender as custard within; the salty-sweet dashi dissolved on my tongue and sent a warmth down my throat that settled somewhere in my chest. It might be the best street breakfast I've ever eaten.

After the omelet I walked ten minutes south along Teramachi-dori to Inoda Coffee's main shop, founded in 1940. Kissaten culture in Kyoto matters just as much as the coffee itself. Most of the regulars here are middle-aged or elderly men in suits or work clothes, each sitting at what is clearly 'their' table, and the staff seem to know their orders without asking. I ordered the house specialty, 'Arabia no Shinju' — a coffee already mixed with milk and sugar in the traditional ratio, no adjustments needed, a habit Inoda has kept for more than seventy years. It was smooth with a faintly bitter finish, and I paired it with their butter toast, crisp at the edges and soft in the middle, the butter melting the instant it touched the bread. Two elderly men at the next table were discussing last night's baseball game in a tone as unhurried as the coffee itself, in no rush to be anywhere. That was the moment I understood: Kyoto people don't 'go for' coffee, they 'live inside' the coffeehouse for a slice of their morning.

People in Kyoto don't go out for coffee — they set aside a piece of the morning and live inside the coffeehouse.

Noon: A Picnic in the Heart of the Imperial Park

At noon I took the Keihan line to Demachiyanagi Station, where the Kamo and Takano rivers meet to form a small delta the locals call 'Kamogawa Delta,' a place where Kyoto families picnic and children splash on weekends. Beside the station stands Demachi Futaba, selling mame-mochi since 1899, the kind of shop you can smell red bean paste from before you even see it. I waited in line for about twenty minutes, mostly behind grandmothers carrying cloth bags who looked like regulars, buying their mochi and disappearing straight into the side streets — clearly not tourists. I bought two, at 220 yen each. The pale pink mochi skin, studded with salted pea grains, gave way first to a savory bite before the sweetness of the red bean filling slowly surfaced, the two flavors tugging against each other and then merging, so good I almost didn't want to swallow the last piece.

I carried my mochi down to the delta itself, where the river splits into two channels. I hopped across on the turtle-shaped stepping stones and found a flat rock to sit on. The river breeze carried the smell of grass and water; on the opposite bank a group of university students played guitar, and a few barefoot children chased each other through the shallows, water catching the sunlight in little bursts. Eating my mochi and watching all this, I suddenly felt like I wasn't 'traveling' at all — just living an ordinary, solid Saturday afternoon. Afterward I walked into Kyoto Gyoen, the old imperial park, whose grounds are so vast that between the ancient pines and sandy paths there were almost no visitors at all — just a few elderly people walking dogs, and a woman in a kimono dozing under a tree. I followed her example, found a bench, and dozed for about twenty minutes myself, waking up with sticky rice crumbs still at the corner of my mouth.

Three in the Afternoon: Funaoka Onsen, a Real Neighborhood Bathhouse

At three in the afternoon I took a bus from Karasuma-Imadegawa, rattling along for about fifteen minutes to Senbon-Kuramaguchi, a one-way fare of 230 yen. From there I walked five minutes west along Murasakishikibu-dori and turned into a narrow residential lane, where the Taisho-era wooden building of Funaoka Onsen came into view from a distance — a karahafu gable carved into the eaves, and exterior tiles in a faded blue-green that has clearly seen a century of weather. This bathhouse is more than a hundred years old and was registered as a national tangible cultural property in 1997, yet it carries none of the polish of a tourist attraction. A noren curtain reading 'Motoyu' hangs at the entrance, next to a few worn coin-operated lockers.

I fed 490 yen into the ticket machine at the door and handed the ticket to the old man sitting at the bandai counter. He glanced up at me once, then went back to reading his sports newspaper without asking a single question. The changing room smelled faintly of mothballs, and several regulars folded their clothes neatly into bamboo baskets with the ease of long habit. I did the same and undressed completely — there is no tradition here of covering yourself with a small towel, and if you want to belong, you have to let go of the shyness. Inside, a row of washing stools faced tiled walls painted with a fading mural of Mount Fuji, its colors chipped in places, which only made it more charming. I scrubbed myself thoroughly at the stool before entering any bath, an unbreakable rule here — nobody enters the water without washing first, and skipping it draws immediate disapproving looks.

The baths came in several varieties: a medicinal bath, a denki-buro or 'electric bath,' an open-air rotenburo, and a deep pool lined with colorful mosaic tiles. I tried the electric bath first, a pool that runs a faint current through the water; the moment I stepped in, my muscles twitched involuntarily. A regular, an older man nearby, laughed at my startled expression and said in simple English, 'Massage, massage,' explaining that older bathers use it to loosen their joints, and telling me to relax and sit still. The rotenburo was out back, open to a small rectangle of sky cut by the eaves; the evening air was cool against the heat of the water, a contrast so comfortable I nearly fell asleep in it. I soaked for almost fifty minutes altogether, came out with my skin flushed and warm, and bought a bottle of coffee milk from the vending machine by the lockers — its icy sweetness sliding down my throat gave a satisfaction no gym or ryokan spa has ever managed to replicate.

In those fifty minutes at Funaoka Onsen, I finally shed the identity of a tourist and became, for a while, just another neighbor in the alley.

Dusk into Night: A Jizo Festival in the Nishijin Backstreets

Stepping out of Funaoka Onsen, it was already nearly five-thirty and the sky had begun to take on a pale violet tint. I wandered aimlessly through the backstreets of Nishijin, the birthplace of Kyoto's famous textile weaving tradition, where many machiya townhouses still stand along the lanes — lattice windows, inuyarai bamboo guards, noren curtains, all still intact. At the mouth of one narrow alley, I saw a group of women setting up a red-and-white tent in front of a small stone Jizo statue, hanging strings of paper lanterns, laying out straw mats on the ground, and setting a low table piled with candy and crackers.

Curious, I stood at the entrance and watched. A woman in a floral apron noticed me and, in a mix of broken English and Japanese, explained that this was Jizo-bon, a small neighborhood festival held every year in late August across Kyoto's old districts to honor the Jizo bodhisattva who watches over children. Local kids gather here to play games and eat snacks, and in the evening there's a simple sutra chanting to thank Jizo for a year of protecting the children of the lane. She told me this custom survives fully only in Kyoto and the surrounding Kansai region — in Tokyo it has almost disappeared. As she spoke she handed me a wrapped fruit candy and said, 'Issho ni tabete' — eat one with us, share in the blessing. I crouched down to look at the Jizo statue, its stone surface worn smooth by time, its face still touched with a faint red on the lips — repainted, she said, by neighbors every year. It struck me that this is what 'local life' really means: something that needs no translation, something a single piece of candy can communicate perfectly.

Past seven that evening, my stomach growling, I ducked into a tiny six-seat counter restaurant called Nishijin Hanamusubi, run single-handedly by its owner. Chalked on a blackboard was the day's obanzai home cooking: simmered taro, dressed taro stems, mackerel simmered in miso, and a Kamo eggplant dengaku, a summer dish typical of Kyoto. I ordered a three-dish omakase set with a glass of local Fushimi sake, all for about 2,200 yen. The taro had been simmered until it barely needed chewing; the eggplant dengaku carried a thick, lightly charred miso glaze that paired perfectly with the warm sake, sending heat all the way down to my stomach. When the owner heard I was traveling through Kyoto alone, slowly, with no fixed plan, she laughed and said, 'Kyoto isn't a city built for rushing — it's a city built for living in, even if you only have one day.' I've been carrying that sentence with me ever since.

After dinner I walked back to the Kamo River. The banks had gone quiet for the night, with only faint laughter and the clink of glasses drifting down from the riverside dining platforms further along. I sat on the embankment for a long time, running through the whole day in my head: the freshly grilled omelet at the market, the old men's baseball talk in the coffeehouse, myself eating mochi on the riverbank, the faint tremor of the electric bath at Funaoka Onsen, and the fruit candy the Jizo-bon grandmother pressed into my hand. I never went to Kiyomizu-dera. I never went to Arashiyama. And yet, this time, I felt I had finally walked into Kyoto.
