中文

回到台北的第三天,我還是會在半夜醒來,以為聞到了丁香菸混著雞蛋花的味道。這篇筆記,是我在烏布待了九天之後,終於捨得寫下的私房路線——沒有網美鞦韆,沒有排隊排到馬路上的咖啡廳,只有當地人日常走的岔路、吃的攤子、拜的聖泉。如果你也厭倦了在同一張梯田照片前跟兩百個人擠在一起,這篇或許用得上。

清晨五點四十,坎普安之後的岔路

民宿主人尼歐曼前一晚提醒我,坎普安山脊(Campuhan Ridge)六點半以後會湧進帶著空拍機的旅行團,「想安靜,就趁公雞叫第二次的時候出門」。我五點四十摸黑走出比斯瑪路(Jalan Bisma)上的住處,空氣還帶著昨夜的雨水味,混著某戶人家已經點起的第一炷香——早晨的天神供養(canang sari)香氣,是烏布這個時間唯一的味道。

從古老的甘露河神廟(Pura Gunung Lebah)旁的階梯走上山脊,鋪好的水泥步道大概走了二十分鐘,天色從靛藍轉成灰粉色。就在步道兩座小丘之間、鋪面即將轉成碎石路的那個彎道,我遇見一位晨跑的當地大哥,他順手一指:「往右邊那條土路,才是我們自己走的路。」我照做了——那是一條僅容一人通過的泥土小徑,兩側長滿象草,踩下去帶著昨夜雨水的濕軟觸感,偶爾陷進小水窪,運動鞋整雙濕透也顧不得。

這條岔路通往一個地圖上幾乎查不到名字的小聚落,班基安西登(Bangkiang Sidem)。走了大約四十分鐘,梯田忽然在眼前展開,層層疊疊卻不是遊客熟悉的整齊弧線,而是隨地勢自然歪斜的形狀,水渠裡的水聲比想像中大聲,嘩嘩地灌進下一層田。一位光腳站在田埂上的農夫巴布·尼歐曼看到我,笑著用印尼語混英文問我要不要喝咖啡——他家就在田邊,是一間鐵皮搭的小屋。我们蹲在屋簷下,他用鋁製滴漏壺煮了峇里島本地產的羅布斯塔咖啡,粗粗的咖啡渣直接沉在杯底,苦得直接,配著他老婆炸的傳統糕點皮桑戈朗(pisang goreng),外皮酥脆、香蕉燙口而綿密。整趟來回大約五公里、耗時兩個半小時,全程沒有花一毛錢,除了那杯咖啡我硬塞給他的兩萬印尼盾(約新台幣四十元)。回到坎普安主步道時剛好八點,第一批遊覽團才正要下車。

真正的烏布,不在网红鞦韆上,而在那條連在地人才會轉進去的泥土岔路裡。

上午九點半,努玉光寧的鑿刻聲

沖完澡换了乾的衣服,我騎租來的機車往南騎了十分鐘,拐進猴林旁的努玉光寧村(Nyuh Kuning)。這裡沒有招牌寫著「傳統工藝村」,只有家家戶戶敞開的車庫改成的工作室,木屑味混著漆料的刺鼻氣息撲面而來。我在一間掛著半成品面具的院子前停下,主人瓦揚先生正蹲在矮凳上,用一把生鏽的鑿刀刻著林伽面具(Barong)的獠牙。他說這門手藝是跟他阿公學的,整座村子幾乎家家都做木雕,專供烏布市場與皇宮的儀式用具,「觀光客只買便宜的猴子雕像,真正的面具是要開光的,不能隨便賣。」他讓我摸了摸一尊剛完成、還沒開光的巴龍(Barong)面具,木頭表面溫潤,鬍鬚是真的馬尾毛,眼睛鑲了小片玻璃反光。院子角落的收音機放著甘美朗(gamelan)錄音,叮叮咚咚的金屬聲混著鑿刀敲打木頭的節奏,竟意外和諧。

正午,肯迪灣路上一碗傳說中的煙燻雞飯

肚子開始叫的時候剛好十一點半,我照著民宿主人畫在紙巾背面的地圖,沿著肯迪灣路(Jalan Raya Kedewatan)往烏班農莊(Ubud Hanging Gardens)方向騎,在一排民宅前找到「Warung Bu Mangku Nasi Ayam Kedewatan」——一間沒有招牌只掛著手寫紙板的小食堂,裡面擠著清一色講印尼語的當地人和摩托車。老闆娘布·芒姑站在大鍋前,親手把整隻水煮雞撕成細絲,拌進椰肉絲、辣椒與香料現場翻炒,油鍋滋滋作響,煙燻味從灶邊直衝而出。

我點了招牌的煙燻雞飯(nasi ayam kedewatan),四萬五千印尼盾(約新台幣九十元),配料一次上齊：白飯、雞絲、串烤肉沙嗲利里(sate lilit)、峇里島式蔬菜沙拉拌辣椒(lawar)、酥炸花生米,還有一小坨紅椒巴東生辣醬(sambal matah)。第一口雞肉帶著明顯的椰香與炭燻味,鹹中帶甜,是甜醬油(kecap manis)收的尾韻;沙嗲利里外層微焦,咬下去是碎肉混著椰漿與檸檬葉的清香;辣醬一入口先是酸,接著是薑黃與蝦醬堆疊出的後勁辣,逼得我一口氣灌了半杯冰茶(es teh manis)。整頓飯加飲料花了不到四萬八千印尼盾,吃完全身冒汗卻捨不得放下最後一口飯。店家只賣到食材售完為止,通常下午一點前就打烊——這也是為什麼遊覽大巴根本擠不進來,巷子太窄,他們寧可去有停車場的連鎖餐廳。

午後,塞巴圖聖泉的淨身儀式,以及黃昏白鷺歸巢

下午一點半,我騎車北上約二十分鐘,避開了觀光客擠爆的德哥拉朗梯田(Tegalalang),轉進更少人知道的塞巴圖聖泉神廟(Pura Gunung Kawi Sebatu)。門口一位頭髮花白的阿嬤賣著現做的供品籃(canang sari),十萬盾買一份足夠用一整個下午,裡面是椰葉編的小盒子裝著雞蛋花、米粒與一炷香。我在小竹屋換上租借的紗籠與腰帶(捐獻約兩萬盾),赤腳走進廟埕,水聲從一整排石雕出水口(pancoran)嘩啦啦流出,注入一方及腰深的池子,池底能看見信眾丟入的小銅板閃著光。

廟裡的祭司瑪德先生看我猶豫地站在池邊,主動走來,用簡單的英文教我:先在池邊合十默念心願,接著依序走過每一個出水口,把頭整個浸到冰涼的水柱下,吸一口氣、閉眼、讓水沖過頭頂,通常是奇數次——三、五、七——象徵洗去身上的「污穢」(leteh)。清晨兩百公尺外的班家(banjar)公所已經傳來甘美朗練習的叮咚聲,和水聲交疊在一起。第一柱水沖下來時冷得我倒抽一口氣,但沖過第五柱之後,那種發抖竟慢慢變成一種清醒——瑪德先生後來跟我解釋,這種淨化儀式(melukat)通常在迎接大型節慶如加隆安節(Galungan)或家廟週年慶(odalan)前舉行,峇里島人依循兩百一十天一輪的帕烏坤曆(Pawukon)推算每座廟的生日,而正值月經的女性依習俗是不能進入廟宇核心的——瑪德先生解釋這不是歧視,而是不同「潔淨狀態」在不同場域裡的分工,他說得平靜,沒有一絲說教的語氣,我聽完只是點頭,心裡卻對這套延續千年的邏輯多了一分敬意。

冰涼的泉水沖過頭頂那一刻,我才明白,峇里島人拜的不是神像,而是一種每天都要重新整理好的心。

換回乾衣服已近下午五點,我沒有直接回烏布,而是繞道經過佩圖魯白鷺村(Petulu)。村道兩側的大樹上,成千上萬隻白鷺正一批批飛回來過夜,翅膀拍打聲此起彼落,像是傍晚特有的鼓聲。當地人說這個現象從一九六五年動亂之後突然出現,他們相信是亡魂化身的守護鳥,從此全村立誓不准傷害牠們。我站在路邊,看著夕陽把整片樹梢染成橘紅,白鷺的剪影一隻接一隻落下,遠處班家公所的甘美朗排練聲隱約傳來,忽然覺得,這趟旅行真正留在身上的,不是任何一張打卡照,而是這些藏在清晨岔路、雕刻院子、辣醬碗底與泉水冰涼觸感裡,烏布人日復一日、認真活著的樣子。

ENGLISH

Three nights after landing back in Taipei, I still woke up at 2 a.m. convinced I could smell clove cigarettes and frangipani. This is the notebook I finally sat down to write after nine days in Ubud — no photogenic swings, no cafés with queues spilling onto the road, just the back roads locals actually walk, the stalls they actually eat at, and the spring they actually bathe in. If you're tired of fighting two hundred strangers for the same rice-terrace photo, keep reading.

5:40 a.m. — The Fork Past Campuhan Ridge

My homestay host, Nyoman, warned me the night before that Campuhan Ridge fills with drone-toting tour groups by 6:30. 'If you want quiet, leave when the rooster crows the second time,' he said. So at 5:40 I stepped out of my losmen on Jalan Bisma into air still damp from last night's rain, threaded with the smoke of the first canang sari offering someone down the lane had already lit — that faint incense is the only scent that belongs to this hour.

I climbed the stone steps beside Pura Gunung Lebah onto the ridge trail, walked the paved section for maybe twenty minutes as the sky shifted from indigo to dusty pink. Right at the bend between the two small hills, where the concrete gives way to gravel, a local jogger passing me pointed off the main path: 'That dirt track to the right — that's the one we actually use.' I took it. It was barely wide enough for one person, elephant grass brushing both shoulders, the mud from last night's rain sucking at my sneakers with every step until both shoes were soaked through and I stopped caring.

The track dropped into a hamlet you won't find labeled on most maps — Bangkiang Sidem. After maybe forty minutes of walking, the terraces opened up, but not in the tidy postcard curves everyone photographs; these leaned and folded with the natural slope of the land, and the irrigation channels ran loud, water slapping down into the next paddy. A barefoot farmer, Pak Nyoman — a different Nyoman, everyone here seems to be one — waved me over from his rice-field embankment and asked, in a mix of Indonesian and English, if I wanted coffee. His house was a tin-roofed shack right at the field's edge. We crouched under the eaves while he brewed local Robusta in a battered aluminum drip pot, grounds settling thick at the bottom of the glass, bitter and unfiltered, alongside his wife's fresh pisang goreng — crisp outside, the banana inside scalding and dense. The whole loop was about five kilometers and two and a half hours, cost nothing except the twenty thousand rupiah (roughly a dollar thirty) I insisted on leaving for the coffee. By the time I got back to the main ridge it was 8 a.m. sharp, and the first tour groups were just climbing off their vans.

The real Ubud isn't on the Instagram swing — it's down the dirt fork that only locals bother turning into.

9:30 a.m. — The Sound of Chisels in Nyuh Kuning

After a quick shower and dry clothes, I rode a rented scooter ten minutes south into Nyuh Kuning, the village tucked beside the Monkey Forest's back gate. There's no sign announcing 'traditional craft village' — just garage after garage turned into open-air workshops, the air thick with sawdust and the sharp bite of lacquer. I stopped in front of a courtyard hung with half-finished masks, where a carver named Pak Wayan crouched on a low stool, chiseling the fangs of a Barong mask with a rusted blade. He'd learned the trade from his grandfather, he told me, and the whole village still makes ceremonial pieces for temples and the royal palace. 'Tourists just buy the cheap monkey carvings,' he said. 'A real mask has to be blessed — you can't sell that casually.' He let me touch a finished, not-yet-consecrated Barong mask; the wood was warm under my fingers, the beard genuine horsehair, the eyes set with tiny slivers of glass that caught the light. A radio in the corner played a gamelan recording, its metallic chime tangling oddly beautifully with the rhythm of his chisel against wood.

Noon — A Bowl of Legendary Smoked Chicken Rice on Jalan Kedewatan

By 11:30 my stomach was growling, so I followed a map Nyoman had sketched on the back of a napkin, riding along Jalan Raya Kedewatan toward the Ubud Hanging Gardens until I found Warung Bu Mangku Nasi Ayam Kedewatan — a place with no proper sign, just a hand-lettered cardboard placard, packed entirely with Indonesian-speaking locals and parked motorbikes. The owner, Bu Mangku, stood over a giant wok, shredding an entire boiled chicken by hand and tossing it with grated coconut and spices right there over the flame, the smoke curling straight up into the rafters.

I ordered the signature smoked chicken rice — forty-five thousand rupiah, about ninety New Taiwan dollars — and it arrived loaded: white rice, shredded chicken, grilled minced-meat satay lilit, spiced vegetable lawar, crushed fried peanuts, and a spoonful of sambal matah on the side. The chicken hit first with coconut and smoke, salty with a sweet finish from kecap manis; the satay lilit was charred at the edges, its minced meat perfumed with coconut milk and lime leaf; the sambal started sour, then built into a slow burn of turmeric and shrimp paste that had me draining half a glass of iced sweet tea just to keep eating. The whole meal, drink included, came to under fifty thousand rupiah, and I finished sweating and reluctant to put down the last spoonful of rice. The warung only sells until the ingredients run out, usually closing by early afternoon — which is exactly why the tour buses never make it here; the lane's too narrow, and they'd rather stop somewhere with a parking lot.

Afternoon — A Purification Ritual at Sebatu, and Herons Coming Home at Dusk

At 1:30 I rode north for another twenty minutes, bypassing the tourist crush at Tegalalang entirely and turning instead into Pura Gunung Kawi Sebatu, a spring temple far fewer visitors bother finding. An elderly woman at the entrance sold hand-made canang sari offerings — a hundred thousand rupiah bought enough to last the afternoon, each one a little palm-leaf tray of frangipani, rice grains, and a stick of incense. I changed into a rented sarong and sash in a small bamboo hut for a twenty-thousand-rupiah donation, then walked barefoot onto the temple grounds, where a row of carved stone spouts, pancoran, poured water into a waist-deep pool, coins glinting on the tiled bottom where worshippers had tossed them.

A temple priest, Pak Made, noticed me hesitating at the pool's edge and came over, explaining in simple English: press your palms together at the water's edge and silently state your intention, then move down the line of spouts, ducking your whole head under each stream, usually in odd numbers — three, five, seven — to wash away leteh, spiritual impurity. From a banjar hall two hundred meters off, a gamelan rehearsal was already clinking away, its rhythm bleeding into the sound of falling water. The first jet of water knocked the breath out of me with cold; by the fifth, the shivering had turned into something closer to clarity. Pak Made told me afterward that this ritual, melukat, is usually performed before major ceremonies like Galungan or a temple's odalan anniversary, dates calculated on the 210-day Pawukon calendar; and that women during menstruation traditionally don't enter the temple's inner grounds — not out of judgment, he said evenly, but as a division of 'states of purity' assigned to different spaces and times. He said it without a trace of lecturing, and I just nodded, feeling an odd new respect for a logic that has held for a thousand years.

The moment that cold water hit the crown of my head, I understood — the Balinese don't pray to a statue. They pray to a mind they rebuild, every single day.

By the time I'd changed back into dry clothes it was almost 5 p.m., and instead of heading straight back to Ubud I detoured through Petulu, the heron village. Thousands of white herons were streaming back into the trees lining the road for the night, wingbeats overlapping like a percussion section warming up. Locals told me the phenomenon appeared suddenly after the political violence of 1965, and the village has sworn ever since never to harm the birds, believing them to be guardian spirits. I stood at the roadside as the sunset turned the treetops the color of embers, silhouette after silhouette of herons dropping into the branches, the gamelan rehearsal from the banjar hall drifting faintly through it all. It struck me then that nothing I'd carry home from this trip would be a photo — it would be all of this: a muddy fork at dawn, sawdust in a carver's courtyard, sambal burning at the bottom of a bowl, and the cold shock of holy water — the small, daily seriousness with which Ubud simply keeps living.
