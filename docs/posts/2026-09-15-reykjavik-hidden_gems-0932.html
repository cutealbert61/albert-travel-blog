中文

飛機降落在凌晨,雷克雅維克機場外的天色像是被水洗過的灰藍色,我拖著行李箱站在巴士站牌前,呼出的白氣還沒散開,已經聞到空氣裡那股熟悉的、混著海水與淡淡硫磺的味道。這不是我第一次來冰島,但這次不一樣——我把地圖上所有寫著「必訪」的景點都收進口袋深處,決定只跟著當地朋友埃爾娜(Erla)手寫給我的清單走,那張紙上密密麻麻的地名,連我手機裡的翻譯軟體都得停頓幾秒才反應過來。接下來的四天,我沒有踏進藍湖一步,卻在雷克雅維克的巷弄、泳池與潮汐之間,找到了一種更貼近這座城市真實呼吸的方式。

清晨港邊的秘密早餐

第一天早上七點十五分,天色還沒完全亮,我沿著格蘭達碼頭(Grandagarður)走,腳下的木棧道被夜裡的濕氣浸得發黑,踩上去發出輕輕的悶響。這裡沒有遊客,只有幾艘漆著紅白油漆的漁船正卸貨,柴油與海水混合的氣味撲面而來,海鷗在頭頂盤旋卻異常安靜,好像也還沒睡醒。我要找的地方叫Kaffivagninn,一家1935年就開在碼頭邊的老餐館,據說是雷克雅維克現存最老的餐廳,招牌小到我走過去了才折返。推門進去,暖氣夾著咖啡香撞上臉,裡頭坐著七、八個穿著橘色工作服的漁工,沒有人抬頭看我這個顯然是外來客的東方臉孔,倒是老闆娘直接遞了菜單過來,用英文問我:「魚湯,還是魚湯?」我笑了,點了那碗fiskisúpa魚湯,價格是2400冰島克朗(約新台幣550元),配一片抹了厚厚黃油的黑麥麵包。湯端上來時還冒著煙,裡頭是鱈魚和黑線鱈的碎肉,湯底是番茄與奶油調和的,酸中帶甜,魚肉入口即化沒有一絲腥味,喝到底部還能撈到切得極細的胡蘿蔔絲。咖啡是續杯不用加錢的老規矩,老闆娘每隔十分鐘就會巡一次桌,不問你要不要,直接倒。我坐在窗邊,看漁工們用冰島語低聲聊天,聽不懂內容,但那種節奏——短促、帶點喉音、偶爾爆出笑聲——比任何觀光導覽都更讓我覺得「這才是雷克雅維克」。

真正的雷克雅維克,不在旅遊書的地圖上,而在漁工們每天早上喝的那碗魚湯裡。

西城泳池:雷克雅維克人的客廳

當天下午三點,我按埃爾娜的指示走到西城區(Vesturbær)一條完全沒有招牌指引的巷子裡,一棟毫不起眼的黃色建築就是Vesturbæjarlaug社區泳池。入場費1200冰島克朗,櫃檯的阿姨看我拿著相機,搖搖手指說「裡面不能拍」,語氣不嚴厲,但那種篤定讓我立刻收起相機。更衣室門口貼著一張圖示,清楚標明進池前必須全裸淋浴,這是冰島泳池文化裡不容妥協的規矩——不是禮貌建議,是硬性規定,連牆上的警示牌都寫著「工作人員有權檢查」。我站在蓮蓬頭下,聽著隔壁老先生們邊沖水邊用冰島語討論昨晚的議會新聞,毫無尷尬,彷彿這裡就是他們每天固定的社交場合。泳池外的熱水池(heitur pottur)才是重點,水溫42度,我一腳踩下去整個人瞬間放鬆,池邊坐著三個看起來七十歲上下的老太太,其中一位叫西格倫(Sigrún)的主動跟我聊起來,她說自己在這個泳池泡了四十年,「這裡沒有老闆也沒有員工,大家都只穿一件泳衣,誰也看不出誰的銀行帳戶。」她告訴我這叫pottaspjall,「熱水池閒聊」,是冰島人真正討論國家大事、八卦鄰居、抱怨天氣的地方,比咖啡館更誠實,因為沒有人穿著西裝來裝腔作勢。夕陽從雲層縫隙斜射進來,水面泛著一層淡金色,蒸氣裊裊升起,我聽著她們用冰島語笑罵著誰家的女婿又遲到,雖然一句都聽不懂,卻覺得自己被邀進了這座城市最柔軟的內部。

火山丘與地熱沙灘:一條沒有導遊會告訴你的路線

第二天清晨九點,我從市中心的BSÍ巴士站出發,沒有搭車,直接徒步走進歐斯克赫利森林(Öskjuhlíð),這是一座人工造林的火山丘,山頭上頂著珍珠樓(Perlan)那顆銀色球體,但我要走的路線不通往那裡,而是繞進樹林深處的碎石小徑。走了大約十五分鐘,兩側樹木漸密,空氣裡松針味變濃,忽然眼前出現一個半掩埋在土坡裡的水泥碉堡入口,鐵門鏽蝕但沒鎖,這是二戰時期英美駐軍留下的防空設施遺跡,埃爾娜特別提醒我一定要看,因為連很多冰島年輕人都不知道它存在。裡頭黑得什麼都看不見,我用手機手電筒照了照,牆上還留著模糊的塗鴉字跡,潮濕的水泥味撲鼻,忽然一陣風從通氣孔灌進來,把我嚇得往後退了兩步。走出森林後繼續往南,約二十分鐘後坡度開始下降,眼前忽然開闊,是Nauthólsvík地熱人工沙灘——這裡免費入場,只有想使用置物櫃或租毛巾才要付費(約600冰島克朗),沙灘用地熱水加溫成一個半月形的溫水潟湖,與旁邊冰冷的大西洋僅隔著一道防波堤。我脫了鞋踩進潟湖,水溫大約攝氏18到20度,再往外幾步跨進大西洋的海水,瞬間像被冰針刺了一下,溫差之大讓我忍不住尖叫出聲,岸上曬太陽的冰島爸爸帶著孩子哈哈大笑,顯然這是他們熟悉的日常把戲。整趟路線我走了大約五公里,花了將近三小時,建議挑平日上午前往,週末下午本地家庭會擠滿沙灘;出發前最好查一下潮汐時間,退潮時沙灘面積會大很多,體驗完全不同。

一腳踩進溫熱的潟湖,另一腳跨進冰冷的大西洋,那一瞬間的溫差,就是冰島這座島嶼性格的縮影。

格里特小巷與黑色木屋:雷克雅維克最後一個老鎮

第三天下午,我特意避開勞加維格大街(Laugavegur)人潮最擠的路段,轉進市政廳後方的格里特小鎮(Grjótaþorp),這是雷克雅維克僅存的一片十九世紀老木屋街區,房子低矮,漆成深綠、暗紅與煤黑色,窗台上擺著凍得半僵的天竺葵盆栽。我推門走進Skólavörðustígur街上一間毫不張揚的黑膠唱片店12 Tónar,店裡飄著淡淡的木頭與紙張氣味,老闆一如往常招呼客人「先坐下,聽完再決定買不買」,遞給我一杯免費的黑咖啡,讓我窩在一張破舊的皮沙發上戴著耳機翻唱片。旁邊一位六十來歲、留著灰白鬍子的男人自我介紹叫比亞尼(Bjarni),是個玩了三十年民謠吉他的當地音樂人,聊開後他問我知不知道Þorrablót——那是每年一月底、二月初的冰島「仲冬節」,人們會穿上手織的傳統羊毛衫lopapeysa,聚集在社區活動中心,吃發酵鯊魚肉hákarl和黑死酒brennivín,一邊唱古老的民謠歌曲,一邊用力拍桌子打拍子。他笑著形容那股鯊魚肉的氨水味「第一次聞到會讓你想奪門而出,但吞下去配一口烈酒,就懂了為什麼老祖先靠這個活過漫長的冬天。」他說這個節慶其實不是為了觀光表演,而是社區真正聚在一起、互相確認彼此還活著、還能笑的方式。走出唱片店時天色已經轉暗,巷子裡路燈一盞一盞亮起,木屋窗內透出溫暖的黃光,我忽然明白,這座城市的靈魂不在寬闊的大馬路上,而是藏在這些窄巷與願意跟你多聊十分鐘的陌生人之間。

格羅塔角的低語:潮汐、燈塔與極光

最後一晚,我搭上11號公車到塞爾蒂雅納內斯半島(Seltjarnarnes),沿著海岸步道走向格羅塔角(Grótta)。出發前我特別查了潮汐表——這座小燈塔立在一座潮間島上,只有退潮時才能徒步走過去,漲潮時那條沙徑會完全被海水吞沒,埃爾娜再三提醒我千萬別算錯時間困在島上。晚上九點抵達時,九月的天色仍帶著一層淡淡的藍紫色餘光,風很大,帶著濃重的海藻與鹹味,遠處有一群絨鴨在礁石間浮沉,叫聲低沉斷續。步道盡頭有一座小巧的地熱足浴池,免費使用,幾個當地人正捲起褲管把腳泡進去,邊喝著保溫瓶裡的熱可可邊聊天,完全不理會遠處天空是否會有極光,對他們來說這就是週三晚上再平常不過的散步行程。我也脫了鞋泡進去,溫熱的水包住腳踝,抬頭望向漸漸暗下來的天空,雲層邊緣泛起一絲極光特有的淡綠色光暈,雖然那晚極光最終沒有真正爆發成肉眼可見的大片舞動,但那種等待本身——海風、溫泉、遠處燈塔的白光規律地掃過海面——已經讓我明白,雷克雅維克真正的魅力從不在於你打勾了多少必訪清單,而在於你願不願意放慢腳步,坐進一池溫水裡,跟這座城市一起,靜靜地等。

等待極光的那半小時,我才明白,雷克雅維克教會我的不是怎麼旅行,而是怎麼慢下來。

回程巴士上,我把埃爾娜那張手寫清單重新摺好放進背包內袋,決定下次再來,還要跟她要一張新的。這座城市的私房秘境從來不是秘密景點的名單,而是一種願意被本地人邀請進入日常節奏的心情——碼頭邊的一碗魚湯,泳池裡的一句閒聊,潮間島上的一次等待,都比任何一張門票更讓我記得雷克雅維克的模樣。

ENGLISH

The plane touched down before dawn, and the sky outside Keflavík looked like it had been rinsed in grey-blue water. I stood at the bus stop dragging my suitcase, my breath still hanging in the air, already catching that familiar smell of salt water laced faintly with sulphur. This wasn't my first time in Iceland, but this trip was different — I'd tucked every 'must-see' landmark deep into my pocket and decided to follow only the handwritten list my local friend Erla had given me, a scrap of paper crammed with place names so obscure my translation app hesitated before spitting out a guess. Over the next four days I never once set foot in the Blue Lagoon, yet somewhere between Reykjavik's back alleys, neighborhood pools, and tidal shores, I found a way of breathing with this city that felt far more honest.

A Secret Breakfast by the Harbor

At 7:15 on the first morning, the sky still hadn't fully lifted as I walked along Grandagarður pier. The wooden boardwalk, dark and damp from the night's mist, gave a soft hollow thud under my boots. There were no tourists here, only a few red-and-white painted fishing boats being unloaded, the smell of diesel and seawater hitting me square in the face. Gulls circled overhead, unusually quiet, as if they too hadn't fully woken up. I was looking for a place called Kaffivagninn, a harborside diner that's been running since 1935 and is said to be the oldest restaurant still operating in Reykjavik — the sign was so small I walked past it and had to turn back. Inside, warm air thick with coffee hit my face; seven or eight men in orange work overalls sat scattered around, none of them looking up at the obviously foreign face that had just walked in. The owner simply slid a menu across the counter and asked in English, 'Fish soup, or fish soup?' I laughed and ordered the fiskisúpa for 2,400 ISK, served with a thick slice of rye bread slathered in butter. The soup arrived still steaming — flaked cod and haddock in a tomato-cream base, tangy with a hint of sweetness, the fish dissolving on the tongue without a trace of fishiness, finely shredded carrot settling at the bottom of the bowl. Coffee refills were free, an old house rule; the owner made her rounds every ten minutes without asking, simply pouring. I sat by the window watching the fishermen murmur to each other in low, guttural Icelandic, understanding none of it but somehow finding the rhythm — clipped, occasionally erupting into laughter — more revealing of this city than any guidebook could ever be.

The real Reykjavik isn't on any tourist map — it's in the bowl of fish soup the harbor workers eat every single morning.

The West Side Pool: Reykjavik's Living Room

At three that afternoon, following Erla's directions, I found myself on an unmarked lane in the Vesturbær district, where an unassuming yellow building housed Vesturbæjarlaug, a neighborhood swimming pool. Admission was 1,200 ISK. The woman at the counter noticed my camera and simply wagged a finger — no photos inside — her tone not harsh but absolute, and I put the camera away immediately. A sign on the changing room door made the rule explicit: full nude showering before entering the pool is mandatory in Icelandic pool culture, not a polite suggestion but a hard rule, with a warning that staff reserve the right to check. Standing under the showerhead, I listened to a group of elderly men beside me discussing last night's parliamentary news mid-rinse, utterly unselfconscious, as if this were their regular social club. The real destination, though, was the hot tub outside — heitur pottur, kept at 42 degrees Celsius. I stepped in and felt my whole body loosen instantly. Three women in their seventies sat at the edge; one, Sigrún, struck up a conversation, telling me she'd been soaking in this exact pool for forty years. 'There's no boss and no employee here,' she said. 'Everyone's wearing the same swimsuit, and you can't tell anyone's bank balance.' She called this ritual pottaspjall — 'hot-pot talk' — the place where Icelanders truly discuss politics, gossip about neighbors, and complain about the weather, more honest than any café because nobody shows up in a suit to posture. The sun slanted through a gap in the clouds, gilding the water's surface pale gold, steam curling upward as the women laughed and scolded someone's late son-in-law in Icelandic I couldn't understand a word of — yet I felt, somehow, invited into the softest, most private layer of this city.

The Volcanic Hill and the Geothermal Beach: A Route No Tour Guide Will Show You

At nine the next morning I set out on foot from the BSÍ bus terminal, skipping the bus entirely, and walked straight into Öskjuhlíð, a man-made forested hill capped by the silver dome of Perlan — though the path I was after didn't lead there. Instead I veered onto a gravel trail deep into the trees. About fifteen minutes in, the woods thickened, the air turning sharp with pine needles, when suddenly a concrete bunker entrance appeared, half-buried in the slope, its rusted iron door unlocked. This was a leftover World War II anti-aircraft installation from the British and American occupation forces — Erla had specifically told me to find it, since even many young Icelanders have no idea it exists. Inside was pitch black; my phone flashlight caught faded graffiti on the damp concrete walls, and a sudden draft through a vent made me jump back two steps. Emerging from the forest, I continued south, and after another twenty minutes the slope dropped and the view opened up onto Nauthólsvík, a geothermal artificial beach — free to enter, with lockers and towel rental available for about 600 ISK. The crescent-shaped lagoon is heated with geothermal water and separated from the icy Atlantic by only a low breakwater. I kicked off my shoes and stepped into the lagoon, the water around 18 to 20 degrees Celsius, then a few steps further into the open sea — the temperature shock hit like needles of ice, and I yelped loud enough that an Icelandic father sunbathing nearby with his kids burst out laughing, clearly a trick they play on themselves regularly. The full route took roughly three hours and five kilometers on foot. I'd recommend going on a weekday morning — weekends bring crowds of local families — and checking the tide tables beforehand, since low tide dramatically widens the sand and changes the whole experience.

One foot in the warm lagoon, the other in the freezing Atlantic — that single stride of temperature difference is Iceland's whole character, condensed into one second.

Grjótaþorp's Wooden Lanes: Reykjavik's Last Old Town

On the afternoon of the third day, I deliberately avoided the most crowded stretch of Laugavegur and turned instead into Grjótaþorp, the small pocket of nineteenth-century wooden houses tucked behind City Hall — the last surviving old quarter in Reykjavik. The houses sit low, painted deep green, dark red, and coal black, geraniums half-frozen in their window boxes. I ducked into 12 Tónar, an unassuming vinyl record shop on Skólavörðustígur, where the smell of wood and old paper hung faintly in the air. As always, the owner told me to sit down and listen before deciding whether to buy anything, handing me a free cup of black coffee as I sank into a worn leather sofa with headphones on, flipping through records. Beside me sat a grey-bearded man in his sixties named Bjarni, a folk guitarist of thirty years, who asked if I knew about Þorrablót — the midwinter festival held every January and February when Icelanders pull on their hand-knit lopapeysa sweaters and gather in community halls to eat fermented shark, hákarl, chased with the black death schnapps, brennivín, singing old folk songs while pounding the tables in rhythm. He laughed describing the ammonia bite of the shark, 'the first whiff makes you want to run for the door, but swallow it down with a shot of that schnapps and you'll understand how our ancestors survived the long dark winters.' The festival, he insisted, was never really for tourists to gawk at — it's how a community reaffirms, once a year, that everyone's still alive, still able to laugh together. By the time I left the shop, night had fallen and the streetlamps flicked on one by one along the lane, warm yellow light spilling from the wooden houses' windows, and I understood, finally, that this city's soul doesn't live on the wide main roads but in these narrow lanes and in strangers willing to talk to you for ten minutes longer than they need to.

The Whisper of Grótta: Tides, a Lighthouse, and the Northern Lights

On my last evening, I took bus number 11 out to the Seltjarnarnes peninsula and walked the coastal path toward Grótta lighthouse. Before setting out I'd checked the tide tables carefully — this small lighthouse sits on a tidal islet, only reachable on foot when the tide is out, and the sandy path back is completely swallowed by seawater at high tide. Erla had warned me more than once not to miscalculate and get stranded. I arrived around nine, and in September the sky still held a faint violet-blue afterglow. The wind was strong, thick with the smell of seaweed and salt, and a flock of eider ducks bobbed among the rocks, calling out in low, broken notes. At the end of the trail sat a small free geothermal footbath, where a few locals had rolled up their trousers to soak their feet, sipping hot cocoa from thermoses, entirely unconcerned about whether the aurora would appear — for them, this was simply an ordinary Wednesday evening stroll. I sat down and dipped my own feet in, the warm water wrapping around my ankles as I looked up at the darkening sky, catching the faint green shimmer along the cloud edges that hints at aurora activity. That night the lights never fully erupted into the sweeping dance you see in photographs, but the waiting itself — the sea wind, the warm water, the lighthouse beam sweeping steadily across the water in the distance — made something clear to me: Reykjavik's real charm was never about how many boxes you tick off a checklist. It's about whether you're willing to slow down, sink your feet into warm water, and simply wait alongside the city.

In that half hour of waiting for the lights, I realized Reykjavik hadn't taught me how to travel — it had taught me how to slow down.

On the bus back, I folded Erla's handwritten list carefully and tucked it into my backpack's inner pocket, already planning to ask her for a new one next time. This city's hidden gems were never really a list of secret sights — they were an invitation into the ordinary rhythm of local life: a bowl of fish soup by the harbor, idle chatter in a hot pool, a long wait on a tidal islet. Each of those moments, in the end, told me more about Reykjavik than any ticket ever could.
