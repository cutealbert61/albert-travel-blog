中文

飛機降落關西機場那天,天還沒亮透,我在機場巴士上打盹,直到司機用日語廣播「もうすぐ京都駅」才驚醒過來。這已經是我第三次來京都了。前兩次我跟著旅遊書按圖索驥,清水寺、伏見稲荷、嵐山竹林,每張照片都被人潮擠成同一張構圖,回家翻照片時甚至認不出哪張是哪天拍的。這一次,我把地圖收進背包最底層,聽從一位在一乘寺開了二十年舊書店的老闆娘的建議——她說「京都人自己會去的地方,才是真的京都」。接下來五天,我幾乎沒有踏進任何一張明信片會出現的畫面,卻意外撞見這座城市最真實的呼吸與體溫。

清晨五點半:法然院苔徑上的露水

第一天清晨五點半,我從一乘寺的町家民宿走出來,巷子裡還沒有一盞店燈亮起,只有自動販賣機發出微弱的嗡嗡聲。我沿著白川通往南走,轉進哲學之道,這條路白天會被舉著自拍棒的人潮塞滿,但此刻只有一位遛狗的老先生,狗繩鈴鐺叮叮作響,和著疎水道的水聲。走到若王子神社附近一個不起眼的岔路口,我拐進一條幾乎沒有路牌的小徑,五分鐘後,茅草屋頂的總門出現在眼前——法然院到了。

這座寺院不對外開放本堂,平常只能走進山門與白砂壇之間的那段參道,但正因為如此,幾乎沒有旅行團會安排這裡。清晨的空氣濕冷,青苔還帶著露水,踩在石階上會發出細微的「噗、噗」聲響,像是踩在濕海綿上。兩座白砂壇被耙成水紋的形狀,光線從杉樹縫隙斜斜切進來,落在砂面上形成一條會隨著時間移動的金線。我站在那裡大概十五分鐘,期間只有兩位當地老太太經過,對著本堂合掌後又靜靜離開,連交談都是氣音。那一刻我才明白,京都的美不在於景點本身多麼壯觀,而在於這種被留白出來的安靜。

京都的美不在於景點多麼壯觀,而在於那些被特意留白出來的安靜時刻。

離開法然院後,我沒有往銀閣寺的方向走,而是逆向往南禪寺後山繞了一小段,那裡有一條在地人晨跑的野徑,踩過去滿地都是松針,空氣裡有濕木頭與苔蘚混合的味道,像是剛掀開一罐放了很久的茶葉罐。走到六點四十分左右,太陽終於整個爬上東山稜線,金色的光斜斜灑在瓦片屋頂上,遠處傳來附近人家收音機播放的晨間新聞,那一刻我確定,這趟旅行選對了方向。

一整天的慢行:大原三千院與寂光院的隱藏路線

第二天我決定去大原,這裡是京都近郊的一個小盆地,對外國旅客來說偏遠又麻煩,正因如此,巴士上大多是拿著買菜籃的當地老太太。我在京都駅前的巴士總站搭上京都巴士17號,車程約莫五十五分鐘,單程票價560日圓,山路蜿蜒,窗外景色從市區的水泥公寓漸漸換成一片一片的水田與杉樹林。巴士在「大原」站放我下車時已經快十點,空氣裡有一股濕潤的草腥味,混著遠處燒柴的煙味。

從巴士站走到三千院大約十五分鐘,沿路是賣紫蘇漬物與栃餅的小攤,一位阿婆坐在自家門口曬柴魚片,看到我探頭張望,主動遞給我一小片試吃,鹹鮮帶點煙燻味,我後來買了一小包當伴手禮,約500日圓。三千院的入園費是700日圓,園內的杉苔庭園「有清園」是我此行看過最細膩的苔景,整片地面像鋪了一層天鵝絨,幾尊圓潤的小地藏石像半埋在苔蘚裡,表情溫柔,遊客紛紛蹲下來拍照,但因為地處偏遠,人潮遠比嵐山天龍寺稀薄許多,我甚至能找到一處緣廊角落,坐上整整二十分鐘,聽風吹過杉樹梢的聲音,像海浪一波波退去。

離開三千院後,我沒有原路折返,而是照著民宿老闆娘畫的手繪地圖,沿著呂川溪流往上游走。這條小路窄到只能容一人通行,溪水清澈見底,偶爾有蜻蜓低飛掠過水面,走了大約二十分鐘後接上另一條路,才抵達寂光院——這裡供奉著建禮門院的故事,寺院不大,入園費600日圓,遊客比三千院更少,我遇到的只有三三兩兩的當地攝影愛好者,扛著老式底片相機安靜地等光。整趟路線從巴士站到三千院、寂光院再走回巴士站,前後大概花了四個半小時,中途在路邊食堂吃了一碗野菜蕎麥麵,750日圓,湯頭清淡帶著柴魚香,配著剛採的紫萁野菜,那種樸素的滋味,是我在市區任何一間網紅蕎麥麵店都吃不到的。

巷弄裡的真滋味:出町ふたば的豆餅與進々堂的老木桌

第三天早上七點半,我特地繞去下鴨神社附近的出町ふたば排隊。這間創業超過百年的和菓子店,招牌是名代豆餅,外皮是帶點鹹味的紅豆麻糬,裡面包著顆粒分明的紅豆餡,黑豆整顆鑲在麻糬皮裡,咬下去先是麻糬微微的鹹,接著紅豆餡的甜緩緩化開,最後舌尖還能咬到黑豆本身微硬的顆粒感,層次遠比外觀樸素的長相豐富。一顆220日圓,我排了大約二十分鐘,隊伍裡幾乎都是拎著竹籃的附近婆婆媽媽,不是觀光客,她們熟練地一次買十顆帶回家當早餐,我學著她們的樣子買了四顆,坐在下鴨神社糺の森的長椅上,就著晨光一口氣吃掉兩顆,糯米的溫熱還留在指尖。

中午我搭公車到京都大學北門前的進々堂,這是一間開業近百年的老麵包店,店內那張據說出自民藝運動木工大師黑田辰秋之手的長木桌,已經被磨得油亮,坐下時能感覺到木頭表面細微的凹凸紋理。我點了咖哩麵包配一杯附咖啡套餐,約900日圓,咖哩麵包外皮酥脆帶油香,咬開後咖哩餡濃稠帶著淡淡孜然味,不辣但香氣足,配著微酸的黑咖啡剛好中和油膩感。隔壁桌是兩個京都大學的學生,攤開筆記本小聲討論功課,店裡沒有人大聲說話,只有咖啡機運轉與翻書頁的沙沙聲,那種安靜的日常感,讓我這個外地人也不自覺放低了說話音量。

在地人排隊買的,從來不是網美打卡點,而是他們每天早餐桌上都會出現的那一顆豆餅。

地蔵盆:西陣巷子裡教會我的京都人情

第四天傍晚,我原本只是想穿過西陣的織物工坊街區抄近路回民宿,卻在一條窄巷口被攔了下來——巷子兩側掛滿了紅色燈籠,地上鋪著藍色帆布,幾張矮桌排成一列,擺著西瓜、彈珠汽水與零食,十幾個孩子圍著一尊被裝飾得花花綠綠的地藏石像玩套圈圈。一位坐在門口搧扇子的歐巴桑看我探頭張望,笑著用簡單的英日夾雜對我說「今日は地蔵盆やで,入っておいで」,招手要我進去看看。

我後來才知道,地蔵盆是京都及近畿一帶特有的社區型祭典,每年八月下旬,各個町內會的居民會一起清洗、裝飾守護孩童的地藏石像,擺出供品,舉辦兩天的小型廟會,重點不在觀光,而是鄰里之間一年一度的聚會,連平常互不相識的住戶都會在這兩天搬椅子出來聊天。歐巴桑遞給我一碗冰鎮素麵,說是「附近太太們一起煮的」,不收錢,只讓我隨意投個香油錢到地藏前的木箱。那碗素麵冰得透心涼,沾醬帶著柴魚與淡淡薑味,吃完她又塞給我一支撈金魚的小網子,說小孩子都玩膩了,要我試試看。那一晚我在巷子裡待了將近兩小時,聽她講起這條巷子五十年前還是織布工廠聚集地的故事,聽著聽著,巷口的燈籠被晚風吹得輕輕搖晃,發出布料摩擦的細微聲響。

黃昏的尾聲:西陣さらさ與東寺弘法市的剪影

離開巷子後,我走去不遠處的さらさ西陣續攤——這間咖啡館的前身是1930年代的錢湯(公共澡堂),牆面保留著整片馬約利卡花磚,色彩斑斕的磁磚圖案在昏黃燈光下顯得特別溫潤。我點了一份咖哩飯配一杯生薑汁氣泡飲,合計約1400日圓,咖哩偏濃郁帶著蘋果的甜味,坐在原本應該是浴池位置的座位上用餐,有種奇妙的時空錯位感,店裡放的爵士樂音量剛好蓋過隔壁桌情侶的談話聲,卻又不至於吵。

隔天正好是每月21日,我特地起了個大早去東寺趕弘法市。清晨七點的東寺境內已經擺滿上千個攤位,賣舊和服、古董鐵壺、佛具、醃菜與烤糰子,攤主多半是上了年紀的京都人,討價還價全用京都腔進行,我用蹩腳的日文問一件藍染舊布多少錢,老闆娘笑著比手勢說算我1000日圓就好,還多送我一小塊碎布。整條市集逛完花了快三小時,五重塔就靜靜立在市集另一端,偶爾探頭出來,提醒我這裡其實是一座千年古寺,而不只是跳蚤市場。回程巴士上,我抱著一袋戰利品,想起這五天沒去清水寺,也沒去伏見稲荷的千本鳥居,卻覺得自己好像真的見過了京都的臉,而不只是它精心整理過的側臉。

晚上收拾行李時,我把那張老闆娘手繪的地圖攤在榻榻米上重新看了一遍,上面歪歪扭扭寫著幾個地名,有些字跡已經被我這幾天的汗水暈開。我忽然覺得,比起帶走多少張打卡照片,能帶走這樣一張被使用過的地圖,才是這趟京都私房行程給我最好的紀念品。

ENGLISH

The plane touched down at Kansai Airport before the sky had fully brightened, and I dozed on the airport bus until the driver's announcement of Kyoto Station jolted me awake. This was my third trip to Kyoto. The first two times I had followed the guidebooks obediently—Kiyomizu-dera, Fushimi Inari, the bamboo grove in Arashiyama—and every photo ended up looking the same, crowded into identical frames. This time I buried the map at the bottom of my backpack and took the advice of a woman who has run a secondhand bookshop in Ichijoji for twenty years: 'The places locals actually go to, that's the real Kyoto.' Over the next five days I never once stepped into a postcard scene, and instead stumbled onto something closer to the city's real pulse.

5:30 AM: Dew on the Moss Path at Honen-in

On my first morning I left my machiya guesthouse in Ichijoji at half past five, when not a single shop light was on in the alley and only a vending machine hummed faintly in the dark. I walked south along Shirakawa Street and turned onto the Philosopher's Path, a route so packed with selfie sticks by daytime that it's almost unrecognizable at dawn. At this hour there was only an old man walking his dog, its collar bell ringing faintly against the sound of the canal water. Near an unmarked fork by Nyakuoji Shrine, I turned down a narrow lane with no signage at all, and five minutes later the thatched main gate appeared in front of me—Honen-in.

The main hall itself is closed to visitors most days, so all you can really do is walk the short stretch between the gate and the two white sand mounds—which is exactly why tour buses never bother stopping here. The morning air was cold and damp, the moss along the stone steps still beaded with dew, letting out a faint squelch with every step, like walking on a wet sponge. The two sand mounds had been raked into rippling patterns, and light slanted in through the cedar trees, laying a thin gold line across the sand that shifted slowly as I watched. I stood there for almost fifteen minutes; only two elderly local women passed through, pressed their palms together toward the hall, and left without a word louder than a breath. That was when it hit me: Kyoto's beauty isn't in how spectacular a site looks, but in the silence deliberately left unfilled around it.

Kyoto's beauty isn't in how spectacular a place looks, but in the silence deliberately left unfilled around it.

Instead of heading toward Ginkaku-ji afterward, I doubled back toward the hills behind Nanzen-ji, following a dirt trail locals use for morning runs. Pine needles covered the ground, and the air carried a mix of wet wood and moss, like opening a tin of tea leaves left sealed for years. By around 6:40, the sun finally cleared the ridge of Higashiyama, casting gold light across the tiled rooftops below, and somewhere nearby a radio was playing the morning news through an open window. That was the moment I knew I'd chosen the right direction for this trip.

A Full Day in Ohara: The Hidden Route to Sanzen-in and Jakko-in

On day two I headed to Ohara, a small basin valley on Kyoto's outskirts that's inconvenient enough for most foreign tourists to skip entirely—which is exactly why the bus was full of local grandmothers carrying grocery baskets. I caught Kyoto Bus No. 17 from the terminal in front of Kyoto Station; the ride took about fifty-five minutes and cost 560 yen one way, winding uphill as the view outside shifted gradually from concrete apartment blocks to terraced rice paddies and cedar forest. By the time the bus dropped me at the Ohara stop it was nearly ten, and the air smelled of damp grass mixed with distant woodsmoke.

It's about a fifteen-minute walk from the bus stop to Sanzen-in, past small stalls selling shiso-pickled vegetables and grilled mochi. One elderly woman sitting outside her house drying bonito flakes noticed me peering over and handed me a small piece to try—salty, savory, faintly smoky—and I later bought a small bag as a souvenir for about 500 yen. Admission to Sanzen-in is 700 yen, and its cedar-moss garden, Yusei-en, was the most exquisite moss landscape I saw on this entire trip: the ground looked like it had been upholstered in green velvet, with a scattering of round little Jizo statues half-buried in the moss, their expressions oddly tender. Because the location is so remote, the crowds here are a fraction of what you'd find at Tenryu-ji in Arashiyama; I even found a quiet corner on the veranda where I sat for a full twenty minutes, listening to wind pass through the cedar canopy in waves, like tide pulling back from shore.

Rather than retracing my steps, I followed a hand-drawn map my guesthouse host had sketched for me, tracing the Rogawa stream upstream on a path barely wide enough for one person, the water clear enough to see the bottom, dragonflies occasionally skimming its surface. About twenty minutes later the path met another road leading to Jakko-in, a small temple tied to the story of Kenreimon-in. Admission is 600 yen, and the crowd here was even thinner than at Sanzen-in—just a couple of local photographers with old film cameras, quietly waiting for the light. The whole loop, from the bus stop to Sanzen-in, on to Jakko-in, and back, took about four and a half hours. Halfway through I stopped at a roadside noodle shop for a bowl of wild vegetable soba, 750 yen, the broth light and bonito-scented, topped with freshly picked fiddlehead ferns—a plainness you simply won't find at any trendy soba shop downtown.

Flavors of the Back Streets: Demachi Futaba's Mamemochi and Shinshindo's Old Wooden Tables

On the morning of day three, I got in line at 7:30 outside Demachi Futaba near Shimogamo Shrine. This confectionery, over a century old, is famous for its mamechi—a slightly salty red-bean mochi studded with whole black soybeans baked right into the dough. Biting in, you first taste the faint salt of the mochi skin, then the sweetness of the coarse-textured red bean paste unfolds slowly, and finally your teeth catch the firm little pop of a black soybean—a far more layered experience than its plain appearance suggests. Each one costs 220 yen. I waited about twenty minutes in a line made up almost entirely of neighborhood grandmothers carrying bamboo baskets, not tourists, buying ten at a time for the family breakfast table. I followed suit and bought four, then sat on a bench in the Tadasu no Mori forest by Shimogamo Shrine and ate two in the morning light, the warmth of the rice still lingering on my fingertips.

At lunchtime I took the bus to Shinshindo, a nearly hundred-year-old bakery just outside Kyoto University's north gate, known for its long communal wooden table said to have been crafted by Tatsuo Kuroda, a master woodworker of the mingei folk-craft movement, its surface worn smooth and faintly glossy under decades of elbows. I ordered a curry bread set with coffee for about 900 yen; the bread was crisp and faintly oily on the outside, giving way to a thick curry filling laced with a whisper of cumin—not spicy, just deeply fragrant—balanced nicely by the slightly bitter black coffee. At the next table, two Kyoto University students had their notebooks spread out, murmuring over homework, and the only sounds in the room were the hiss of the espresso machine and the soft rustle of turning pages. That kind of quiet made me, an outsider, instinctively lower my own voice.

What locals line up for was never the photogenic landmark—it's the same mochi that ends up on their breakfast table every single day.

Jizo-bon: What a Nishijin Alley Taught Me About Kyoto

On the evening of day four, I was only trying to cut through the textile workshop district of Nishijin on my way back to the guesthouse when I was stopped in my tracks at the mouth of a narrow alley. Red paper lanterns hung along both sides, blue tarps were laid over the ground, low tables lined up with watermelon, ramune soda, and snacks, while a dozen or so children crowded around a small Jizo stone statue decorated with colorful ribbons, playing a ring-toss game. An elderly woman fanning herself by her doorway noticed me peering in and called out in a mix of simple English and Japanese, 'It's Jizo-bon today—come on in and take a look,' waving me over.

I later learned that Jizo-bon is a neighborhood festival unique to Kyoto and the surrounding Kansai region, held every late August, in which residents of a single town block wash and decorate the local Jizo statue that watches over children, lay out offerings, and hold a small two-day gathering. It's not really for outsiders at all—it's the one time each year that even neighbors who barely speak to each other otherwise pull out chairs and sit together in the street. The woman handed me a bowl of chilled somen noodles, explaining that the neighborhood wives had made it together that afternoon, refusing payment except for a small coin dropped into the offering box in front of the Jizo. The noodles were ice-cold, the dipping sauce carrying a faint hint of ginger and bonito. When I finished, she handed me a little net for goldfish scooping, saying the kids had already gotten bored of it. I stayed in that alley for nearly two hours, listening to her recall how, fifty years ago, this same lane had been lined with weaving workshops, the lanterns swaying softly in the evening breeze with a sound like fabric brushing against fabric.

Dusk Interlude: Sarasa Nishijin and the Kobo-san Flea Market

After leaving the alley, I wandered to nearby Sarasa Nishijin, a café housed in a former 1930s public bathhouse, its walls still lined with vividly colored Art Nouveau majolica tiles glowing warm under the dim lighting. I ordered a curry rice set with a ginger soda for about 1,400 yen; the curry leaned rich and slightly sweet with apple, and eating it while seated roughly where the old bathtub would have been gave the whole meal a strange, pleasant sense of time displacement. The jazz playing over the speakers sat at just the right volume—loud enough to soften the couple's conversation at the next table, but never intrusive.

The next morning happened to fall on the 21st of the month, so I got up early to catch the Kobo-san flea market at To-ji Temple. By seven, the temple grounds were already packed with more than a thousand stalls selling secondhand kimono, antique iron kettles, Buddhist ritual objects, pickled vegetables, and grilled dango. Most of the vendors were older Kyoto locals, haggling in thick local dialect. I asked, in halting Japanese, how much an old indigo-dyed cloth cost, and the shopkeeper laughed and waved me off with a price of just 1,000 yen, tossing in a small scrap of extra fabric for free. It took nearly three hours to walk the whole market, the five-story pagoda standing quietly at the far end, occasionally visible through the stalls as a reminder that this was, underneath everything, still a thousand-year-old temple and not just a flea market. On the bus back, clutching my bag of small treasures, I realized I hadn't been to Kiyomizu-dera once in five days, nor to the thousand torii gates of Fushimi Inari—and yet I felt like I had finally seen Kyoto's actual face, rather than the carefully arranged profile it usually shows visitors.

That night, packing my bag, I spread the guesthouse owner's hand-drawn map out on the tatami one more time. A few place names were scrawled crookedly across it, some of the ink smudged from days of sweat and folding. It occurred to me then that carrying home a well-used map like this, rather than a phone full of landmark photos, was the best souvenir this quiet, private version of Kyoto could have given me.
