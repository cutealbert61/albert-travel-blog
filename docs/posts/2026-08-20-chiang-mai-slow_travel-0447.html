中文

我在清邁待的第五天,終於把相機收進背包裡。前四天我幾乎是用跑的:雙龍寺、寧曼路、大象保護區,一個景點接一個景點,回到民宿倒頭就睡,腦袋裡卻空空的,好像什麼都看了,又好像什麼都沒真正留下。直到那天清晨,我在鬧鐘響之前先醒了過來,聽見窗外傳來低沉綿長的鐘聲,才第一次覺得,自己終於「到」了清邁。

那是溫拉查隆寺(Wat Ket Karam)的晨鐘,而我住的民宿就在河邊的query Charoenrat路上——這是清邁最老的社區之一,曾是十九世紀外國商行與華人、印度商人聚居的碼頭區,如今白天安靜得像個尋常住宅巷弄,只有到了假日午後才會冒出幾間文青咖啡館。我選這裡落腳,不是因為它上鏡,而是因為它「活著」:巷口有人在洗衣服,廟埕有狗趴著睡覺,連早餐攤都懶得寫招牌。

五點半,巷口的鐘聲喚醒清邁

清晨五點半,天色還是那種介於藍與灰之間的曖昧顏色,我光腳踩在民宿外還帶著夜涼的水泥地上,一路走向巷口。空氣裡有股潮濕的茉莉香混著隔壁人家煮飯的柴煙味,遠遠就看見一列橘紅色的身影安靜地從巷子那頭走來——是附近廟裡出來托鉢的僧侶,大約七、八位,赤腳、低頭、缽抱在胸前,腳步聲幾乎聽不見,只有偶爾涼鞋踏過水窪的輕響。

巷口一位賣糯米飯的阿嬤看我站在那裡手足無措,笑著招手要我過去。她的小攤子只有一個保溫飯鍋、一疊香蕉葉包和幾串小蕉,一份二十泰銖。她教我把鞋子脫下、跪坐在墊子上,雙手捧著飯糰,等僧侶經過時輕輕放進缽裡,而且提醒我——女性絕對不能直接碰到僧侶的身體或衣角,遞供品前要先確認對方有沒有攤開布巾接應。整個過程安靜到我幾乎不敢呼吸,直到一位年輕僧侶低聲用巴利語念了一小段祝福,我才意識到自己剛剛完成了一件泰國人每天在做、卻對我來說無比陌生的事。

阿嬤告訴我,這不是表演給遊客看的儀式,是他們家三代人每天早上都會做的功課,「做功德不是為了求什麼,是讓自己的心先安靜下來。」她說這句話時,語氣平淡得像在講今天菜市場的菜價,卻讓我站在原地愣了好一會兒。

沒有人為誰表演,這條巷子的清晨,本來就長這個樣子。

一台腳踏車,騎向烏蒙寺的森林

托鉢結束後,我沒有直接回房間,而是走去河邊一個連招牌都沒有的咖啡攤,老闆是位年約六十的伯伯,用一支長布袋濾網沖泡「古式咖啡」(กาแฟโบราณ),咖啡粉混著烤玉米與焙過的黃豆,沖出來的顏色深得像醬油,他熟練地加進煉乳,遞給我時杯子還燙手,一杯只要十五泰銖。喝下第一口,苦中帶焦香,甜得毫不掩飾,徹底把我的睡意沖掉。

吃完早餐,我走到塔佩門(Tha Phae Gate)附近一間路邊單車行租車,老闆沒有問太多,只登記我的護照影本當押金,一台老式菜籃單車一天租金一百五十泰銖,沒有安全帽,他隨口說了句「小心狗」就轉身去忙別的客人。我盤算了一下路線:從瓦拉羅市場附近沿著河騎過那瓦叻橋(Nawarat Bridge),進入古城後沿著喇差當能路(Ratchadamnoen Road)橫穿舊城,再從素坦門(Suan Dok Gate)出城,一路往西騎上蘇貼路(Suthep Road),終點是烏蒙森林寺(Wat Umong Suan Phutthatham)。全程大約六公里,我騎得很慢,加上迷路兩次、閃避三輛橫衝直撞的雙條車(songthaew),整整花了五十分鐘。

快接近寺廟入口時,柏油路變成碎石與紅土交錯的小徑,兩旁樹蔭突然濃密起來,氣溫像被按了開關似的降了幾度。烏蒙寺建於七百多年前,是蘭納時期為了讓僧侶靜修而挖鑿的地下磚造隧道,我彎腰走進其中一條低矮的通道,裡面幽暗潮濕,牆上還留著斑駁的佛像壁龕,只有從天井灑下的幾道光線劃過磚牆的縫隙。走出隧道,眼前是一片開闊的樹林,松鼠在地上竄來竄去,幾棵大樹的枝椏上掛著寫了泰文與英文法語的木牌,是歷任住持留下的法語小語,風一吹,木牌互相碰撞發出清脆的聲音。寺內免費入場、隨喜添香油錢即可,池塘裡的鯰魚會成群游到岸邊討食,一包魚飼料十泰銖,我蹲在池邊看著魚群翻攪出水花,忽然覺得腿上的痠痛都值得了。

回程我特意繞了小路,經過一片稻田,騎累了就把單車牽到路邊樹下坐著,看一位農婦彎腰插秧,完全沒有意識到我的存在——那一刻我才明白,慢旅行真正的重點從來不是「去了哪裡」,而是「花了多少時間,願意停下來看什麼」。

巷子裡那碗五十泰銖的靈魂——咖哩麵

中午十二點半,我滿身大汗地把單車騎回古城,肚子餓得咕嚕作響,直奔昌普門(Chang Phuak Gate)附近那間開業超過三十年的「Khao Soi Mae Sai」。這間店沒有華麗裝潢,鐵皮屋簷下擺著十幾張塑膠桌椅,牆上貼滿泛黃的剪報,老闆娘在店門口一口大鍋前站了一輩子,通常賣到下午兩點左右就會掛出「今日售罄」的牌子。

我點了一碗雞肉咖哩麵(Khao Soi Gai),五十泰銖,附上一小碟酸芥菜、生紅蔥頭、一片萊姆和一小碗自製辣椒油。麵端上桌時,金黃色的椰奶咖哩湯上鋪著一把炸得酥脆的麵條,底下是燙軟的雞蛋麵,一整隻燉得軟嫩、幾乎脫骨的雞腿肉沉在湯裡。我先喝了一口湯,椰奶的濃郁厚度撞上薑黃與咖哩的辛香,喉頭泛起一股溫熱的甜,接著擠入萊姆汁,酸味瞬間把整碗湯的層次都打開;拌入酸芥菜的鹹脆與紅蔥頭的嗆,最後舀一勺辣椒油,辣感像火柴一樣「刷」地點著,額頭立刻冒出細汗。

店裡的電風扇吱嘎轉著,收音機播著泰式民歌(luk thung),一隻虎斑貓窩在隔壁桌下打盹,幾位穿著警察制服的常客用泰語跟老闆娘閒聊,沒有人看手機,吃完就起身付錢走人。我一個人坐在角落,把整碗湯喝到見底,滿頭大汗卻捨不得停下來扇風——這大概是我在清邁吃過最誠實的一餐,便宜到不像話,卻好吃到讓我隔天又騎車繞回來一次。

五十泰銖買不到米其林的排場,卻能買到一整條巷子活了三十年的味道。

午後小憩與東龍艾花市的黃昏

下午兩點的清邁熱得誇張,蟬聲吵得像高壓電流,我回到河邊民宿的吊床上晃了將近兩個小時,半夢半醒間聽著湄濱河水拍打岸邊木樁的聲音,手上的書翻不到十頁就睡著了。這種毫無生產力的午睡,竟是整趟旅程裡我最捨不得結束的時刻。

傍晚五點,天色轉橙,我步行到瓦洛洛市場(Warorot Market)旁邊的東龍艾花市(Ton Lamyai Flower Market)。這裡幾乎沒有觀光客,滿地是萬壽菊、茉莉花串與玫瑰花瓣,空氣裡混雜著濃烈的花香與潮濕的水氣,一位頭髮花白的阿嬤坐在矮凳上,手指飛快地把茉莉花一朵朵串成花環(phuang malai),她告訴我,這些花環是拿去供奉家門口的土地公神龕(spirit house)、掛在計程車後照鏡上求平安,或是送去廟裡添香油用的。她隨口提到今天是「戒律日」(wan phra),按泰曆算是每個月固定的佛教齋戒日,虔誠一點的人家會多買一份花環、多煮一份糯米,拿去廟裡做功德。她一邊說,一邊把做好的花環掛在腕上示範給我看該怎麼提才不會散開,動作熟練得像呼吸一樣自然。

市場另一頭,幾位大叔蹲在路邊修剪蓮花花瓣,準備做成蓮花座供品;賣檳榔的小攤旁,一位老先生用收音機聽著泰語新聞,音量開得很大,沒人覺得吵。我買了一串二十泰銖的茉莉花環掛在背包上,一路走回河邊,花香跟著我走了整條街。

晚上七點多,我在河畔一間路邊攤吃了碗簡單的燉豬腳飯(khao kha moo),配著冰涼的泰式奶茶,看著對岸零星的燈火倒映在湄濱河面上,一艘載著遊客的木船緩緩划過,船夫哼著我聽不懂的調子。那一刻我才真正明白,這趟旅行最珍貴的畫面,從來不是任何一張明信片式的合照,而是一整天下來,我用腳踏車的速度、用一碗麵的溫度、用一串花環的重量,重新學會怎麼「待在」一個地方,而不只是「經過」它。

慢下來之後我才發現,清邁根本不需要我趕行程——是它教會我,旅行原來可以這麼安靜。

隔天清晨五點半,鬧鐘還沒響,我又醒了,聽見那熟悉的鐘聲從巷口傳來,忍不住笑了出來——原來我已經開始習慣這座城市的時區了。

ENGLISH

By my fifth day in Chiang Mai, I finally put my camera away. The first four days had been a blur of temples, Nimman cafes, and an elephant sanctuary tour—one stop after another, collapsing into bed each night with a head full of photos and strangely empty of feeling. Then one morning I woke before my alarm, hearing a low, unhurried bell rolling in through the window, and for the first time I felt like I had actually arrived.

The bell belonged to Wat Ket Karam, and my guesthouse sat just off Charoenrat Road, along the Ping River in one of the city's oldest quarters—once a bustling trading post for European merchants, Chinese shopkeepers, and Indian traders in the nineteenth century. Today it's quiet enough that dogs sleep in the temple courtyard and half the breakfast stalls don't even bother with a sign. I chose it not because it photographs well, but because it still feels lived-in.

5:30 AM: Woken by the Bells, Not an Alarm

At half past five the sky hung in that ambiguous shade between grey and blue. I stepped out barefoot onto still-cool concrete, the air thick with jasmine and someone's woodsmoke breakfast fire. Down the lane came a line of saffron robes, seven or eight monks walking barefoot and silent, alms bowls cradled against their chests, sandals occasionally slapping through a puddle.

An elderly woman selling sticky rice at the corner waved me over, amused by how lost I looked. Her cart held one rice warmer, a stack of banana-leaf packets, and small bunches of bananas—twenty baht a set. She showed me how to kneel on the mat, hold the rice with both hands, and place it gently into a passing bowl, warning me firmly: women must never touch a monk directly, and offerings should be handed over a cloth if he holds one out. I barely dared to breathe through the whole exchange, until a young monk murmured a brief Pali blessing and I realized I had just taken part in something Thai families do every single morning without a second thought.

She told me this wasn't staged for tourists—it was homework her family had done for three generations. 'Making merit isn't about asking for things,' she said, as casually as if quoting today's vegetable prices. 'It's about quieting your own mind first.' I stood there a long moment after she'd already turned back to her cart.

Nobody was performing for anyone. This is simply what this lane looks like every morning at dawn.

One Bicycle, a Slow Road to the Tunnels of Wat Umong

After the alms round I didn't head straight back to bed. Instead I wandered to a nameless riverside coffee cart run by a man in his sixties, brewing 'boran' coffee through a long cloth filter, grounds mixed with roasted corn and soybeans, the liquid coming out nearly the colour of soy sauce before he drowned it in condensed milk. Fifteen baht, scalding hot, bitter and shamelessly sweet—it blasted the sleep right out of me.

Breakfast done, I walked to a roadside bicycle stall near Tha Phae Gate. The owner barely glanced up, just photocopied my passport as deposit and handed over an old market bike—150 baht for the day, no helmet, his only advice a mumbled 'watch out for dogs.' I'd mapped a rough route: along the river past Warorot Market, over Nawarat Bridge, straight through the old city along Ratchadamnoen Road, out through Suan Dok Gate, then west on Suthep Road toward Wat Umong Suan Phutthatham. Roughly six kilometers. I rode slowly, got lost twice, dodged three reckless songthaews, and the whole trip took nearly fifty minutes.

Near the temple gate the asphalt gave way to a red dirt track, the tree canopy thickened overnight it seemed, and the temperature dropped as if someone had flipped a switch. Wat Umong's brick tunnels were dug more than seven hundred years ago as meditation chambers for monks. I ducked into one—dim, damp, the walls pocked with weathered Buddha niches, light slicing through cracks in the ceiling above. Outside, squirrels darted across the forest floor, and wooden signs painted with dharma sayings in Thai, English, and French hung from the branches, clacking softly against each other in the breeze. Entry is free, donations welcome; catfish mob the pond's edge for ten-baht bags of feed, and I crouched there watching the water churn, forgetting entirely about my aching legs.

On the ride back I took a detour down a dirt path beside a rice paddy, parked the bike under a tree, and just watched a farmer bent over her seedlings, utterly unaware of me. That was the moment it clicked: slow travel was never about which places you tick off—it's about how long you're willing to sit still and actually look at something.

A Fifty-Baht Bowl of Soul — Khao Soi

By 12:30 I was drenched in sweat and starving, so I pedaled straight for Khao Soi Mae Sai near Chang Phuak Gate, a shophouse that's been running for over thirty years. No decor to speak of—just plastic stools under a tin roof, yellowed newspaper clippings taped to the wall, and the owner standing over the same giant pot she's stood over for decades. They usually sell out and close by early afternoon.

I ordered khao soi gai—chicken curry noodles, fifty baht—with a side plate of pickled mustard greens, raw shallots, a lime wedge, and a small bowl of homemade chili oil. The bowl arrived with crispy fried noodles piled over soft egg noodles submerged in golden coconut curry, a whole chicken leg braised so long the meat slipped off the bone. The broth hit first—thick coconut richness colliding with turmeric and curry spice, a warm sweetness at the back of the throat—then the lime cut clean through it, the pickled greens added crunch and salt, and a spoon of chili oil lit a small fire that had sweat beading on my forehead within seconds.

A ceiling fan creaked overhead, Thai luk thung music crackled from a radio, a tabby cat dozed under the next table, and a couple of off-duty police officers chatted with the owner in rapid Thai, nobody looking at a phone. I sat alone in the corner and drained the bowl completely, sweating hard but unwilling to stop for the fan. It was, without question, the most honest meal I ate in Chiang Mai—absurdly cheap, good enough that I biked back the very next day for another bowl.

Fifty baht won't buy you a Michelin table—but it will buy you a flavor this lane has been perfecting for thirty years.

An Afternoon Nap and Dusk at the Flower Market

Two in the afternoon in Chiang Mai is aggressively hot, cicadas screaming like a live wire. I sank into a hammock back at the guesthouse for nearly two hours, half-listening to the Ping River lapping against the wooden posts below, my book unread past page ten before I dropped off entirely. That aimless nap turned out to be the moment I least wanted to let go of on the whole trip.

By five, the sky had turned orange, and I walked over to Ton Lamyai Flower Market beside Warorot. Almost no tourists here—just heaps of marigolds, strings of jasmine, and scattered rose petals, the air thick with floral sweetness and damp concrete. An elderly woman with silver hair sat on a low stool, fingers flying as she threaded jasmine buds into a phuang malai garland. She told me these garlands go up at spirit houses outside people's front gates, hang from taxi rearview mirrors for safe travel, or get offered at temples. Almost as an afterthought, she mentioned today was 'wan phra,' one of the fixed Buddhist observance days on the lunar calendar—devout families buy an extra garland, cook an extra batch of sticky rice, and take it to temple to make merit. She demonstrated exactly how to carry the garland without it unraveling, her hands moving like it was second nature—because it was.

Further down the market, men squatted trimming lotus petals into offering shapes; beside a betel-nut stall, an old man listened to Thai news on a radio turned up far too loud, and nobody minded. I bought a twenty-baht jasmine garland, hung it off my backpack, and let its scent trail me the whole walk home.

Past seven, I ate a simple bowl of khao kha moo—stewed pork leg over rice—at a riverside stall, iced Thai milk tea beside it, watching scattered lights ripple across the Ping River as a longtail boat carrying a few tourists drifted by, the boatman humming a tune I couldn't place. That's when it hit me: the most valuable image from this whole trip was never going to be a postcard-perfect shot. It was the speed of a bicycle, the warmth of a bowl of noodles, the weight of a garland on my shoulder—all of it teaching me how to actually stay in a place, instead of merely passing through it.

Slowing down, I realized Chiang Mai never needed me to keep an itinerary—it simply taught me travel could be this quiet.

The next morning, half past five, before my alarm even rang, I woke again to that same bell rolling down the lane—and laughed at myself. I'd already started keeping this city's time.
