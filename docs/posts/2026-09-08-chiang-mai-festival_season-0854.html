中文

飛機降落清邁國際機場那一刻,我先做的不是收拾行李,而是把臉貼近機窗——外頭的空氣看起來竟然是乾的。十一月中旬的清邁,不再是雨季那種濕黏黏的悶熱,陽光是亮的,但風裡帶著一點涼意,這是泰北人口中的「涼季」,也是水燈節與天燈節即將登場的季節。我在曼谷轉機時就聽當地朋友提醒:「這個月去清邁,運氣好的話,你會看到整座城市的人一起把願望放上天空。」我把這句話記了一路,直到後來親眼看見,才知道那不是誇飾。

抵達古城:涼季第一口空氣與塔佩門的黃昏

下午四點半,我拖著行李走進古城東側的塔佩門(Tha Pae Gate)廣場,夕陽把紅磚城牆染成橘紅色,一群鴿子被路過的孩子驚起,撲翅的聲音在廣場上迴盪。廣場邊的護城河飄著淡淡的水草味,幾個賣現榨柳橙汁的攤販已經在收攤,老闆娘一邊用泰語跟熟客閒聊,一邊把冰塊倒進保麗龍箱裡。這是我對清邁的第一印象:不是喧鬧的觀光城市,而是一個把節慶當成生活節奏的地方。城裡幾乎每根路燈桿上都掛著小小的黃色三角旗,上面印著蓮花與天燈圖案,那是水燈節籌備委員會掛的裝飾,提醒著每個路過的人——十一月,是這座城市一年之中最亮的月份。

我在古城內一間老宅改建的青年旅館放下行李,老闆是土生土長的清邁人,姓宋提,四十多歲,說話總帶著笑。他告訴我,涼季(十一月到二月)是清邁人一年裡最喜歡的季節,因為濕度低、蚊蟲少,傍晚坐在戶外喝茶不會滿身是汗。「我們清邁人不太喜歡潑水節那個月的濕熱,」他一邊遞給我一杯冰涼的泰式奶茶一邊說,「但水燈節這個月,晚上出門是舒服的,你會想一直待在外面不想回家。」他說這話時窗外正好有風吹過院子裡的緬梔花樹,白色花瓣落了滿地,空氣裡混著花香與炭火的味道——隔壁鄰居已經開始在院子裡烤雞準備晚餐。

清邁人不是在慶祝一個節日,而是在慶祝空氣終於變得可以呼吸的那個月份。

瓦洛洛市場的節慶備貨:一碗咖哩麵與滿街的花香

隔天清晨七點,我走進湄平河西岸的瓦洛洛市場(Warorot Market)。市場外的騎樓下,擺滿了成堆的萬壽菊與白色茉莉花,幾位阿嬤蹲坐在小板凳上,手指飛快地把花瓣穿成一串串花環,準備賣給要供奉神龕或裝飾水燈的顧客。空氣裡是濃烈的花香混著油炸物的香氣,還有一點點魚露發酵的鹹味,這是屬於泰式市場獨有的氣味層次。我在市場二樓一間沒有招牌、只有塑膠圓桌的小攤坐下,老闆娘用大湯勺舀起金黃色的咖哩湯淋在油炸脆麵上,這是清邁最具代表性的小吃——咖哩麵(Khao Soi)。我點的那碗大約四十泰銖,湯頭濃稠帶椰奶甜香,底下臥著軟嫩的雞腿肉,咬下去先是椰奶的圓潤,接著是咖哩粉與薑黃的辛香竄上鼻腔,最後撒上的紅蔥酥帶來一點焦香的尾韻。老闆娘看我吃得認真,笑著又端來一小碟醃芥菜和青檸角,示意我擠一點提味,酸味瞬間把整碗湯的層次拉開。

市場裡因為水燈節將近,賣香蕉葉、竹籤與鮮花的攤位特別多。一位賣香蕉葉的阿伯跟我解釋,水燈(Krathong)傳統上要用香蕉樹幹當底座,插上香蕉葉摺成的蓮花造型,再放上蠟燭、線香和鮮花,象徵向河神告罪並帶走一整年的晦氣。他指著自己攤位上一綑綑修剪整齊的香蕉葉說,這幾天他每天四點就要起床去郊區農地砍葉子,「一年就靠這幾天,平常沒人要買。」他的語氣裡沒有抱怨,反而帶著一種期待——這是屬於整座城市的生意旺季,也是他每年重新感受節慶意義的方式。

湄平河畔學做水燈:一個下午的手作課

下午兩點,我沿著察拉沙特路(Charoen Prathet Road)走到湄平河畔,參加一間小型手作工作室的水燈製作體驗,地點就在河邊一棟老柚木屋裡,離Nawarat橋步行約十分鐘。從古城塔佩門走過去大約二十五分鐘,我選擇用走的,一路經過幾間賣天燈與蠟燭的小店,順便觀察街上已經開始搭起的節慶燈飾骨架。抵達工作室時,老師是一位五十多歲的婦人,叫娜妲,她的家族三代都在做水燈生意。體驗課大約一個半小時,費用是兩百五十泰銖,包含材料與一杯泰式冰茶。

娜妲先示範怎麼把香蕉樹幹橫切成厚圓片當底座,再教我們把香蕉葉裁成長條,一片片摺出蓮花瓣的弧度,用牙籤固定。我的手指笨拙地跟葉子搏鬥了快二十分鐘才摺出第一片像樣的花瓣,葉片邊緣的青草味沾滿了我的指尖,摸起來微微粗糙又帶點濕潤的韌性。娜妲笑我摺得太用力容易破,教我「要像對待嬰兒的手一樣輕」。完成底座後,插上白色的緬梔花、萬壽菊,最後放上三炷香、一根蠟燭和一枚小硬幣——她說硬幣是給河神的禮物,象徵歸還祂借給我們的水。整個過程中,工作室的收音機放著泰北蘭納風格的傳統音樂,窗外湄平河的水流聲隱約可聞,河面上已經有幾艘載著遊客練習放水燈的長尾船經過,船夫用竹竿撐著河底,發出沉悶的敲擊聲。做完屬於自己的水燈,娜妲特別提醒,水燈節當晚河邊人潮眾多,建議提早在傍晚五點前到河岸卡位,並且要注意水燈裡的蠟燭如果用一次性打火機點,風大時最好蹲低身體擋風,免得反覆點燃浪費時間也危險。

我摺的那朵香蕉葉蓮花歪歪扭扭,卻是我這趟旅程裡最誠實的一份手作。

桑派天燈節:萬人仰頭的那一刻

水燈節與天燈節(Yi Peng)在清邁通常前後腳舉行,今年我特地買了票參加位在清邁北郊、湄喬大學(Mae Jo University)附近舉辦的桑派國際天燈節（Yi Peng Sansai）。傍晚五點半,我搭上主辦單位安排的接駁車,從古城出發約四十分鐘車程抵達會場,票價含接駁與座位大約落在兩千至三千五百泰銖之間,依座位區而定。抵達時天色未暗,巨大的空地上已經席地坐了上萬人,空氣裡瀰漫著蠟油與線香混合的味道。主持人先帶著全場誦念一段巴利文經文,聲音透過麥克風低沉迴盪,周圍原本喧鬧的人群漸漸安靜下來,只剩下風吹過稻田的沙沙聲。

晚上八點四十分,倒數結束的瞬間,數萬盞天燈同時被點燃,橘紅色的光點如潮水般從地面湧向天空,火光映在每個人仰起的臉上,我聽見身旁一位清邁老先生用泰語低聲跟他孫子說著什麼,後來一位懂泰文的旅伴幫我翻譯,他說的是:「把不好的事情都放進去,讓它飛走。」那一刻我鼻頭發酸,不完全是因為煙霧,而是被那種集體釋放的情緒打動——沒有人在拍照時大聲喧嘩,大家幾乎是屏著氣息看著自己手中的燈緩緩升空,直到變成星星大小的光點,消失在深藍色的夜幕裡。空氣中飄著淡淡的燒焦味與蠟燭香氣,遠處隱約傳來寺廟的誦經聲透過擴音器傳來,和天燈冉冉升起的畫面疊在一起,形成一種奇異的莊嚴感。

節慶之後:松通寺的燭光與素帖山的清晨薄霧

天燈節隔天晚上,我特地走進古城裡的松通寺(Wat Phan Tao),這座全柚木搭建的寺廟在節慶期間會在庭院擺滿蠟燭,信眾手持蓮花、香與蠟燭繞行大殿三圈,這個儀式叫「Wian Tian」。我跟著人群赤腳走在微涼的石板地上,腳底傳來白天陽光殘留的餘溫,燭光把整座木造寺廟映得金黃,空氣裡是蜂蠟融化的甜膩氣味。一位穿著紫色紗籠的阿嬤看我拿著蠟燭有點手忙腳亂,主動幫我把快熄滅的燭芯重新點燃,她用簡單的英文說:「慢慢走,不用趕。」這句話後來變成我這趟旅程裡最常想起的一句話。

離開清邁前的最後一個清晨,我五點起床包了一輛雙條車上素帖山,車程約四十分鐘,費用大約六百泰銖來回。清晨的素帖寺(Wat Phra That Doi Suthep)幾乎沒有遊客,只有幾位僧侶在誦經,山下的清邁市區被一層薄霧籠罩,只露出幾座尖頂佛塔,空氣冰涼到我得把外套拉鍊拉到頂。站在觀景台往下望,前一晚滿天飛舞的天燈痕跡早已散去,但我知道,那股讓整座城市在涼季裡發光的情緒,並沒有跟著煙火一起熄滅——它藏在每一個清邁人準備下一次節慶的日常裡,等待著明年十一月,再次被點燃。

ENGLISH

The moment the plane touched down at Chiang Mai International Airport, I pressed my face against the window instead of reaching for my bag. The air outside looked dry — not the sticky, heavy air of the rainy season I'd been warned about. It was mid-November, and northern Thais call this the 'cool season,' the very stretch of weeks when Loy Krathong and Yi Peng, the lantern festivals, take over the city. A friend in Bangkok had told me before I left: 'Go to Chiang Mai this month. If you're lucky, you'll see an entire city send its wishes into the sky at once.' I carried that sentence with me for days, not fully believing it until I saw it with my own eyes.

Arriving in the Old City: The First Breath of Cool Season at Tha Pae Gate

At 4:30 in the afternoon I dragged my suitcase into the square in front of Tha Pae Gate, the old brick archway on the eastern edge of the old city. The setting sun turned the red bricks a deep orange, and a flock of pigeons scattered as a child ran past, their wings beating loudly across the open plaza. The moat beside the gate smelled faintly of pond weed, and a few juice vendors were already packing up their carts, an aunty chatting in Thai with a regular customer while dumping ice into a styrofoam cooler. This was my first real impression of Chiang Mai: not a loud, touristy city, but one that lives by the rhythm of its festivals. Small yellow triangular flags printed with lotus and lantern motifs hung from almost every lamppost in the old town, put up by the local Loy Krathong committee — a quiet reminder that November is the brightest month of the year here.

I checked into a guesthouse converted from an old teak house, run by a Chiang Mai native named Songthi, in his forties, who laughed easily and often. He told me the cool season, roughly November through February, is locals' favorite time of year, because the humidity drops and the mosquitoes disappear, and you can sit outside in the evening without sweating through your shirt. 'We don't love the heat of Songkran month as much as tourists think,' he said, handing me a glass of iced Thai milk tea, 'but this month, going out at night feels good. You don't want to go back inside.' As he spoke, a breeze moved through the frangipani tree in the courtyard, scattering white petals across the ground, and the air mixed the flower's sweetness with the smoky smell of a neighbor already grilling chicken for dinner.

Chiang Mai people aren't celebrating a holiday so much as celebrating the one month when the air finally becomes breathable again.

Stocking Up for the Festival at Warorot Market: A Bowl of Khao Soi and Streets Full of Flower Scent

The next morning at seven, I walked into Warorot Market on the western bank of the Ping River. Under the covered walkway outside, piles of marigolds and white jasmine were stacked on tarps, with grandmothers sitting on low stools, fingers moving quickly to string flowers into garlands for shrines and krathong decorations. The air was thick with floral perfume mixed with the smell of frying oil and a faint, briny hit of fermented fish sauce — that particular layered scent unique to Thai wet markets. I sat down at a stall with no sign, just plastic stools and a folding table on the market's second floor, where the owner ladled a thick golden curry broth over crispy fried noodles — khao soi, Chiang Mai's signature dish. My bowl cost about 40 baht. The broth was rich with coconut milk, sweet and heavy, resting a tender braised chicken thigh underneath. The first taste was all coconut roundness, then the sharp warmth of curry powder and turmeric rushed up through my nose, finishing with the smoky crunch of fried shallots on top. Seeing me eat with such focus, the owner smiled and brought over a small plate of pickled mustard greens and lime wedges without being asked, gesturing for me to squeeze some in — the acidity cut straight through the richness and opened the whole bowl up again.

Because Loy Krathong was approaching, stalls selling banana leaves, bamboo skewers, and fresh flowers were everywhere. An elderly vendor selling neatly trimmed banana leaves explained that a traditional krathong is built on a slice of banana trunk, decorated with folded banana-leaf lotus petals, then topped with a candle, incense sticks, and flowers — an offering to the river spirits to release a year's worth of misfortune. He pointed to his stacks of leaves and told me he gets up at four every morning during festival season to cut leaves from a farm on the outskirts of town. 'This is my whole year's business, packed into a few days,' he said, without a trace of complaint — more like anticipation. It's the one stretch of the year when the entire city's economy and mood shift toward the same celebration, and for him, it's also an annual reminder of what the festival actually means.

An Afternoon on the Ping River, Learning to Fold a Krathong

At two in the afternoon I walked along Charoen Prathet Road to the banks of the Ping River, to a small workshop run out of an old teak house near Nawarat Bridge, about a ten-minute walk from the water. From Tha Pae Gate it took me roughly twenty-five minutes on foot, passing shops selling lanterns and candles along the way, watching the metal frameworks for festival light displays already going up on the streets. My teacher was a woman in her fifties named Nada, whose family has made krathong for three generations. The workshop ran about ninety minutes and cost 250 baht, including materials and a glass of iced Thai tea.

Nada first showed us how to slice a banana trunk into a thick round base, then how to cut banana leaves into strips and fold them into the curved shape of lotus petals, pinned with toothpicks. My fingers fumbled with the leaves for nearly twenty minutes before I managed a single presentable petal, the grassy smell of the leaf clinging to my fingertips, which felt slightly rough yet damp and pliant. Nada laughed at how hard I was pressing, telling me to handle it 'like a baby's hand.' Once the base was done, we added white frangipani and marigolds, then three incense sticks, a candle, and a small coin — the coin, she said, is a gift returned to the river spirit, repayment for the water it lends us all year. Traditional Lanna music played softly from a radio in the corner of the workshop the whole time, and through the window I could hear the Ping River flowing, long-tail boats occasionally drifting past carrying tourists practicing their krathong releases, the boatman's bamboo pole striking the riverbed with a dull, rhythmic thud. Once my krathong was finished, Nada warned me that the riverbanks get extremely crowded on the night of the festival itself, so it's worth claiming a spot before five in the evening, and that if using a disposable lighter to light the candle in windy conditions, it helps to crouch low to block the wind rather than wasting time and risking burnt fingers relighting it repeatedly.

My banana-leaf lotus came out crooked and lopsided, but it was the most honest thing I made on this entire trip.

Yi Peng Sansai: The Moment Ten Thousand Faces Turned Skyward

Loy Krathong and Yi Peng usually fall on consecutive nights in Chiang Mai, and this year I'd booked a seat at the Yi Peng Sansai international lantern festival, held on open fields near Mae Jo University on the city's northern outskirts. At 5:30 in the evening I boarded a shuttle bus arranged by the organizers, about a forty-minute ride from the old city; tickets including transport and seating ran between 2,000 and 3,500 baht depending on the seating zone. When I arrived, the sky was still light, and tens of thousands of people were already seated on the ground across the massive field, the air heavy with the mixed scent of candle wax and burning incense. An announcer led the entire crowd through a chant in Pali, his voice low and resonant through the speakers, and gradually the noisy chatter died down until all that remained was the sound of wind moving through the surrounding rice paddies.

At 8:40pm, the moment the countdown ended, tens of thousands of lanterns were lit almost simultaneously, and waves of orange light rose from the ground into the sky, the glow lighting up every upturned face around me. Beside me, an elderly Chiang Mai man murmured something in Thai to his grandson; a fellow traveler who spoke Thai later translated it for me: 'Put everything bad inside it, and let it fly away.' My eyes stung in that moment, and not entirely from the smoke — it was the pull of that collective release. No one shouted or laughed loudly while photographing; almost everyone held their breath, watching their own lantern drift slowly upward until it shrank to the size of a star and vanished into the deep blue night. The air carried a faint burnt smell mixed with candle sweetness, and somewhere in the distance, temple chanting drifted through loudspeakers, layering over the rising lanterns to create a strange, collective solemnity I hadn't expected to feel so deeply.

After the Festival: Candlelight at Wat Phan Tao and Mist on Doi Suthep

The night after Yi Peng, I walked into Wat Phan Tao, the all-teak temple inside the old city, where during festival season the courtyard fills with candles and worshippers walk three times around the main hall carrying lotus flowers, incense, and candles — a ritual called Wian Tian. I joined the barefoot procession across the cool stone floor, still warm in patches from the day's sun, the wooden temple glowing gold in the candlelight, the air thick with the sweet smell of melting beeswax. An elderly woman in a purple sarong noticed me fumbling to keep my candle lit and reached over to relight it for me without a word exchanged first, then said in simple English, 'Walk slow. No hurry.' That sentence stayed with me for the rest of the trip, resurfacing more than anything else I saw.

On my last morning in Chiang Mai, I woke at five and hired a songthaew up Doi Suthep, a forty-minute drive costing around 600 baht round trip. At dawn, Wat Phra That Doi Suthep was almost empty of tourists, just a handful of monks chanting, while the city below lay wrapped in a thin layer of mist, only the tips of a few golden chedis poking through. Standing at the viewpoint, the air was cold enough that I zipped my jacket all the way up. The trails of the previous night's lanterns had long since disappeared, but I understood by then that the feeling that made this entire city glow during the cool season hadn't burned out with the fireworks — it lives quietly in the daily lives of Chiang Mai people as they prepare, year after year, for the next November when it will all be lit again.
