中文

飛機降落在馬拉喀什曼蘇爾機場時已近深夜十一點,艙門一開,一股混合著沙塵、汽油與某種說不出名字的香料氣味的熱風撲面而來。後來我才明白,那氣味幾乎是這座城市的簽名——番紅花、孜然、曬乾的玫瑰花瓣,黏在皮膚上怎麼洗都洗不掉。計程車司機用生澀的法文覆誦我訂的里亞德地址,車子一路顛簸開進舊城麥地那(Medina)近乎違反物理定律的巷弄裡,最後停在一扇毫不起眼的木門前。門後別有洞天,噴泉與柑橘樹藏在斑駁的老宅中央。那一刻我就懂了,這座城市所有的秘密,大概都藏在「外表看不出來」的邏輯裡。

尖塔下的第一道光

清晨五點五十分,喚拜聲把我從時差的迷糊中拉醒。我索性不睡了,披上外套走出里亞德,巷子裡還沒有人,只有一隻瘦貓踩過我腳邊的水窪。十分鐘後我站在庫圖比亞清真寺(Koutoubia Mosque)腳下,七十七公尺高的尖塔在藍紫色的天光裡幾乎是黑色的剪影,鸛鳥的巢一團團架在塔頂邊角,像是誰隨手扔上去的柴堆。當地朋友優瑟夫後來告訴我,眼前這座尖塔其實是「第二代」——西元一〇六二年,柏柏爾人的阿爾摩拉維德王朝(Almoravid)在此建城,首任統治者尤蘇夫·伊本·塔什芬(Yusuf ibn Tashfin)選中這片被阿特拉斯山雪水灌溉的紅土平原,築起夯土紅牆,馬拉喀什因此被稱為「紅色城市」。八十多年後,阿爾摩哈德王朝(Almohad)揮軍推翻前朝,發現舊清真寺的朝向竟未精準對準麥加,於是整座拆除重建,才有了我眼前這座尖塔——它後來還成了塞維亞大清真寺鐘樓「吉拉達」與拉巴特哈桑塔的建築範本。身為非穆斯林,我進不去寺內,只能在對街的花園長椅坐下,點一杯路邊小販的薄荷茶,看第一道陽光把紅牆染成近乎血色的橘。

有些城市用博物館收藏歷史,馬拉喀什卻把歷史直接砌進牆裡,每天清晨重新唤醒一次。

紅色城牆與失落的陵墓

早上九點半,我從里亞德出發,沿著傑馬夫納廣場往南走,穿過巴伯阿格瑙城門(Bab Agnaou)——這座十二世紀阿爾摩哈德時期的城門,雕花至今仍銳利得像是昨天才刻上去的。走到卡斯巴清真寺(Kasbah Mosque)旁邊,如果不留意,很容易錯過寺牆左側那條僅容一人通過的窄巷,巷口沒有招牌,只有零星遊客探頭張望——那就是薩阿德陵墓(Saadian Tombs)的入口。我排了大約二十分鐘的隊,買票七十迪拉姆(約台幣兩百二十元左右),隨人潮擠進去,才發現裡頭別有洞天:三座相連的大理石廳堂,天花板是雪松木雕金箔,牆面大理石據說當年是用等重的糖從義大利卡拉拉換來的。陵墓的主人是薩阿德王朝的黃金君主艾哈邁德·曼蘇爾(Ahmad al-Mansur),一五七八年他在「三王之戰」中擊潰葡萄牙國王塞巴斯蒂安,趁勢掌控延伸至廷巴克圖的黃金與鹽貿易路線,富可敵國,「黃金王」的稱號由此而來。可惜好景不長,十七世纪末,阿拉維王朝的穆萊·伊斯梅爾出於忌妒與宗教理由,下令將陵墓四周砌牆封死,只留一條窄巷供人祕密安葬,自此這座陵墓從馬拉喀什的記憶裡消失了整整兩百年。直到一九一七年,法國殖民時期的將軍利奧泰讓人拍攝空照圖,才意外在牆垣縫隙間發現這座被遺忘的黃金廳堂。我站在天井裡,看著幾隻慵懶的貓在大理石墓碑上曬太陽,忽然覺得,牠們才是真正繼承這座陵墓的主人。

染坊巷弄裡的秘密

中午時分,豔陽把整座麥地那曬得發燙,我鑽進拉巴凱迪馬廣場(Rahba Kedima)後方的染坊巷(Souk Sabbaghine),想躲個蔭涼,卻一頭撞進另一個世界。空氣裡是刺鼻又帶點甜味的靛藍氣味,頭頂上曬滿了剛染好的羊毛線,紫紅、藏藍、番紅花黃,像一整排濕淋淋的彩虹旗。一位滿手靛青、指甲縫都染成深藍色的老師傅哈山(Hassan)看我盯著木桶發呆,便招手要我過去。他家三代都是染匠,從他祖父那輩就在同一個木桶前工作,染料配方——靛藍葉、石榴皮、番紅花蕊——從不外傳,只靠父傳子的方式延續。他一邊攪動染缸,一邊用生硬的英文告訴我,馬拉喀什的手工藝行會制度延續了近千年,鐵匠、皮革匠、染匠各自聚居一條巷子,彼此監督品質,也彼此照顧,「我們不是同行競爭,我們是同一家人。」他遞給我一杯薄荷茶,那是整個摩洛哥最基本的待客禮儀——無論你買不買東西,茶一定要先喝,拒絕反而失禮。哈山還提到,齋戒月期間,凌晨會有一位「納法爾」(nafar)提著鼓、吹著長號在巷弄裡遊走,挨家挨戶敲門叫醒居民起來吃封齋飯,這個職業在馬拉喀什舊城至今仍有人在做,是我原本以為只存在於書本裡的細節,如今活生生站在我面前。

在這裡,一杯茶不是待客的裝飾,而是關係開始前必須完成的儀式。

巴迪王宮的鸛鳥與黃昏

傍晚五點,我走進巴迪王宮(El Badi Palace)遺址,買票七十迪拉姆。眼前是大片斷垣殘壁圍出的巨大庭院,中央下沉式的柑橘園早已荒蕪,只剩幾隻鸛鳥站在崩塌的城牆頂端築巢,長喙敲擊聲在空曠的庭院裡迴盪,像某種古老的鐘。這座宮殿同樣是黃金君主艾哈邁德·曼蘇爾的手筆,建於十六世紀末,據說當年他為了在鄂圖曼使節面前展示財力,連宮殿地板都鋪上黃金與孔雀石,牆面用義大利大理石與印度玉石鑲嵌,富麗程度讓來訪使節嘖嘖稱奇。然而榮景僅維持了一個世紀,一六九六年,阿拉維王朝的蘇丹穆萊·伊斯梅爾下令將宮殿內所有黃金、大理石、雪松木樑全部拆下,運往新首都梅克內斯,只留下我眼前這副空蕩蕩的骨架。導覽牌上引述了一句傳說中刻在宮牆上的銘文:「當我離去之時,這一切也將隨我遠去。」站在夕陽把紅土城牆染成一片焦糖色的當下,我忽然覺得這句話比任何歷史課本都更誠實——王朝來去,黃金流轉,唯獨鸛鳥的巢年復一年,固執地蓋在同一段殘牆上。

傑馬夫納廣場的黃昏與深夜

晚上七點,天色剛暗下來,傑馬夫納廣場(Jemaa el-Fna)開始從白日的市集切換成夜晚的嘉年華。這個廣場的名字直譯是「亡者的集會」,據傳中世紀時這裡曾是公開處決的刑場,叛軍首級曾被懸掛示眾以儆效尤——如今卻是二〇〇一年聯合國教科文組織列為「人類口述與非物質遺產傑作」的所在,因為這裡至今仍有說書人(hlaykia)靠聲音與手勢,在圍成一圈的聽眾中即興演出千年流傳的民間故事,雖然我聽不懂阿拉伯語,但光看老說書人忽而低語忽而暴喝、聽眾時而爆笑時而屏息的樣子,就知道那是真正的表演藝術,而不是觀光噱頭。廣場另一角,格納瓦樂手(Gnawa)敲著鐵製卡拉卡布響板,那是十六世紀被帶到摩洛哥的西非奴隸後裔傳承下來的靈修音樂,節奏急促得像心跳。我在編號三十一號的攤位前坐下,老闆布拉辛用長柄勺舀出一碗蝸牛湯(babouche),湯頭用茴香、丁香與十幾種辛香料熬煮了一整天,湯色深褐,喝下去先是辛辣,尾韻卻回甘,一碗大約十迪拉姆,約台幣三十出頭。隔壁攤是專賣坦吉亞(tanjia)的老鋪,這是馬拉喀什最具身世的料理——傳統上是單身漢的食物,把牛肉、油封檸檬與孜然一起塞進陶罐,拿去公共澡堂的爐灶餘燼裡悶煮六到八個小時,肉質軟爛到用手一撥就化開,我配著現烤圓餅吃完一整份,大約六十迪拉姆。廣場中央,炭烤羊肉串的煙霧混著現榨柳橙汁攤販此起彼落的招呼聲(一杯只要四迪拉姆),我端著薄荷茶爬上「法蘭西咖啡館」(Café de France)的露台,居高臨下看整座廣場在燈火與煙霧中翻騰,忽然明白為什麼這座名為「亡者集會」的廣場,反而是全馬拉喀什最有生命力的地方——王朝興衰、宮殿傾頹,唯獨市井的喧嘩,從一〇六二年建城至今,從未真正安靜下來。

深夜十一點,我沿著同一條巷子走回里亞德,喚拜聲、鼓聲、叫賣聲漸漸遠去,只剩腳步聲迴盪在夯土牆之間。躺在天井旁的房間裡,我想起哈山染缸裡那鍋千年不變的靛藍色,想起薩阿德陵墓被封死又重見天日的兩百年,想起巴迪王宮牆上那句「這一切也將隨我遠去」——馬拉喀什教會我的,大概就是這種矛盾的平衡:帝國會傾頹,黃金會被搬空,但廣場上的說書人、巷弄裡的染匠、爐灶裡悶煮的坦吉亞,始終在原地,一代一代地,把這座紅色城市重新說一遍。

My plane touched down at Marrakech Menara airport close to eleven at night, and the moment the cabin door opened, a wall of warm air rolled in, thick with dust, diesel, and some unnamed spice I couldn't place. I would later learn that smell is essentially the city's signature — saffron, cumin, and dried rose petals, clinging to skin in a way that never quite washes off. The taxi driver repeated my riad's address in halting French, and we lurched through the medina's alleys, which seemed to defy the laws of geometry, before stopping in front of an unremarkable wooden door. Behind it was another world entirely — a fountain, orange trees, a century-old house folded quietly into itself. In that instant I understood something essential about this city: every secret here hides behind a facade that gives nothing away.

The First Light Under the Minaret

At 5:50am the call to prayer pulled me out of jet-lagged half-sleep. I gave up on sleeping altogether, threw on a jacket, and stepped into an alley still empty except for a thin cat picking its way through a puddle. Ten minutes later I was standing beneath the Koutoubia Mosque, its 77-meter minaret a near-black silhouette against the violet dawn, stork nests piled untidily along its upper corners like someone had tossed firewood up there. A local friend, Youssef, later told me the tower I was looking at was actually the second attempt. In 1062, the Berber Almoravid dynasty founded the city here, choosing a red-earth plain fed by snowmelt from the Atlas Mountains, and encircling it with rammed-earth walls — the origin of Marrakech's nickname, the Red City. Eight decades later, the Almohads overthrew the Almoravids and discovered, to their horror, that the original mosque was misaligned with Mecca. They tore the whole thing down and rebuilt it — the very minaret in front of me, which would later become the architectural template for Seville's Giralda tower and Rabat's Hassan Tower. As a non-Muslim I couldn't enter, so I sat on a bench in the garden across the street with a glass of roadside mint tea, watching the first sunlight turn the red walls the color of dried blood.

Some cities keep their history in museums. Marrakech built it directly into the walls, and wakes it up again every single morning.

The Red Walls and the Lost Tombs

By 9:30am I'd set off from the riad, walking south past Jemaa el-Fna and through Bab Agnaou, the twelfth-century Almohad gate whose carved stonework still looks sharp enough to have been cut yesterday. Beside the Kasbah Mosque, easy to miss if you're not paying attention, is a passage barely wide enough for one person, unmarked except for a scatter of curious tourists peering in — the entrance to the Saadian Tombs. I queued about twenty minutes and paid 70 dirhams, roughly seven US dollars, then squeezed in with the crowd to find three interconnected marble chambers, cedar ceilings gilded in gold leaf, and walls of marble reportedly traded for its weight in sugar from Carrara, Italy. The tombs belong to Ahmad al-Mansur, the Saadian dynasty's so-called Golden Sultan, who in 1578 crushed the Portuguese king Sebastian at the Battle of the Three Kings and seized control of the gold and salt trade routes stretching to Timbuktu, earning him his nickname and immense wealth. The glory didn't last. By the late seventeenth century, the jealous Alaouite sultan Moulay Ismail had the tombs sealed behind a wall, leaving only a narrow passage for secret burials, and the site vanished from Marrakech's memory for two centuries. It wasn't until 1917, when French colonial general Lyautey commissioned aerial photographs, that the hidden golden chambers were rediscovered in the gaps between walls. Standing in the courtyard, I watched a few lazy cats sunning themselves on the marble gravestones and thought that they, more than anyone, are the tombs' true present-day inheritors.

Secrets in the Dyers' Alley

By noon the sun had turned the whole medina into a furnace, so I ducked into Souk Sabbaghine, the dyers' alley behind Rahba Kedima square, hoping for shade, and instead walked straight into another world. The air was sharp with the smell of indigo, sweetish and acrid at once, and overhead hung freshly dyed skeins of wool — violet, deep blue, saffron yellow — like a row of dripping flags. An old craftsman named Hassan, his hands and fingernails permanently stained indigo blue, noticed me staring at his wooden vats and waved me over. His family has worked the same trade for three generations, at the very same vat his grandfather used, and the dye recipe — indigo leaf, pomegranate skin, saffron thread — is never written down, only passed from father to son. Stirring the dye bath, he explained in broken English that Marrakech's craft guild system has held for nearly a thousand years, with blacksmiths, leatherworkers, and dyers each clustered in their own alleys, watching over each other's quality and welfare. 'We're not competitors,' he said, 'we're the same family.' He handed me a glass of mint tea, the most basic gesture of hospitality here — whether or not you intend to buy anything, you drink the tea first, and refusing would be rude. Hassan also mentioned that during Ramadan, a man called the nafar still walks the alleys before dawn with a drum and a long horn, knocking on doors to wake residents for the pre-fast meal — a detail I'd assumed only existed in books, standing right in front of me in the flesh.

Here, a glass of tea isn't a decorative gesture of hospitality — it's a ritual that has to happen before any relationship can begin.

Storks and Sunset at El Badi Palace

At five in the evening I walked into the ruins of El Badi Palace, paying another 70 dirhams at the gate. What greeted me was a vast courtyard framed by crumbling walls, its sunken orange grove long gone wild, storks nesting on the broken ramparts, their beaks clacking a strange rhythm that echoed through the empty space like an ancient clock. This palace, too, was the work of the Golden Sultan Ahmad al-Mansur, built in the late sixteenth century, allegedly to impress an Ottoman ambassador — the floors were said to have been laid with gold and malachite, the walls inlaid with Italian marble and Indian jade, wealth so ostentatious it left visiting envoys speechless. The glory lasted barely a century. In 1696, Sultan Moulay Ismail of the Alaouite dynasty ordered every scrap of gold, marble, and cedar beam stripped out and hauled off to his new capital at Meknes, leaving only the bare skeleton I stood inside. A placard nearby quoted a legendary inscription once carved into the palace wall: 'When I am gone, all this too shall pass.' Standing there as the sunset turned the red earthen walls the color of burnt caramel, that line felt more honest than any history textbook — dynasties rise and fall, gold gets carted away, but the storks keep rebuilding their nests on the same broken wall, year after year, indifferent to who ruled last.

Dusk and Deep Night in Jemaa el-Fna

By seven, as the light dropped, Jemaa el-Fna began its nightly transformation from daytime market into carnival. The square's name translates roughly to 'assembly of the dead,' a reference, legend has it, to the public executions once staged here, with the severed heads of rebels displayed as warning. Today it's the site UNESCO named a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity in 2001, largely because it still hosts hlaykia — storytellers who work a circle of listeners with nothing but voice and gesture, improvising tales that have been passed down for a thousand years. I couldn't understand a word of the Arabic, but watching an old storyteller drop his voice to a whisper and then suddenly roar, while his audience alternated between laughter and held breath, made it obvious this was real performance art, not a show staged for tourists. In another corner, Gnawa musicians clashed iron qraqeb castanets in a rhythm as urgent as a heartbeat — descendants of West African slaves brought to Morocco in the sixteenth century, whose music began as spiritual healing ritual. I sat down at stall number 31, where a vendor named Brahim ladled out a bowl of snail soup, babouche, simmered all day with fennel, cloves, and a dozen other spices into a deep brown broth that hit sharp and spicy up front with a sweet aftertaste — about 10 dirhams, roughly a dollar. Next door was a stall devoted entirely to tanjia, arguably Marrakech's most storied dish: traditionally a bachelor's meal, beef packed into a clay urn with preserved lemon and cumin and sent to slow-cook for six to eight hours in the dying embers of a public hammam's furnace, the meat so tender it falls apart at a touch of the fork. I finished a full portion with fresh flatbread for about 60 dirhams. Smoke from charcoal-grilled lamb skewers drifted across the square, mixing with the calls of fresh orange juice vendors — four dirhams a glass — and I carried my mint tea up to the terrace of Café de France, looking down over the whole square boiling with light and smoke, finally understanding why a square named for the dead is, in fact, the most alive place in all of Marrakech. Dynasties collapsed, palaces were stripped bare, but the noise of the marketplace, unbroken since the city's founding in 1062, has never really gone quiet.

Walking back to the riad past eleven that night, the calls to prayer, drumbeats, and vendor cries faded behind me, leaving only my own footsteps echoing off the rammed-earth walls. Lying in my room beside the courtyard, I thought about the same indigo simmering in Hassan's vat for generations, about the Saadian Tombs sealed away and rediscovered two centuries later, about the line carved into El Badi's walls — 'when I am gone, all this too shall pass.' What Marrakech taught me, in the end, is that particular kind of balance: empires collapse, gold gets carried off, and yet the storytellers in the square, the dyers in the alley, the tanjia slow-cooking in the embers — they all stay exactly where they are, telling this red city's story over again, one generation at a time.
