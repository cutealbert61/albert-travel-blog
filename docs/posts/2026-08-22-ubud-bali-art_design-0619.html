中文

清晨五點四十分,我被公雞的啼叫和摩托車引擎聲喚醒,窗外的天色還帶著靛藍色的餘溫。三天前我從丹帕沙機場搭車北上烏布,司機大哥說了一句話我記到現在:「烏布不是用來看的,是用來聞的。」當時我聽不太懂,直到推開民宿房門,聞到院子裡剛點燃的線香混著雞蛋花的甜香,才明白他指的是什麼——這座山城的美,從來不是掛在牆上等你欣賞的畫,而是滲進空氣、街角、餐桌與信仰裡的日常設計。這趟旅程,我決定不當一個只逛博物館打卡的觀光客,而是想搞清楚一件事:烏布口中的「藝術」,到底是怎麼長出來的。

清晨破曉:烏布市場與皇宮的雕刻密碼

六點十分,我沿著主街 Jalan Raya Ubud 走向烏布皇宮 Puri Saren Agung。天還沒全亮,店家已經蹲在自家門口,用椰葉編的小盒子裝著白飯、花瓣與餅乾,那是「canang sari」,峇里島人每天要做兩次的獻祭品,早上一次、傍晚一次,一年三百六十五天不間斷。我蹲下來看一位阿嬤把最後一撮米粒放進去,她抬頭對我笑了笑,沒說話,繼續把線香插進小盒的一角,煙一縷一縷往上飄,混著隔壁攤剛炸好的甜甜圈香氣。

皇宮的正門是典型的「candi bentar」分裂式牌樓,兩座對稱的石塔中間留出一條走道,象徵陰陽或善惡在入口處被劈開、留給人自己選擇要走哪一邊。再往裡走是「kori agung」正門,門楣上雕著一顆咬牙瞪眼的「Boma」面具頭像,傳說祂會把邪靈嚇跑,不讓壞東西跟著人一起進門。整座宮殿用的是當地稱為「paras」的凝灰岩,質地鬆軟到用美工刀都能刻出線條,所以工匠可以把大蕉葉、那伽蛇神、飛天女神一層層堆疊雕滿整面牆,遠看像浮雕壁畫,近看才發現連葉脈的捲曲弧度都不放過。我伸手摸了摸石雕表面,粗糙帶著顆粒感,曬過一夜露水,還有點涼。

在烏布,雕刻不是裝飾建築的最後一道工序,而是建築本身講故事的方式。

七點左右,我穿過馬路到對面的傳統市場 Pasar Ubud,清晨場其實只做到八點多,九點後這裡就會換成賣紀念品的觀光市集。清晨的市場才是本地人的市場:賣椰糖的阿姨、賣現剖檳榔的老爺爺、還有一整排賣祭祀用品的攤子,花瓣論斤秤重賣,金盞花跟雞蛋花分開堆成兩座小山。我買了一份用香蕉葉包的糯米糕,老闆娘找錢時順手往我腳邊放了一份還沒點燃的供品——原來這一區每天都要固定擺放,連攤位地板都算「門檻」的一部分,要獻給看不見的存在。

上午:內卡美術館裡的三段繪畫時光

九點半,我從市場走回民宿牽了腳踏車,沿 Jalan Raya Sanggingan 騎了將近二十分鐘上坡,抵達內卡美術館 Neka Art Museum,門票十萬印尼盾(約新台幣兩百元)。這間美術館由收藏家 Suteja Neka 在一九八二年創立,厲害的地方在於它不是隨便掛畫,而是按照時間軸把峇里島繪畫史分成好幾個展館,走一圈等於上了一堂免費的美術史課。

第一館是「卡瑪珊」古典風格,顏料用的是天然礦石與煤灰,畫的多半是印度史詩《羅摩衍那》的場景,人物臉部全部側面呈現,像皮影戲偶被畫上了紙。第二館是「烏布畫派」,主角是雕刻家出身的畫家 I Gusti Nyoman Lempad,他的線條極簡到近乎書法,一根曲線就能畫出一整條手臂的重量感,館內光線刻意壓得很暗,只留一盞聚光燈打在畫布上,我站在那幅未完成的《火葬儀式》草圖前站了快十分鐘,墨線的呼吸感讓我起了雞皮疙瘩。

再往裡走是「學院自然主義」展廳,牆上掛的是荷蘭畫家魯道夫・邦內特(Rudolf Bonnet)與德國畫家華特・史畢斯(Walter Spies)畫的峇里島日常——市場裡的女人、稻田裡彎腰的農夫,西方油畫技法第一次被拿來畫東南亞的光。最後一館是「年輕藝術家」風格,荷蘭裔畫家艾瑞・史密特(Arie Smit)教會了一群沒受過正規訓練的本地青少年用高彩度、近乎野獸派的顏色畫稻田與市集,整面牆亮得像被打翻的水彩盤,跟前一室的沉穩線描形成強烈對比。我在美術館的木造迴廊坐下休息,柚木百葉窗把正午陽光切成一條一條,灑在磨石子地板上,涼涼的、安安靜靜的,只有冷氣機低鳴聲和隔壁展間偶爾傳來的快門聲。

中午:巷弄裡的日常盛宴——Warung Biah Biah

十二點半,肚子開始抗議,我騎車繞回市區,轉進 Jalan Gootama 這條窄巷。這條巷子本身就是一件作品:牆面畫滿在地藝術家的塗鴉與壁畫,有巨大的迦樓羅、也有超現實風格的女神臉孔,轉角還貼著手工絹印海報,宣傳兩天後的甘美朗音樂會。我在巷子中段找到了在地人跟我推薦的 Warung Biah Biah,一間沒有華麗裝潢、塑膠椅配長桌的家常小館。

我點了 Ayam Sisit(手撕辣拌雞肉,店家用檸檬葉、辣椒跟青檸現拌,吃起來又酸又辣又帶點煙燻雞肉的甜)、Tum Ayam(用峇里島綜合香料 basa gede——薑黃、南薑、蝦醬、辣椒磨成醬——拌雞絞肉,包進香蕉葉裡蒸熟,打開葉子那一刻香氣整個衝出來)、還有 Sayur Urab(燙青菜拌現刨椰絲跟一點椰糖),配一份紅米飯跟一杯冰檸檬茶,加起來大概十萬印尼盾出頭(約新台幣兩百元)。Tum Ayam 咬下去先是椰糖的甘,接著南薑的辛香從喉頭冒上來,最後才是辣椒的後勁,層次分明到我忍不住抬頭跟老闆比讚。隔壁桌一位在地阿伯用手抓飯吃,我學著他也用右手,米飯的溫度跟醬汁混在指尖,那種觸感是叉子絕對給不了的親密感。

Tum Ayam 打開香蕉葉的那一秒,我才懂什麼叫「用嗅覺讀一道菜的身世」。

午後:騎進馬斯木雕村,親手觸摸神話

下午一點四十分,我在 Jalan Monkey Forest 上一間小店租了輛摩托車,一天六萬印尼盾(約新台幣一百二十元),沒有押證件,只留了旅館名片。沿 Jalan Raya Mas 往南騎大約六公里、二十分鐘車程,兩旁從商店街逐漸變成稻田跟低矮工作坊,空氣裡開始飄著木屑跟清漆的味道——這裡就是馬斯木雕村。這段路沒有人行道,貨卡會突然從小路竄出來,我建議下午兩點到四點之間騎,這時候觀光巴士還沒開始收班,路況相對安靜,也避開了傍晚常見的午後雷陣雨。

我把車停在 Njana Tilem Gallery 門口,這間畫廊由已故木雕大師 Ida Bagus Njana 與其子 Ida Bagus Tilem 創立,兩人被視為讓峇里島木雕從「廟宇裝飾」走向「獨立藝術品」的關鍵人物。館內一樓是完成品展售區,一尊真人比例的猴神哈努曼雕像索價超過八千萬印尼盾,木紋因為長年上油而泛著深咖啡色的光澤;後院則是工作坊,四五位師傅盤腿坐在木屑堆裡,手裡的鑿子「叩、叩、叩」敲在酸豆木或鱷魚木上,節奏比想像中快,木屑一片片彈起來沾在他們的頭髮跟眼睫毛上,沒有人戴口罩或護目鏡。

一位師傅 I Wayan 願意讓我試著刻幾刀,我握著鑿子的手抖得像帕金森氏症患者,他笑著糾正我的角度:「要順著木頭的呼吸,不是逼它。」他說這門手藝是跟父親學的,父親又是跟祖父學的,村子裡幾乎每一戶人家都有人做雕刻,彼此之間有點像非正式的公會,誰家接到大訂單忙不過來,鄰居會過來搭把手,利潤再分。我最後買了一尊巴掌大的迦樓羅木雕,二十五萬印尼盾(約新台幣五百元),老闆用報紙隨手一捲就遞給我,完全不像在賣藝術品,更像鄰居送你一份自家做的醬菜。

黃昏到夜晚:坎普安山脊的金色稻浪與廟埕裡的日常藝術

傍晚五點,我把摩托車停回市區,徒步走到坎普安河谷步道 Campuhan Ridge Walk 的起點——龍布河神廟 Pura Gunung Lebah,這裡剛好是兩條河交會之處,「Campuhan」這個字本身就是「交匯」的意思,峇里島人相信水流交會處具有淨化的力量,所以廟就蓋在這裡。步道全長大約四十五分鐘,不收門票,沿途沒有遮蔭,兩側是起伏的草坡而不是想像中的稻田,傍晚風大,吹得芒草沙沙作響。走到中段回頭看,夕陽把整片山谷染成蜂蜜色,遠處寺廟的多層屋頂剪影一座疊一座,像被切割過的黑色紙雕。

七點半,我趕回烏布皇宮看每晚固定上演的雷貢舞 Legong Dance,票價十萬印尼盾,現場甘美朗樂團 Semar Pegulingan 的銅鑼跟竹片琴聲一下子就把廣場的空氣震得發亮,舞者手指反折的角度、眼球快速左右移動的技巧,都是從六七歲就開始訓練的肌肉記憶。散場後我沿著小巷往回走,遇到白天在市場遇過的那位放供品的阿嬤——她叫 Wayan,正蹲在自家門口的小神龕前重新編一批椰葉盒子,準備隔天的「Odalan」廟慶,那是依照峇里島特有的兩百一十天巴烏干曆法計算出的寺廟週年祭。她邀我坐下,一邊教我把花瓣塞進椰葉夾層,一邊用簡單的英文跟手勢告訴我,前陣子街上立起的那些高高彎彎的竹竿裝飾「Penjor」,是為了慶祝「Galungan」——象徵善戰勝惡的節日,每一戶人家門口的竹竿弧度、上面掛的椰葉編織品,做工好壞其實也是鄰里之間心照不宣的比較。

原來在烏布,連一份用完就丟的供品,都要求做工精細——因為美不是給人看的,是給神看的。

晚上九點半,我走回民宿,身上還帶著線香、木屑跟椰糖混合的氣味。這一天我沒有踏進任何一間號稱「必去」的網美咖啡廳,卻覺得比逛遍所有打卡點更貼近這座城市的核心。烏布的藝術從來不是被裱框保護起來的東西,它是清晨門口的一撮米粒、是巷子裡辣雞肉的香氣、是木屑黏在師傅睫毛上的樣子、也是廟慶前一晚阿嬤手裡編織的椰葉盒子。這裡沒有「參觀」跟「生活」的界線,只有一群人日復一日地把信仰、手藝與美感,活成日子本身。

ENGLISH

At 5:40am I woke to a rooster crowing somewhere below my window and the whine of a scooter engine cutting through the indigo half-light. I'd arrived in Ubud three days earlier, and the driver who picked me up from Denpasar airport said something I still think about: 'Ubud isn't for looking at, it's for smelling.' I didn't understand him then. It wasn't until I stepped out of my guesthouse into a courtyard thick with incense smoke and the sweet rot-sugar scent of frangipani that I got it — this hill town's beauty isn't hung on walls waiting to be admired, it's stitched into the air, the doorsteps, the dinner table, the daily offerings. I'd decided this trip wouldn't be about ticking off museums. I wanted to understand what 'art' actually means when the word comes out of an Ubud local's mouth.

Dawn: The Carved Code of Ubud Palace and the Morning Market

At 6:10am I walked down Jalan Raya Ubud toward Puri Saren Agung, the Ubud Palace. The sky was still soft. Shopkeepers were already crouched at their thresholds, folding little woven trays out of coconut palm leaf, filling them with rice, flower petals, biscuits — canang sari, the offering made twice a day, every single day, three hundred and sixty-five days a year. I crouched next to an elderly woman finishing hers, watched her tuck in the last few grains of rice, stick a smoking incense stick into one corner. She looked up, smiled without a word, and kept working. The smoke curled upward and mixed with the smell of fresh doughnuts frying two stalls down.

The palace gate is a classic candi bentar — a split tower gate, two symmetrical stone towers with a walkway carved right through the middle, said to represent the division between good and evil that every visitor must choose to walk through. Deeper inside is the kori agung, the inner gate, its lintel dominated by a snarling Boma mask meant to frighten off anything malevolent trying to follow you in. The whole structure is built from paras, a soft volcanic sandstone so workable that carvers can layer banana leaves, naga serpents and flying apsara figures across an entire wall — from a distance it reads like a relief mural, up close you realize even the curl of a single leaf vein hasn't been skipped. I ran my hand across the surface: gritty, still faintly damp from the night's dew, slightly cool.

In Ubud, carving isn't the finishing touch on a building — it's the building's way of telling a story.

By 7am I'd crossed the street to Pasar Ubud, the traditional market, which really only runs as a locals' market until around 8:30 before it flips into a tourist souvenir bazaar. The early version belongs to the neighborhood: a woman selling palm sugar in cones, an old man slicing fresh betel nut, and a whole row of stalls selling nothing but offering materials, flower petals sold by the kilo, marigold and frangipani piled into separate little hills. I bought a banana-leaf-wrapped sticky rice cake, and while making change the vendor casually set an unlit offering tray by my feet — apparently even the floor space in front of a stall counts as a threshold that needs its daily gift to whatever unseen presence watches over it.

Morning: Three Eras of Painting Inside the Neka Art Museum

At 9:30am I cycled about twenty minutes uphill along Jalan Raya Sanggingan and arrived at the Neka Art Museum, entrance fee 100,000 rupiah (roughly USD 6.50). Founded by collector Suteja Neka in 1982, what makes this museum brilliant is that it doesn't just hang paintings — it organizes Balinese art history chronologically across several pavilions, so walking through feels like a free crash course in art history.

The first pavilion holds classical Kamasan-style works, pigments made from ground mineral stone and lamp soot, mostly depicting scenes from the Ramayana with every figure rendered in strict profile, like shadow puppets pressed flat onto paper. The second pavilion belongs to the 'Ubud style,' anchored by I Gusti Nyoman Lempad, a carver-turned-painter whose lines are so minimal they read almost like calligraphy — a single curved stroke conveys the entire weight of an arm. The room is kept deliberately dim, a single spotlight trained on each canvas; I stood in front of his unfinished cremation-ceremony sketch for nearly ten minutes, and the breathing quality of the ink line genuinely gave me chills.

Further in is the Academic Naturalism wing, hung with paintings by Dutch artist Rudolf Bonnet and German artist Walter Spies, who turned Western oil technique toward Balinese daily life — women at market, farmers bent double in flooded rice paddies. It was the first time Southeast Asian light had been rendered through that particular European lens. The final room belongs to the 'Young Artists' movement, where Dutch-born painter Arie Smit taught a group of untrained local teenagers to render rice fields and markets in oversaturated, almost fauvist color; the whole wall glows like a spilled watercolor tray, a jarring, thrilling contrast to the restrained ink lines one room over. I sat in the museum's teak corridor afterward, sunlight sliced into stripes by wooden louvers, falling across the terrazzo floor — cool, quiet, just the hum of the air conditioner and the occasional click of a camera shutter next door.

Noon: A Home-Style Feast Down a Back Alley — Warung Biah Biah

By 12:30pm my stomach was staging a protest. I cycled back toward the town center and turned into Jalan Gootama, a narrow alley that is, in itself, a work of art — walls covered floor to ceiling in murals by local artists, a towering Garuda here, a surreal goddess face there, hand screen-printed posters taped to the corner advertising a gamelan concert two nights away. Halfway down the alley I found Warung Biah Biah, a no-frills spot with plastic chairs and long communal tables that a local had insisted I try.

I ordered ayam sisit — shredded chicken tossed table-side with lime leaf, chili and fresh lime, sour and sharp with a smoky sweetness underneath; tum ayam — minced chicken mixed with basa gede, Bali's signature spice paste of turmeric, galangal, shrimp paste and chili, wrapped in banana leaf and steamed until the aroma bursts out the second you unfold the leaf; and sayur urab, blanched vegetables tossed with fresh grated coconut and a touch of palm sugar. With a scoop of red rice and an iced lemon tea, the whole meal came to just over 100,000 rupiah (about USD 6.50). Biting into the tum ayam, I got the sweetness of palm sugar first, then galangal's peppery heat rising up the back of my throat, and finally the chili's slow burn — the layering was so precise I actually looked up and gave the owner a thumbs up. At the next table, an older local man was eating with his bare hand; I copied him, and the warmth of the rice mixing with the sauce against my fingertips was an intimacy no fork could ever deliver.

The second the banana leaf unfolds on a plate of tum ayam, you understand what it means to read a dish's entire history through smell alone.

Afternoon: Riding Into Mas Village to Touch Mythology With My Own Hands

At 1:40pm I rented a scooter from a small shop on Jalan Monkey Forest — 60,000 rupiah for the day (about USD 4), no ID held as deposit, just my guesthouse's business card left behind as reassurance. Heading south along Jalan Raya Mas for about six kilometers, roughly twenty minutes, the shopfronts gradually gave way to rice paddies and low workshop sheds, the air starting to smell of sawdust and varnish — I'd arrived in Mas, the woodcarving village. There's no proper sidewalk on this stretch, and trucks can pull out of side lanes without warning, so I'd recommend riding between 2 and 4pm, after the tour buses have finished their morning rounds and before the afternoon thunderstorms that Bali is famous for.

I parked outside Njana Tilem Gallery, founded by the late master carver Ida Bagus Njana and his son Ida Bagus Tilem, two figures widely credited with pulling Balinese woodcarving out of purely temple-decorative work and into the realm of standalone fine art. The ground floor is a showroom — a life-sized Hanuman statue priced at over 80 million rupiah, its wood grain glowing deep brown from years of oiling — while the back courtyard is an active workshop, four or five craftsmen sitting cross-legged in a pile of shavings, chisels striking suar wood or crocodile wood in a rhythm faster than I expected, wood chips flying up to catch in their hair and eyelashes, nobody wearing a mask or goggles.

One of the carvers, I Wayan, let me try a few strokes myself. My hand shook like I had Parkinson's. He laughed and corrected my angle: 'Follow the wood's breathing, don't force it.' He learned the craft from his father, who learned it from his own father; nearly every household in the village has someone in the trade, and they operate almost like an informal guild — when one family gets a big order they can't handle alone, neighbors pitch in and split the profit. I left with a palm-sized Garuda carving, 250,000 rupiah (about USD 16), which the owner casually rolled up in newspaper and handed over — nothing like buying art, more like a neighbor handing you a jar of their homemade pickles.

Dusk to Night: Golden Waves on the Campuhan Ridge, Everyday Art on the Temple Steps

At 5pm I parked the scooter back in town and walked to the start of the Campuhan Ridge Walk, at Pura Gunung Lebah — a temple sitting exactly where two rivers meet. 'Campuhan' literally means 'confluence,' and Balinese belief holds that the meeting point of two flows of water carries a purifying power, which is why the temple was built precisely there. The walk itself takes about forty-five minutes, no entrance fee, unshaded the whole way, running along rolling grass ridges rather than the rice terraces most people picture. Halfway across, I turned back to see the entire valley dyed honey-gold, the multi-tiered silhouettes of distant temple roofs stacked one behind another like layered black paper cutouts.

At 7:30pm I hurried back to Ubud Palace for the nightly Legong dance, tickets 100,000 rupiah. The moment the Semar Pegulingan gamelan orchestra's gongs and bamboo xylophones struck up, the whole courtyard's air seemed to shimmer — the dancers' fingers bending backward at impossible angles, their eyes flicking rapidly side to side, all muscle memory trained into their bodies since the age of six or seven. Walking back through the alleys afterward, I ran into the same elderly woman from the morning market, Wayan, crouched by her family shrine reweaving a fresh batch of coconut-leaf offering trays for the next day's Odalan, the temple's anniversary ceremony calculated according to Bali's unique 210-day Pawukon calendar. She waved me over, showed me how to tuck petals into the woven layers, and explained — in simple English and a lot of gesturing — that the tall, gracefully curved bamboo poles I'd seen lining the streets, called penjor, were decorations for Galungan, the festival celebrating good's victory over evil. The curve of each family's bamboo pole, and the quality of the woven palm ornaments hanging from it, is also a quiet, unspoken measure of pride between neighbors.

Turns out in Ubud, even an offering that gets thrown away within hours has to be made beautifully — because it isn't made to be seen by people. It's made to be seen by gods.

By 9:30pm I walked back to my guesthouse still carrying the smell of incense, sawdust and palm sugar on my clothes. I hadn't set foot in a single one of those must-visit, Instagram-famous cafés all day, and yet I felt closer to the actual pulse of this town than any checklist of sights could have gotten me. Art in Ubud was never something framed and protected behind glass. It's a pinch of rice left on a doorstep at dawn, the smell of spicy shredded chicken drifting down an alley, wood shavings caught in a carver's eyelashes, a coconut-leaf tray woven by lamplight the night before a temple festival. There is no line here between 'visiting' and 'living' — just a community that, day after day, turns faith, craft and beauty into the ordinary texture of life itself.
