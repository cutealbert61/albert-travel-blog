中文

清晨四點五十六分,喚拜聲從石頭城某座清真寺的擴音器裡傳出來,拉得又長又緩,穿過我下榻的尚吉巴咖啡屋民宿(Zanzibar Coffee House)那扇木百葉窗,把我從半夢半醒中拉了出來。我沒有立刻起身,只是躺著聽,聽那個聲音在巷弄間彈跳、迴盪,和著遠處印度洋的浪聲混在一起。這是我在尚吉巴的第三個早晨,也是我決定這一天什麼都不排、只跟著這座城市的呼吸走的一天。對我來說,慢旅行從來不是一句口號,而是像現在這樣——願意在天還沒亮時就醒著,只為了看一座城市怎麼睜開眼睛。

清晨五點半:漁人與咖啡香

五點四十分,我走出民宿,沿著米津加尼路(Mizingani Road)往弗羅達尼花園(Forodhani Gardens)方向走。天還沒全亮,海面是一種介於藍和灰之間的顏色,細窄的木船(當地人叫ngalawa)一艘接一艘從外海划回來,船身刷過水面幾乎沒有聲音,只有船夫偶爾的吆喝和漁網甩上岸的濕重聲響。空氣裡有柴油、鹹魚和露水混在一起的氣味,老城堡(Ngome Kongwe)厚重的珊瑚礁石牆在微光裡只剩一個輪廓,像一頭趴著的獸。

就在海堤邊,我遇到賣咖啡的阿里(Ali)。他推著一個裝滿黃銅咖啡壺(dalla)的小推車,壺嘴細長彎曲,壺身被炭火烘得發亮,壺裡是加了小豆蔻的濃咖啡,他一邊搖鈴一邊喊「kahawa, kahawa」。一小杯只要五百先令(約新台幣六塊多),他順手遞給我一塊花生芝麻糖(kashata)當作配咖啡的小點。我們就坐在海堤的石階上,他說自己賣咖啡賣了二十二年,「以前我爸爸也在這裡賣,同一個位置。」咖啡很燙,小豆蔻的香氣先衝上鼻腔,苦味才慢慢跟上,配著海風,那一刻我忽然明白,自己終於不是在「看」這座城市,而是坐進了它的日常裡。

慢旅行不是走得慢,而是願意停下來,讓一座城市把它的故事說給你聽。

早市迷宮:達拉賈尼市場的煙火氣

喝完咖啡,我沿著溪流路(Creek Road)走了大約十二分鐘,八點整抵達達拉賈尼市場(Darajani Market)。這棟建於一九零四年的紅磚市場建築,是整座石頭城最不觀光、最真實的地方。一走進去,魚市的腥氣撲面而來,大理石檯面上擺著整條的鮪魚、旗魚,還有一整盆一整盆還在微微蠕動的章魚,魚販拿著長刀俐落地分切,水花濺得到處都是,我得踮著腳走才不會弄濕鞋子。

穿過魚市,轉進香料區,空氣瞬間變得溫暖香甜——丁香、肉桂棒、香草莢、薑黃粉一袋袋堆疊在竹籃裡。我跟一位老闆娘比手畫腳講價,半公斤丁香她開價五千先令,我殺到三千成交(約新台幣四十元),她笑著多塞給我一小包肉桂粉。市場深處是布料區,滿牆的康加布(kanga)印著鮮豔花紋和史瓦希里諺語,老闆說每一句諺語都有弦外之音,是女人之間互相傳話的方式。我在裡頭迷路了將近二十分鐘,巷道窄到只能容一人通過,頭頂上還掛著一串串曬乾的辣椒。這裡沒有價目牌,議價是常態,建議上午九點前來人潮較少也比較涼快,週五中午過後市場會提早收攤讓大家去做主麻聚禮,錢包盡量放在貼身包裡,人多的地方要留意隨身物品。我在市場裡前後待了快一個半小時,走出來時渾身是香料味,連呼吸都是甜的。

午間慢食:Lukmaan餐館的一碗什錦飯

中午十二點半,我照著民宿老闆的推薦,走到老城堡附近姆昌巴威瑪街(Mchambawima Street)上的Lukmaan Restaurant。這是一間沒有菜單的自助式小餐館,鐵盤裡盛著當天現煮的菜色,店員站在櫃檯後面,你只要用手指一指就好。我點了章魚咖哩什錦飯(biryani na pweza)、一片薄餅恰巴提(chapati)、還有一份羅望子醬,加一杯芒果汁,結帳時老闆娘按了計算機給我看:一萬兩千先令,大約新台幣一百六十元。

咖哩醬是濃稠的椰奶底,泛著薑黃的金黃色,章魚燉得軟嫩卻還保有嚼勁,完全沒有腥味,飯粒吸飽了咖哩的香氣,一口下去先是椰香,接著薑和丁香的辛香在舌根散開,最後羅望子醬的酸甜收尾。店裡沒有冷氣,只有一台老吊扇嘎嘎轉著,塑膠椅坐起來有點黏,鄰桌是幾個剛卸貨完的碼頭工人,狼吞虎嚥地扒著飯,還有背包客小心翼翼地用手抓飯吃,場面熱鬧卻一點都不擁擠。吃完那餐,我在心裡默默把它排進這趟旅行最好吃的一餐,沒有之一。

在尚吉巴,時間不是用來趕路的,是用來坐下來好好吃完一碗飯的。

巷弄裡的石頭城:午後的生活紋理

下午一點多,整座城市突然安靜下來,許多店家拉下鐵門午休,我索性放慢腳步,鑽進胡魯姆濟(Hurumzi)和奇彭達(Kiponda)一帶的巷弄裡閒晃。尚吉巴的木門是這座城市最迷人的細節——雕花繁複、鑲著黃銅尖釘,阿拉伯式的門框方正,印度式的則多了拱形雕飾,據說尖釘原本是印度傳統用來防止大象撞門的裝飾,搬到這裡變成一種身分與財富的象徵。我數著門上的釘子走了快一公里,幾隻瘦瘦的橘貓懶洋洋地趴在門檻上曬太陽,對經過的人毫無反應。

巷子頭頂上掛滿了曬衣繩,五顏六色的康加布隨風擺動,把正午的陽光濾成一條條光影,走在下面涼快不少。轉角遇到一間裁縫店,老師傅正踩著老式勝家縫紉機,縫一件男性長袍(kanzu),機器喀噠喀噠的聲音跟外頭孩子們踢塑膠瓶當足球的笑鬧聲混在一起。我在一間不到兩坪的小舖買了一頂繡花小帽(kofia),老闆開價一萬先令,聊了幾句他家鄉的事之後算我八千。他告訴我下午一點到四點是店家公認的午休時段,「連神都會休息」,他笑著說,尚吉巴人不趕時間,趕時間的都是遊客。

日落前的功課:齋戒鐘聲與姆瓦卡科格瓦的故事

傍晚五點,我跟這幾天認識的當地朋友朱瑪(Juma)約在莫可利酒吧(Mercury's)的露台碰面,點了兩杯現榨萊姆蘇打,坐等日落。朱瑪在民宿工作,他跟我解釋為什麼下午街上有些婦女穿著黑色罩袍(buibui),有些完全露臉,有些只露眼睛,「這跟信仰虔誠程度和家庭習慣有關,不是規定,是選擇。」他也提醒我,若是齋戒月(Ramadan)期間造訪,白天在公共場合飲食會被視為不禮貌,即使我不是穆斯林,也最好等日落後再吃東西,以示尊重。

聊著聊著,他說起七月在馬昆杜奇村(Makunduchi)舉行的姆瓦卡科格瓦(Mwaka Kogwa)節——這是源自波斯的希拉齊新年慶典,已經在這座島上流傳了上百年。慶典當天,男人們會拿香蕉樹幹互相輕輕拍打,象徵把一整年積壓的怨氣和仇恨都打出去,「打完之後,不能再記仇,新的一年要重新開始。」接著村民會焚燒一間茅草小屋,火燒得越旺,代表來年收成越好。朱瑪小時候參加過好幾次,「我爸打我表哥打得特別大力,」他笑著說,「因為他們吵了一整年架。」聽他說完,我望著眼前逐漸染紅的海面,突然覺得這座島嶼的溫柔,不是沒有摩擦,而是懂得把摩擦好好地、儀式性地放下。

夜幕降臨:弗羅達尼花園的煙火與塔拉布樂音

六點半左右,弗羅達尼花園整個變了樣。白天的空地被一排排流動攤位佔滿,發電機的燈泡亮起暖黃的光,烤章魚串滋滋作響,油煙混著炭火味飄得老遠。我點了一份尚吉巴披薩(Zanzibar pizza)——其實是一種包著雞蛋、絞肉和起司的煎恰巴提,老闆在鐵板上摺了又摺,兩千先令一份,咬下去外皮酥脆,內餡濃郁流心。旁邊攤位現榨甘蔗汁,加一點薑和萊姆,喝起來清甜帶點辛辣的尾勁,一杯一千五百先令。

我端著食物坐上海堤,遠處幾艘單桅帆船(dhow)的黑影正緩緩橫過橘紅色的天際線,身後隱約傳來塔拉布(Taarab)樂團排練的樂聲——那是一種融合阿拉伯、印度和非洲元素的傳統音樂,絃樂和手鼓交織,聽起來既哀傷又華麗。海風把鹹味和炭烤香一起吹過來,我一邊嚼著章魚串,一邊想著這一整天:從喚拜聲、咖啡香、市場的吵鬧,到那碗什錦飯、木門上的黃銅釘、朱瑪說的香蕉樹枝與新年,原來所謂慢旅行,就是允許自己被一整座城市的日常慢慢包圍,而不是急著奔向下一個景點。那天晚上我很晚才回民宿,不是因為行程滿,而是捨不得那陣海風停下來。

ENGLISH

At 4:56 in the morning, the call to prayer rose from a mosque somewhere in Stone Town, long and unhurried, drifting through the wooden shutters of my room at the Zanzibar Coffee House guesthouse and pulling me out of half-sleep. I didn't get up right away. I just lay there listening to the sound bounce between the alley walls, mixing with the distant hush of the Indian Ocean. It was my third morning in Zanzibar, and the day I'd decided to plan nothing at all — just to follow the rhythm of the city as it woke up. To me, slow travel has never been a slogan. It's exactly this: choosing to be awake before dawn just to watch a place open its eyes.

5:30 AM: Fishermen and the Smell of Coffee

I left the guesthouse at 5:40 and walked along Mizingani Road toward Forodhani Gardens. The sky hadn't fully lightened yet, the sea somewhere between blue and grey, and one narrow wooden ngalawa boat after another was gliding back from the open water, hulls barely making a sound, only the occasional shout from a fisherman and the wet slap of a net landing on the dock. The air smelled of diesel, salted fish, and dew. The thick coral-stone walls of the Old Fort, Ngome Kongwe, were still just a silhouette in the half-light, crouched like some sleeping animal.

Right by the seawall I met Ali, who sells coffee from a cart loaded with brass pots called dalla, their spouts long and curved, the metal blackened and polished from years over charcoal. His coffee is spiced with cardamom, and he rings a small bell while calling out, kahawa, kahawa. A tiny cup costs five hundred shillings, and he handed me a piece of peanut sesame brittle, kashata, to go with it. We sat on the stone steps of the seawall while he told me he'd been selling coffee here for twenty-two years — 'my father sold it here too, same spot.' The coffee was scalding, the cardamom hitting first, bitterness following close behind, the sea breeze cutting through both. That was the moment I stopped watching the city and felt like I'd actually sat down inside it.

Slow travel isn't about moving slowly. It's about being willing to stop long enough to let a city tell you its own story.

The Morning Market Maze: Darajani's Sensory Chaos

After the coffee, I walked about twelve minutes down Creek Road and reached Darajani Market at eight sharp. Built in 1904, this red-brick market is the least polished, most honest place in Stone Town. Walking in, the smell of fish hit first — whole tuna and swordfish laid across marble slabs, basins of octopus still faintly twitching, vendors slicing with quick, practiced strokes while water splashed everywhere. I had to walk on my toes just to keep my shoes dry.

Past the fish hall, the air turned warm and sweet in the spice section — cloves, cinnamon sticks, vanilla pods, and turmeric powder piled high in woven baskets. I haggled with a vendor over half a kilo of cloves; she opened at five thousand shillings, I got it down to three thousand, and she tossed in a small bag of cinnamon powder for free, laughing the whole time. Deeper in was the fabric section, walls covered in kanga cloth printed with bright patterns and Swahili proverbs — a shopkeeper told me every proverb carries a hidden message women use to speak to each other. I got properly lost for almost twenty minutes in aisles barely wide enough for one person, strings of drying chilies hanging overhead. There are no price tags here; bargaining is expected. Go before nine to beat the crowds and the heat, and note that the market closes early on Friday afternoons for communal prayers. Keep your bag close in the crowded sections. I spent about ninety minutes total wandering through, and walked out smelling like spice from head to toe, breathing something that felt almost sweet.

Midday Slow Food: A Bowl of Biryani at Lukmaan Restaurant

At half past noon, following a tip from my guesthouse host, I walked to Lukmaan Restaurant on Mchambawima Street near the Old Fort. It's a no-menu, cafeteria-style place — trays of whatever was cooked that day sit behind the counter, and you just point at what you want. I chose octopus biryani (biryani na pweza), a piece of flaky chapati, a side of tamarind sauce, and a mango juice. The total came to twelve thousand shillings, roughly five US dollars, tallied on an old calculator.

The curry was a thick coconut-milk base tinted golden with turmeric, the octopus tender but still with a satisfying bite, no trace of fishiness. The rice had soaked up all that curry, so the first note was coconut, then ginger and clove warming up the back of the throat, and finally the tamarind sauce cutting through with a tart-sweet finish. There was no air conditioning, just an old ceiling fan clattering overhead, plastic chairs slightly sticky with humidity. Dockworkers who'd just finished unloading crates ate hungrily at the next table, while a couple of backpackers carefully ate rice with their hands. It was loud and completely unpretentious. By the time I finished, I'd quietly filed it away as the best meal of the entire trip, no contest.

In Zanzibar, time isn't something you race against — it's something you sit down and spend properly, one plate of rice at a time.

Wandering Stone Town's Alleys: The Texture of an Ordinary Afternoon

By one o'clock the whole town had gone quiet, shutters coming down for the midday lull, so I slowed down too and drifted into the alleys of Hurumzi and Kiponda. The carved wooden doors are Stone Town's most captivating detail — dense floral carvings studded with brass spikes, Arab-style doors squared off, Indian-style ones arched and more ornate. The spikes, I learned, originally came from Indian tradition as elephant deterrents, and here became a mark of status and wealth. I must have walked close to a kilometer just counting door studs. A few skinny orange cats sprawled on doorsteps in the sun, utterly indifferent to passersby.

Laundry lines stretched overhead between buildings, kanga cloth in every color filtering the midday sun into stripes of shade, making the alleys noticeably cooler underneath. Around one corner, an old tailor worked an antique Singer sewing machine, stitching a man's robe, a kanzu, the machine's rattle mixing with the shouts of kids kicking a ball made from a crushed plastic bottle. I bought an embroidered cap, a kofia, from a shop barely two meters wide — the owner asked ten thousand shillings, and after a few minutes of conversation about his hometown, dropped it to eight. He told me one to four in the afternoon is the unofficial nap hour for the whole town. 'Even God rests then,' he laughed. 'It's only the tourists who are always in a hurry.'

Before Sunset: Ramadan Bells and the Story of Mwaka Kogwa

At five, I met my local friend Juma, who works at my guesthouse, on the terrace of Mercury's for fresh lime soda and the sunset. Juma explained why some women on the street wear the black buibui, some go bare-faced, and some cover everything but their eyes — 'It's about personal devotion and family habit, not a rule. It's a choice.' He also warned me that during Ramadan, eating or drinking in public during daylight hours is considered disrespectful, even for non-Muslims, and it's best to wait until after sunset out of courtesy.

As we talked, he told me about Mwaka Kogwa, the Persian-rooted Shirazi New Year festival held every July in the village of Makunduchi, a tradition on the island for over a century. On the day of the festival, men strike each other lightly with banana tree stalks — a symbolic release of an entire year's grudges and anger. 'Once you've been hit, you can't hold the grudge anymore. The new year has to start clean.' Then villagers burn a small thatched hut; the bigger the flame, the better the coming harvest is said to be. Juma had taken part as a child. 'My dad hit my cousin extra hard,' he laughed, 'because they'd been fighting all year.' Listening to him, watching the sea slowly turn red in front of us, I understood that this island's gentleness doesn't come from an absence of friction — it comes from knowing how to release it, ritually, on purpose, every single year.

Nightfall: Forodhani's Grills and the Sound of Taarab

Around six thirty, Forodhani Gardens transformed completely. Rows of food stalls filled the open lawn, generator bulbs casting a warm yellow glow, grilled octopus skewers sizzling, smoke and charcoal drifting on the breeze. I ordered a Zanzibar pizza — really a folded chapati stuffed with egg, minced meat, and cheese, griddled until crisp — for two thousand shillings, the outside crackling, the inside rich and molten. Next door, a vendor pressed fresh sugarcane juice with ginger and lime for fifteen hundred shillings, sweet with a spicy kick at the finish.

I carried my food to the seawall and sat down. A few dhow silhouettes crossed the orange horizon in the distance, and somewhere behind me a taarab orchestra was rehearsing — that traditional music blending Arab, Indian, and African elements, strings and hand drums weaving together, sounding both mournful and lavish at once. The sea breeze carried salt and charcoal smoke together, and as I chewed on my octopus skewer, I thought back over the whole day: the call to prayer, the smell of coffee, the noise of the market, that bowl of biryani, the brass studs on old doors, Juma's story of banana stalks and a clean new year. Slow travel, I realized, isn't a method — it's simply letting yourself be absorbed by a city's ordinary hours instead of rushing toward the next landmark. I got back to the guesthouse very late that night, not because I had somewhere to be, but because I couldn't bring myself to let that sea breeze go.
