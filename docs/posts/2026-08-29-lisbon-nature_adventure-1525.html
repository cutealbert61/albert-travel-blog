中文

清晨五點四十分,鬧鐘在阿爾法瑪區(Alfama)那間老公寓改建的民宿裡震動,窗外天色還是深藍。我原本以為這趟里斯本之旅會被大教堂的鐘聲、28號電車的鐵軌摩擦聲填滿,但攤開地圖那一刻我就知道,我要的是另一種里斯本——懸崖上的風、海裡的鹽、山林裡野生迷迭香的氣味。這是一篇關於自然與冒險的旅行日記,不談博物館開放時間,只記錄我這雙腿真正走過、我這雙手真正划過的路。

破曉騎行:太加斯河畔的野性序曲

我沿著石板巷弄摸黑下坡,走到河岸邊的Cais do Sodré,城市還沒醒,只有麵包店的烤箱已經飄出奶油香氣。里斯本的公共電動單車系統GIRA在里貝拉達斯瑙什(Ribeira das Naus)旁的118號站點就有十幾台車待命,我掃碼解鎖,系統跳出費用:解鎖1歐元,之後每分鐘再加0.15歐元。天色微亮,河面像一塊被輕輕熨過的錫箔紙,泛著灰藍色的光。

沿著Avenida da Índia的專用單車道往西騎,右手邊是太加斯河,左手邊偶爾閃過巴洛克風格的修道院外牆。騎了約二十分鐘,貝倫塔(Torre de Belém)的輪廓從晨霧裡浮現,陽光正好從塔樓後方爬升,把整座石塔鍍成蜂蜜色。河堤邊已經有幾位老先生架好釣竿,一動也不動地盯著浮標,偶爾交換幾句我聽不懂卻聽得出節奏的葡萄牙語。空氣裡有河水混著海藻的鹹腥味,還夾雜一絲柴油味——渡輪剛好從對岸的卡西利亞什(Cacilhas)駛來。

我繼續騎到阿爾熱斯(Algés),河面越來越開闊,遠方25 de Abril大橋的紅色鋼骨在逆光下幾乎變成剪影,對岸山丘上張開雙臂的基督像(Cristo Rei)靜靜俯視整座城市。這段來回大約十四公里,我騎了將近一個半小時,中途停下來看兩隻鸕鶿在防波堤上曬翅膀,那一刻我確定,里斯本的靈魂有一半是留在這條河上的。

里斯本的自然,不在紀念碑的陰影裡,而在河水拍打石堤的節奏中。

辛特拉山區秘徑:佩妮亞到羅卡角的懸崖健行

還了單車,回民宿沖了個澡,在Manteigaria外帶一顆還溫熱的葡式蛋塔當早餐,接著直奔羅西歐車站(Rossio)。九點十五分的火車開往辛特拉,單程用Viva Viagem卡感應,大約2.30歐元,車程四十分鐘,窗外景色從城市街廓逐漸換成松樹與尤加利樹交錯的丘陵,空氣裡開始有種濕潤的綠意。

辛特拉車站外滿是準備去佩納宮排隊的遊客,我卻反方向走向巴士站牌,搭乘Scotturb公司的403號公車前往羅卡角(Cabo da Roca)。車票向司機購買,單程4.50歐元,盤山公路蜿蜒四十分鐘,穿過整片辛特拉-卡斯凱什自然公園(Parque Natural de Sintra-Cascais)的原始林地。中午時分抵達羅卡角,風大得幾乎要把我的帽子吹進大西洋,那塊寫著「陸止於此,海始於斯」(Onde a terra se acaba e o mar começa)的石碑前擠滿拍照人潮,我卻轉身走向另一側一條沒有正式標示、只在岩石上留有褪色黃漆記號的小徑。

這條步道貼著懸崖邊緣延伸,離海面大約八十公尺高,踩過鬆散的碎石和低矮的岩薔薇灌叢,空氣裡瀰漫著類似百里香又帶點薄荷的草本氣味。走了將近五十分鐘,幾隻伊比利牆蜥從我腳邊竄過石縫,遠處海浪拍打礁石發出低沉的轟鳴,像是整座懸崖在呼吸。接近終點時要走一段陡峭的之字形下坡,碎石易滑,我放慢腳步、手扶著岩壁,才終於下到那片被兩座海蝕柱夾住、名叫烏爾莎(Praia da Ursa)的秘境沙灘。

沙灘上只有三、四組健行客,完全沒有救生員,浪況兇猛,我只敢在潮線邊緣涉水到小腿,冰涼的大西洋海水瞬間讓我倒抽一口氣。出發前我特地查過潮汐表,因為漲潮時這片沙灘會被海水完全封住,退路也沒了。我在礁石上坐了四十分鐘,吃著隨身帶的火腿三明治,看浪花一次次撞碎在海蝕柱上,濺起的水花被風吹成一道道細霧。返程的爬坡比想像中累人,頂著下午的日照走了將近一小時,中途停下來喝水兩次,連同來回車程,這趟懸崖健行前後大約花了我三個小時、交通費用共十四歐元左右,卻是整趟里斯本行程裡最讓我腿軟、心也跟著震動的一段路。

大西洋的鹹味饗宴:阿贊亞斯多馬爾的海景午餐

從羅卡角搭計程車北上約十五分鐘、車資十二歐元,我來到懸崖小村阿贊亞斯多馬爾(Azenhas do Mar)。這個村子整個嵌在峭壁上,白牆屋舍層層疊疊往海面滑落,村子下方有一座利用天然礁岩鑿出的海水游泳池,退潮時池水碧綠,漲潮時海浪會直接灌進去,發出轟然巨響。

我在同名餐廳Restaurante Azenhas do Mar找到一張正對懸崖的露台座位,下午兩點的陽光曬得桌布發燙。服務生推薦當天現撈的多拉多魚(dourada,鯛魚),整條下去炭烤,撒上粗海鹽,配一份用橄欖油壓碎的馬鈴薯(batata a murro)和清炒的青菜,再點一杯只在附近科拉雷斯(Colares)沙丘葡萄園種植、風味特別帶點礦物鹹味的白葡萄酒。魚以秤重計價,這條大約二十二歐元,酒一杯四歐元,加上麵包與橄欖總共花了我三十歐元出頭。

魚皮烤得焦脆微焦,一咬下去發出清脆的聲響,裡面的魚肉卻嫩得幾乎要化開,帶著炭火的煙燻香氣和大西洋魚本身的鮮甜鹹味,我忍不住用麵包把盤底的橄欖油和魚汁都抹乾淨。隔壁桌一對年邁的當地夫妻用我聽不太懂的漁村腔葡萄牙語聊著天,偶爾望向下方拍打泳池的浪花,那種悠然的節奏讓我也跟著放慢了咀嚼的速度,像是把整個下午都嚼進了嘴裡。

卡帕里卡海灘:與衝浪教練逐浪大西洋

隔天清晨,我從Praça de Espanha搭巴士南下,約四十分鐘、車資四歐元,抵達卡帕里卡海岸(Costa da Caparica)的聖若昂海灘(Praia de São João)。我提前在網路上預約了「Sol e Mar Surf School」的兩小時團體衝浪課,費用三十五歐元,包含防寒衣租借。教練里卡多(Ricardo)皮膚曬成古銅色,笑起來眼角滿是紋路,他先讓我們六個學員在沙灘上練習「起立」動作,再帶我們涉入海裡。

七月的海水溫度其實只有十八度左右,套上防寒衣的瞬間還是被冷得一縮,浪一波波打過來,我被灌了好幾口鹹水,鼻腔裡都是海水的刺痛感,同組一個德國女生嗆到咳嗽,我們兩個反而笑成一團。練習了將近一個小時,我終於抓到一道不算大的白浪,雙手一撐、膝蓋一頂,竟然真的站起來滑行了大概三秒鐘——雖然下一秒就整個人栽進水裡,但那三秒鐘的平衡感讓我在浪裡振臂歡呼。上岸後我們一群濕淋淋的初學者圍在沙灘小攤前,各自點了一杯咖啡歐蕾(galão)配一顆蛋塔,遠遠望著海灘另一頭的當地老手輕鬆駕馭比我們大兩倍的浪頭,那份從容讓我明白,這片海還有很多我沒摸透的脾氣。

漁人與燈火:海岸信仰和聖安東尼奧的巷弄夜晚

其實在衝浪課開始前,我提早到海灘散步,正好撞見一群當地漁民在進行一種叫「Arte Xávega」的傳統捕魚方式——用拖拉機取代過去的牛隻,把長長的漁網從海裡緩緩拖上沙灘。一位滿臉皺紋的老漁夫若阿金(Sr. Joaquim)看我好奇張望,主動用簡單英文跟我解釋,這種捕魚法從他祖父那一代就開始,現在村裡剩下的漁船已經不多了。他半開玩笑地說,在船上絕對不能說「兔子」這個詞,那是不吉利的禁忌,至於為什麼,連他自己也說不清楚,只說「老祖宗這樣講,我們就照做」。漁網拉上岸的瞬間,銀色的魚群在網裡拍打彈跳,海鷗成群俯衝搶食,整片沙灘瞬間熱鬧起來,那種原始的生命力跟我前一晚在懸崖上感受到的靜謐截然不同。

回到里斯本市區的那個夜晚,剛好碰上六月十二日聖安東尼奧節(Festa de Santo António)的前夕,阿爾法瑪區的巷弄全被彩色紙燈籠和彩帶包裹起來,空氣裡瀰漫著濃濃的炭烤沙丁魚煙燻味,幾乎每個轉角都有攤子用報紙包著烤沙丁魚配麵包和青椒販售。一位老太太多娜法蒂瑪(Dona Fátima)在自家門口擺了一整排小盆栽,我稱讚她種的羅勒(manjerico)特別茂盛,她笑著隨手摘了一小盆插著紙康乃馨和一張手寫情詩的羅勒送給我,說這是聖安東尼奧節的傳統,象徵愛情與好運,街坊男女會互相贈送這種小盆栽表達心意。巷子深處傳來排練中的花車遊行(marchas populares)音樂,老人小孩跟著節奏搖擺,紅酒杯在人群中傳遞,那一晚我終於明白,里斯本的自然野性不只在懸崖與海浪裡,也藏在這些巷弄的煙火氣與人情味之中。

有些城市要用眼睛欣賞,里斯本卻要用曬傷的皮膚、灌進鼻腔的海水,和手裡那盆羅勒的重量去記住。

尾聲:離開前的回望

離開前最後一晚,我一個人坐在聖佩德羅德阿爾坎塔拉觀景台(Miradouro de São Pedro de Alcântara)看夕陽把整座城市染成橘紅色,腿上還留著爬懸崖時被灌木劃出的細小傷痕,鼻子裡似乎還殘留著海水的鹹味。我想起羅卡角的風、阿贊亞斯多馬爾的魚、卡帕里卡的浪、還有那盆此刻正放在我行李箱旁的羅勒,忽然覺得,這趟旅行我帶走的不是紀念品,而是一整座城市教我重新用身體去感受世界的方式。

ENGLISH

My alarm buzzed at 5:40 a.m. in a converted apartment guesthouse in Alfama, the sky outside still deep navy blue. I had expected this Lisbon trip to be filled with cathedral bells and the screech of Tram 28 on old rails, but the moment I unfolded my map I knew I wanted a different Lisbon — one made of cliff-top wind, salt water, and the smell of wild rosemary in the hills. This is a journal about that Lisbon, the one measured not in museum opening hours but in the distance my own legs walked and my own arms paddled.

Dawn Ride Along the Tagus: A Wild Overture

I felt my way down the cobbled alleys in the dark toward Cais do Sodré, the city still asleep except for a bakery already breathing out the smell of warm butter. Lisbon's electric bike-share system, GIRA, had a dozen bikes waiting at a dock beside Ribeira das Naus. I scanned the app, unlocked one for one euro plus fifteen cents a minute, and pedaled off as the sky turned the color of brushed tin.

Following the dedicated bike path along Avenida da Índia, the river sat on my right and the pale facades of old monasteries flickered by on my left. About twenty minutes in, the silhouette of Belém Tower rose out of the morning haze just as the sun cleared the horizon behind it, turning the pale stone the color of honey. A few old men had already set up fishing rods along the seawall, standing motionless, occasionally exchanging a few words in a rhythm I couldn't understand but somehow recognized. The air smelled of brine and seaweed, cut with a trace of diesel from a ferry crossing in from Cacilhas.

I kept riding toward Algés, where the river widened and the red steel of the 25 de Abril Bridge turned into a backlit silhouette, the Cristo Rei statue on the far hillside watching over the city with open arms. The round trip came to about fourteen kilometers and took roughly ninety minutes, including a pause to watch two cormorants drying their wings on the breakwater. By the time I turned back, I was certain that half of Lisbon's soul lives on this river.

Lisbon's nature doesn't live in the shadow of monuments — it lives in the rhythm of water slapping against stone.

The Secret Trail from Peninha to Cabo da Roca

After returning the bike and showering, I grabbed a still-warm pastel de nata at Manteigaria and headed straight for Rossio Station. The 9:15 train to Sintra cost about €2.30 with a Viva Viagem card and took forty minutes, the scenery shifting from city blocks to hills thick with pine and eucalyptus, the air already carrying a damp green scent through the open window.

Outside Sintra station, crowds queued for buses to the palaces, but I walked the other way to catch the Scotturb 403 bus toward Cabo da Roca. The ticket, bought from the driver, cost €4.50, and the forty-minute ride wound through the Sintra-Cascais Natural Park's untouched forest. I arrived at the Cabo da Roca lighthouse around noon, wind strong enough to nearly rip my hat off. Crowds gathered at the stone marker reading 'Here the land ends and the sea begins,' but I turned away from them toward a faint, unofficial trail marked only by fading yellow paint on the rocks.

The path hugged the cliff edge roughly eighty meters above the water, crossing loose gravel and low rockrose shrubs that gave off a scent somewhere between thyme and mint. About fifty minutes in, a couple of Iberian wall lizards darted across the rocks near my feet while waves boomed against the cliffs below, a sound like the whole coastline breathing. Near the end came a steep, switchbacking descent over loose scree — I slowed down, one hand on the rock face, until I finally reached Praia da Ursa, a hidden beach pinched between two dramatic sea stacks.

Only three or four other hikers were scattered along the sand, no lifeguard in sight, the surf too rough for anything more than wading to my ankles, the cold instantly knocking the breath out of me. I had checked the tide charts before setting out, because at high tide this beach disappears completely, cutting off any way back. I sat on the rocks for forty minutes eating a ham sandwich, watching waves shatter against the stacks and turn to mist in the wind. The climb back up was harder than I expected, taking nearly an hour under the afternoon sun with two water breaks. All told, including transport, this cliffside hike cost me about €14 and three hours — and left my legs shaking and something in my chest shaking right along with them.

A Salty Feast on the Atlantic: Lunch in Azenhas do Mar

From Cabo da Roca I took a fifteen-minute taxi north for €12 to the cliffside village of Azenhas do Mar, its whitewashed houses cascading down the rock face toward a natural seawater pool carved into the stone, turning emerald at low tide and thundering with surf at high tide.

I found a terrace table at the restaurant of the same name, Restaurante Azenhas do Mar, the tablecloth already hot under the two o'clock sun. The waiter recommended the day's catch, a whole grilled dourada crusted in coarse sea salt, served with smashed olive-oil potatoes and sautéed greens, alongside a glass of white wine from the nearby sand-dune vineyards of Colares, known for its faintly mineral, briny character. The fish was priced by weight at around €22, the wine €4 a glass, and with bread and olives the whole meal came to just over €30.

The skin was charred and crackling, giving off a sharp crunch with the first bite, while the flesh underneath fell apart tender and sweet, carrying both smoke and pure ocean brine. I kept tearing off pieces of bread to mop up the olive oil and juices pooling on the plate. At the next table, an elderly local couple chatted in a thick fishing-village dialect I could barely follow, occasionally glancing down at the waves crashing into the pool below — a rhythm so unhurried that I found myself chewing more slowly just to match it.

Chasing Waves in Costa da Caparica

The next morning I caught a bus from Praça de Espanha, about forty minutes and €4, to Praia de São João on the Costa da Caparica. I had booked a two-hour group surf lesson online with Sol e Mar Surf School for €35, wetsuit included. Our instructor, Ricardo, tanned deep bronze with laugh lines around his eyes, had us practice pop-ups on the sand before wading into the water.

Even in July the Atlantic sat around eighteen degrees Celsius, and the cold hit hard the moment we waded past the wetsuit's collar. Wave after wave dunked us, salt water burning my sinuses, and a German woman in our group choked and laughed at the same time, which set the rest of us off too. After nearly an hour of failed attempts, I finally caught a modest white wave, pushed up, planted my back foot, and actually stood — for about three seconds — before toppling straight into the churn. Those three seconds of balance had me punching the air underwater. Afterward, soaked and grinning, our group huddled at a beach kiosk over galão coffee and pastéis de nata, watching local surfers further down the beach glide effortlessly over waves twice our size, a quiet reminder of how much this ocean still had to teach me.

Fishermen and Firelight: Coastal Rituals and the Feast of Santo António

Before the surf lesson, I had wandered the beach early and stumbled upon a group of local fishermen practicing Arte Xávega, a traditional net-fishing method that once used oxen and now uses tractors to haul long nets ashore. An older fisherman named Sr. Joaquim noticed me watching and explained, in simple English, that the practice had been passed down since his grandfather's time, though few boats still work this way. Half-joking, he told me that saying the word 'rabbit' aboard a boat is bad luck — nobody quite remembers why, only that the old ones said so and everyone still obeys. When the net finally reached the sand, silver fish thrashed inside it while gulls dove screaming overhead, the whole beach erupting into a kind of raw, working energy completely different from the stillness I'd felt on the cliffs the night before.

Back in central Lisbon that evening, I happened to arrive on the eve of the Feast of Santo António on June 12th. Alfama's alleys were strung with colorful paper lanterns and streamers, thick smoke from grilled sardines drifting from nearly every corner, vendors wrapping charred fish with bread and roasted peppers in old newspaper. An elderly woman named Dona Fátima had lined her doorstep with small potted basil plants, and when I complimented them, she handed me one topped with a paper carnation and a handwritten verse, explaining that giving a manjerico pot is an old Santo António tradition, a way for sweethearts to express affection and luck. Deeper in the alley, rehearsal music for the marchas populares parade drifted out, old men and children swaying to the rhythm, glasses of red wine passed hand to hand. That night I understood that Lisbon's wildness isn't only in its cliffs and waves — it also lives in the smoke and warmth of these narrow streets.

Some cities are meant to be seen. Lisbon is meant to be remembered through sunburned skin, salt water in your sinuses, and the weight of a small basil pot in your hand.

A Last Look Before Leaving

On my final evening, I sat alone at Miradouro de São Pedro de Alcântara watching the sunset turn the whole city orange, small scratches still on my legs from the cliff shrubs, the taste of salt still faint in my nose. I thought of the wind at Cabo da Roca, the fish in Azenhas do Mar, the waves at Caparica, and the little basil plant now tucked beside my suitcase, and realized what I was carrying home wasn't a souvenir — it was a whole city's way of teaching me to feel the world through my own body again.
