中文

清晨五點四十分,我站在光化門廣場前,呼出的白氣在攝氏零下三度的空氣裡凝成一小片霧。首爾的冬天比我想像中更安靜,連地鐵站口賣魚糕串的攤販都還沒支起爐火。我來這座城市之前讀過太多資料,朝鮮太祖李成桂在1394年選定漢陽為新都、背負著「風水寶地」與「天命所歸」的傳說,但直到親眼站在這座巨大城門下,看見清晨值勤的警衛換上鮮紅色的甲冑準備進行守門將交代儀式,我才真正感覺到——這座城市從來不是一個抽象的歷史名詞,它是一層一層疊上去的傷口與重建。

光化門下的傳說起點:一座都城的誕生與詛咒

光化門前的兩尊海豹形石獸「獬豸」,導覽手冊總是輕描淡寫地寫著「辟邪之用」,但我在光化門廣場遇到一位晨跑經過、後來停下來跟我聊天的首爾大叔金先生告訴我,老一輩人相信獬豸不只是辟邪,它是用來鎮壓漢陽城「火氣過旺」的風水缺陷。傳說當年建都之時,無學大師與鄭道傳為了都城座向爭執不下,最後選擇讓景福宮背靠北岳山、面向南山,卻始終無法完全化解朝鮮半島「木剋土」的風水格局,於是才在宮門前立起獬豸鎮火。我聽著金先生說這段故事時,晨光正好從光化門的木造飛簷邊緣滲出來,把整座城門染成淡淡的橘紅色,像是替這個傳說做了註腳。六點整,守門將交代儀式正式開始,鼓聲、喇叭聲混著觀光客的快門聲一起響起,那一刻我忽然明白,首爾人並不是活在博物館裡懷念過去,他們是把六百年前的儀式原封不動地搬到今天的日常裡繼續呼吸。

首爾不是把歷史鎖進玻璃櫃裡展示的城市,它把六百年前的儀式原封不動地搬進了今天的晨光與呼吸之中。

景福宮深處的血色宮闕:乙未事變與明成皇后

穿過光化門、興禮門,我在上午八點半左右抵達景福宮最深處的乾清宮。這裡遊客比前殿少了許多,大部分旅行團在勤政殿拍完照就折返,很少人願意多走十分鐘來到這座外觀樸素、甚至有點孤單的建築群。乾清宮是1895年10月8日凌晨,明成皇后被日本浪人闖入弒殺的地點,史稱「乙未事變」。我站在坤寧閤前面,導覽牌上只用簡短的韓文與英文寫著事件經過,但那種輕描淡寫反而讓我起了雞皮疙瘩——一位皇后在自己的寢宮裡被外國浪人持刀刺殺,遺體甚至被澆上煤油焚燒滅跡,而這一切發生的地方,如今只是一座安靜的木造建築,晨光斜斜照進格子窗欞,地板上還留著修復時新舊木料交接的痕跡。我請教了現場一位志工導覽阿姨,她告訴我景福宮在日治時期曾被大幅拆除、改建成朝鮮總督府博物館的展示用地,直到1990年代後韓國政府才展開長達數十年的「景福宮復原工程」,乾清宮正是2007年才重建完成的建築之一。換句話說,我腳下踩著的木地板其實才十幾歲,但它承載的記憶卻整整跨越了一個世紀。走出乾清宮後方的香遠亭,池水在冬日結了一層薄冰,幾隻喜鵲停在枯枝上,那畫面安靜得不像是曾經發生血案的地方,卻也因此更讓人感受到歷史的重量——它不喧嘩,只是靜靜地留在原地等人發現。

北村韓屋村的迷宮巷弄:一趟親身走過的路線紀錄

上午十一點,我從景福宮東側的國立民俗博物館出口離開,沿著三清洞路往北村韓屋村走。這段路我沒有事先訂導覽團,單純憑著手機地圖和路標走,全程大約走了兩個半小時,途中在幾條坡度頗陡的巷弄裡迷路了三次。北村韓屋村其實是散落在嘉會洞、桂洞一帶的真實住宅區,不是主題樂園,這件事我到了現場才真正體會——巷子裡有掛著私人門牌、貼著「請勿拍照、居民生活中」告示的實際民宅,也有像「北村文化中心」這種可以免費入內參觀傳統韓屋格局的公共空間。我建議路線是搭地鐵三號線到安國站,從2號出口出來後往北走,先繞去桂洞街的「北村八景」第一景拍全景照,再沿著北村路11街往下走到嘉會洞31番地一帶,那裡的石牆巷弄坡度最陡,也是韓劇最愛取景的地點。整段路我大約花了三小時,中途在一間叫「THE PLACE 카페」的小咖啡店休息,點了一杯手沖耶加雪菲,價格約6000韓元(當時約新台幣140元),窗外正好可以俯瞰整片黑瓦屋頂。要提醒的是,北村巷弄清晨與傍晚人潮最少,中午前後遊覽車一到,巷子會瞬間塞滿舉著自拍棒的旅行團,想拍到安靜畫面,真的建議上午十點前抵達。走完全程我的手機計步顯示走了將近一萬兩千步,腿有點痠,但那種穿梭在真實生活紋理裡的感覺,比任何規劃過的觀光動線都更讓我著迷。

廣藏市場的煙火氣:一碗綠豆煎餅裡的百年庶民史

下午兩點半,我搭地鐵一號線到鐘路5街站,走進廣藏市場。這座市場開設於1905年,是首爾現存最古老的常設市場之一,當年正值大韓帝國末期,市場的誕生本身就是庶民對抗日本商人壟斷布匹貿易的產物。市場裡的空氣混雜著芝麻油、煎餅麵糊與生牛肉的氣味,攤販此起彼落地用韓語吆喝,我幾乎聽不懂,但光看那股熱鬧勁就餓了。我在市場中段一間招牌寫著「元祖阿婆綠豆煎餅」的攤位前坐下,點了一份綠豆煎餅(빈대떡,約8000韓元)和一份傳說中的「毒品紫菜飯捲」(마약김밥,一份15捲約4000韓元)。綠豆煎餅端上桌時還滋滋作響,外層煎得金黃酥脆,咬下去裡面是綿密濕潤的綠豆泥混著豆芽菜與豬肉末,沾一點店家自製的醬油醋汁,酸香瞬間把油膩感中和掉。毒品紫菜飯捲則小巧得像一口一個,裡面只包了醃蘿蔔和紅蘿蔔絲,配上黃芥末醬油沾醬,吃起來清爽帶點嗆辣,難怪韓國人暱稱它「像毒品一樣讓人上癮」。坐在我旁邊的一位阿珠媽自顧自地跟我聊起天,她說她從小跟著母親在這個市場長大,六十年前市場裡賣的是戰後配給的雜貨與二手美軍物資,現在則成了觀光客必訪的美食天堂,她說著說著笑了一下:「歷史這種東西,最後都會變成有人在市場裡賣的一碗吃食。」這句話我後來反覆想了很多次。

歷史這種東西,最後都會變成有人在市場裡賣的一碗吃食——一位廣藏市場阿珠媽這樣告訴我。

德壽宮石牆路的黃昏:大韓帝國的餘暉與分手傳說

傍晚五點,天色已經開始轉暗,我走到市廳站附近的德壽宮,沿著著名的「石牆路」散步。德壽宮曾是大韓帝國末代皇帝高宗在1897年宣布建立帝國、企圖擺脫中日兩國夾擊、走向近代化改革的地方,宮牆外至今還保留著當年英國、美國、俄羅斯領事館的舊址建築,走在這條路上,西式石造洋樓與朝鮮傳統宮牆並列,像是那個新舊交替、進退維谷的時代本身的縮影。我在德壽宮大漢門附近的烤栗子攤前停下,跟一位頭髮花白的阿爸交談,他一邊翻炒鐵鍋裡的栗子一邊笑著跟我說:「你知道嗎,情侶絕對不能走這條石牆路走到底。」他解釋,首爾人普遍相信,情侶如果沿著德壽宮石牆路走完全程,最後一定會分手,原因眾說紛紜,有人說是因為石牆路盡頭連接著法院,以前很多夫妻是走這條路去辦離婚手續,久而久之就變成了都市傳說。我買了一份烤栗子(3000韓元),邊剝邊聽他說,遠處教堂鐘聲剛好響起,黃昏的光線把整條石牆路染成暖橘色,幾對情侶果然在半路上就折返,笑鬧著說「不要,我們不要走到底」。這種混雜著迷信、幽默與生活智慧的小傳說,比任何正史記載都更讓我感覺貼近首爾人真實的日常心跳。晚上七點多,我沿著清溪川一路走回市中心,水道兩旁的燈光倒映在水面上,曾經是朝鮮王朝排水溝渠、後來被高架高速公路覆蓋、2005年才重新拆除復原成親水空間的清溪川,如今成了上班族下班後散步紓壓的日常風景。站在那裡我忽然想起白天在乾清宮看到的那片薄冰池水,同樣安靜,卻代表著完全不同的意義——一個是傷痕被小心翼翼保存下來提醒後人,一個是傷痕被徹底掩埋又重新掀開、賦予嶄新生命。首爾這座城市最迷人的地方,或許正是它從不假裝自己沒有傷口,卻也從不被傷口困住。

那晚我在明洞附近一間小酒館喝著馬格利米酒,想起這五天走過的路線:光化門的傳說、乾清宮的血案、北村的巷弄生活、廣藏市場的煙火氣、德壽宮石牆的都市傳說,每一段都指向同一件事——首爾人把歷史活成了日常的一部分,不供奉在神壇上,而是拿來當作聊天下酒的話題,拿來提醒情侶約會該走哪條路,拿來變成攤位上一碗熱騰騰的煎餅。回程的飛機上我望著窗外首爾漸漸縮小成一片燈海,那片燈海裡藏著六百年來被燒毀又重建的宮殿、被拆除又復原的河道,以及無數個像那位廣藏市場阿珠媽一樣,笑著把歷史煮進日常鍋裡的普通人。

ENGLISH

It was 5:40 in the morning when I stood in front of Gwanghwamun Plaza, my breath turning to fog in the minus-three-degree air. Seoul in winter was quieter than I had imagined — even the fish cake vendors near the subway exits hadn't lit their burners yet. Before this trip I had read piles of history books about how King Taejo of Joseon chose Hanyang as his new capital in 1394, wrapped in legends of feng shui and heavenly mandate. But it wasn't until I stood beneath that massive gate, watching guards in crimson armor prepare for the changing ceremony at dawn, that I truly understood: this city was never an abstract historical term. It was a city built layer upon layer over its own wounds.

The Legend Beneath Gwanghwamun: A Capital Born of Prophecy and Curse

The two seal-like stone beasts in front of Gwanghwamun, called haetae, are usually described in guidebooks with a throwaway line about warding off evil. But a man named Mr. Kim, who was jogging past the plaza and stopped to chat with me, told me the older generation believed haetae did more than that — they were placed there to suppress the excess 'fire energy' believed to plague the geomantic layout of Hanyang. According to legend, the monk Muhak and the statesman Jeong Do-jeon argued fiercely over the orientation of the new capital. In the end, Gyeongbokgung Palace was built with its back to Mount Bugak and its face toward Namsan, yet the perceived imbalance between wood and earth in the peninsula's feng shui could never be fully resolved, so the haetae were installed at the gate as a permanent ward against fire. As Mr. Kim told me this story, the morning light spilled along the wooden eaves of Gwanghwamun, tinting the entire gate a soft orange, as if underlining the legend itself. At exactly six o'clock, the guard-changing ceremony began — drums, horns, and the clicking of tourists' cameras all rising together — and I suddenly understood that Seoulites are not people who preserve their history behind museum glass. They are people who carry a six-hundred-year-old ritual, unaltered, into the rhythm of their daily mornings.

Seoul does not lock its history inside glass cases for display. It carries a six-hundred-year-old ritual, unaltered, straight into the breath of this morning.

Blood in the Deep Palace: The Assassination of Empress Myeongseong

Passing through Gwanghwamun and Heungnyemun Gate, I reached Geoncheonggung, the deepest and quietest compound of Gyeongbokgung, around 8:30 that morning. There were far fewer visitors here than at the main throne hall — most tour groups snap their photos at Geunjeongjeon and turn back, rarely willing to walk the extra ten minutes to this modest, almost lonely cluster of buildings. Geoncheonggung is where, in the early hours of October 8, 1895, Empress Myeongseong was assassinated by Japanese-backed assailants who broke into her private quarters, an event Korean history calls the Eulmi Incident. I stood in front of Gonnyeonghap, where a small placard briefly described what happened in Korean and English — and that understatement gave me chills. A queen had been stabbed to death in her own bedroom by foreign intruders, her body reportedly doused in kerosene and burned to erase the evidence, and yet today, this is simply a quiet wooden building, morning light slanting through the lattice windows, the floorboards showing faint seams where old and new timber meet during restoration. A volunteer guide I spoke with there explained that much of Gyeongbokgung had been demolished during the Japanese colonial period and repurposed as an exhibition ground for the Government-General Museum, and it wasn't until the 1990s that the Korean government began decades of restoration work — Geoncheonggung itself was only rebuilt in 2007. In other words, the wooden floor beneath my feet was barely a teenager, yet the memory it carried spanned an entire century. Walking out behind Geoncheonggung to Hyangwonjeong Pavilion, I found the pond frozen over with a thin layer of winter ice, magpies perched on bare branches. The scene was so still it hardly seemed like the site of a historical bloodbath — and yet that stillness made the weight of history feel heavier, not lighter. It doesn't shout. It simply waits, quietly, for someone to notice.

Getting Lost in Bukchon Hanok Village: A Route I Walked Myself

At eleven that morning, I left through the exit near the National Folk Museum on the eastern side of Gyeongbokgung and walked north along Samcheong-dong Road toward Bukchon Hanok Village. I hadn't booked any guided tour — just followed my phone map and the street signs, and the whole walk took about two and a half hours, during which I got lost in the steep alleys at least three times. Bukchon is not a theme park; it's a real residential neighborhood scattered across Gahoe-dong and Gye-dong, and I only truly understood this once I was standing there — some alleys have private doorplates with signs asking visitors not to photograph, because 'residents are living here,' while places like the Bukchon Culture Center offer free public access to view a traditional hanok layout. My suggested route: take Line 3 to Anguk Station, exit 2, walk north first to catch the panoramic view from the first of the 'Eight Views of Bukchon' on Gye-dong Street, then continue down Bukchon-ro 11-gil to the area around Gahoe-dong 31, where the stone-walled alleys are steepest and most frequently used as Korean drama filming locations. The entire walk took roughly three hours, and I stopped midway at a small café called THE PLACE for a hand-drip Yirgacheffe, about 6,000 won (roughly USD 4.50 at the time), with a window view overlooking the sea of black-tiled roofs. One thing worth noting: the Bukchon alleys are quietest in early morning and evening — by midday, tour buses arrive and the alleys fill instantly with selfie-stick-wielding groups. If you want a quiet frame, arrive before ten. By the end of the walk my phone's step counter read nearly twelve thousand steps, my legs ached a little, but weaving through the actual texture of people's daily lives felt far more captivating than any curated sightseeing route.

The Smell of Gwangjang Market: A Century of Ordinary Life in One Bindaetteok

At half past two in the afternoon, I took Line 1 to Jongno 5-ga Station and stepped into Gwangjang Market. Founded in 1905, it's one of Seoul's oldest surviving permanent markets, and its very origin was an act of resistance — ordinary Korean merchants banding together against Japanese monopolies over the textile trade during the twilight years of the Korean Empire. The air inside was thick with sesame oil, pancake batter, and raw beef, vendors calling out in rapid Korean I could barely follow, and yet the sheer energy of the place made me hungry within minutes. I sat down at a stall marked 'Original Grandmother's Bindaetteok' in the middle aisle and ordered a mung bean pancake (about 8,000 won) and a portion of the legendary 'drug gimbap,' fifteen bite-sized rolls for around 4,000 won. The bindaetteok arrived still sizzling, its exterior fried to a golden crisp, and inside, a dense, moist mash of ground mung beans mixed with bean sprouts and minced pork. Dipped in the stall's homemade soy-vinegar sauce, the acidity instantly cut through the richness. The drug gimbap, tiny one-bite rolls filled only with pickled radish and shredded carrot, dipped in mustard-soy sauce, tasted clean and sharply spicy — no wonder Koreans nicknamed it 'addictive as a drug.' The ajumma sitting next to me struck up a conversation unprompted. She told me she grew up in this market with her mother; sixty years ago the stalls sold rationed postwar goods and secondhand American military supplies, and now the same market has become a must-visit food paradise for tourists. She laughed and said, 'In the end, history always turns into a bowl of something someone's selling at the market.' I kept turning that sentence over in my head for the rest of the trip.

In the end, history always turns into a bowl of something someone's selling at the market — an ajumma at Gwangjang Market told me, and I couldn't stop thinking about it.

Dusk on Deoksugung's Stone Wall Road: The Empire's Last Light and a Breakup Legend

By five in the evening the sky had already begun to dim, and I walked to Deoksugung near City Hall Station, following the famous Stone Wall Road. Deoksugung was where Emperor Gojong declared the founding of the Korean Empire in 1897, an attempt to modernize and escape being crushed between China and Japan. Outside its walls still stand the old buildings of the former British, American, and Russian legations, and walking that road, Western stone architecture stands shoulder to shoulder with traditional Joseon palace walls — a physical embodiment of an era caught between old and new, unable to move forward or back. I stopped at a roasted chestnut stall near Daehanmun Gate and chatted with a gray-haired ajusshi tending his pan. Stirring his chestnuts, he grinned and told me, 'You know, couples are absolutely not supposed to walk this stone wall road all the way to the end.' He explained that Seoulites widely believe any couple who walks the entire Deoksugung stone wall path is destined to break up. Explanations vary — one popular theory is that the road historically led to the courthouse, where many couples once walked to file for divorce, and over time the association calcified into urban legend. I bought a bag of roasted chestnuts for 3,000 won and listened as he talked, church bells ringing somewhere in the distance, the evening light painting the entire stone wall road a warm amber. Sure enough, a few couples turned back halfway, laughing and saying, 'No, no, we're not walking to the end.' This blend of superstition, humor, and everyday folk wisdom felt closer to the real heartbeat of Seoul than any official history could ever be. Past seven, I walked along Cheonggyecheon back toward downtown, the lights on either bank reflected on the water's surface. Once a Joseon-era drainage channel, later buried under an elevated highway, and finally torn open and restored as a public waterway in 2005, Cheonggyecheon has become the everyday backdrop for office workers unwinding after a long day. Standing there, I suddenly thought of the thin ice on the pond behind Geoncheonggung that morning — equally still, yet meaning something entirely different. One wound had been carefully preserved as a reminder; another had been buried completely, then dug up again and given new life. Perhaps what makes Seoul so compelling is that it never pretends its wounds don't exist, and yet it is never trapped by them either.

That night, sitting in a small bar near Myeongdong with a bowl of makgeolli, I thought back over everything I had walked through in five days: the legend beneath Gwanghwamun, the bloodstained quiet of Geoncheonggung, the lived-in alleys of Bukchon, the sizzling energy of Gwangjang Market, the urban legend of Deoksugung's stone wall. Each one pointed to the same truth — Seoulites have folded their history into the fabric of ordinary life, not enshrined on an altar, but turned into bar conversation, into advice for where couples should or shouldn't walk, into a steaming pancake sold from a market stall. On the flight home, watching Seoul shrink into a sea of lights below, I knew that sea concealed palaces burned down and rebuilt, rivers buried and uncovered again, and countless ordinary people like that ajumma at Gwangjang Market, laughing as they cooked history straight into the pot of everyday life.
