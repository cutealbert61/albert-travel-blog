中文

凌晨十一點四十分,我拖著登山包走出甘迺迪機場第四航廈的自動門,冷空氣夾著飛機燃油味撲上臉,手機螢幕顯示只剩百分之十二的電量,而我對這座城市唯一的準備,是背包側袋裡一張皺巴巴印著青年旅館地址的紙條。那一刻我才真正意識到,紐約不會等你準備好,它只會直接把你丟進去。

落地紐約:機場、地鐵與青旅第一夜

AirTrain到牙買加站(Jamaica Station)的票價是八美元,這筆錢無法用地鐵卡折抵,現場很多背包客第一次來都會愣住,以為可以直接刷卡進閘門,結果被卡在閘口手忙腳亂。我在自動售票機前排了快十分鐘隊,才搞懂要先買AirTrain單程票,出站後再另外儲值一張OMNY卡或實體MetroCard轉乘地鐵。從牙買加站接上E線,再轉到皇后區長島市(Long Island City)的Court Square站,全程大約五十分鐘,車廂裡混著西語、韓語、孟加拉語的對話聲,沒有人特別看我這個扛著大背包的亞洲臉孔,這是我對紐約的第一印象——沒有人有空管你。

我訂的是長島市一間叫The Local NYC的青年旅館,六人房一晚含稅大約五十三美元,床位有拉簾和個人插座,櫃檯凌晨十二點半還亮著燈,值班的多明尼加裔小哥一邊嚼著口香糖一邊幫我登記,順口說了一句「別去時代廣場的餐廳吃飯,那是給觀光客的陷阱」。我後來才知道這句話會被我在接下來一週不斷驗證。窗外是曼哈頓天際線,對岸帝國大廈的燈光在凌晨一點依然亮著,那是我在紐約睡著前最後看到的畫面。

紐約不會為誰放慢速度,它只會遞給你一張地鐵卡,然後說:自己跟上。

第二天清晨我去藥妝店CVS買轉接頭時,順手辦了一張七日無限次MetroCard,三十四美元,只要一天搭超過三趟地鐵或公車就回本。這張卡後來成了我整趟旅程最重要的裝備,比任何一雙鞋都更早磨損。地鐵閘門的感應偶爾會失靈,尤其在下雨天,卡片濕掉刷不過去的窘境我遇過兩次,身後排隊的紐約客沒有一個人不耐煩催促,但也沒有人會多問一句你還好嗎,這種疏離又不失禮貌的距離感,是我後來才慢慢學會欣賞的城市性格。

清晨六點,徒步走過布魯克林大橋:一段路線實錄

我特意選在平日清晨六點出發,從長島市搭七號線轉F線到布魯克林橋-市政廳站(Brooklyn Bridge-City Hall),出站後沿著錢伯斯街走五分鐘就能看到橋的步行入口。這個時間點的橋上只有零星幾個晨跑的當地人和送報紙的貨車司機,完全沒有白天那種人擠人到寸步難行的觀光客潮。木棧道踩起來有輕微的彈性震動,腳步聲和頭頂電纜的風聲混在一起,河面上還飄著一層薄霧,自由女神像的輪廓在遠處若隱若現。

全程走完大約需要四十分鐘,從曼哈頓側走到布魯克林DUMBO區的空拍圖打卡點——那條夾在兩棟磚造建築中間、正對曼哈頓大橋的華盛頓街(Washington Street),清晨七點抵達時完全沒人排隊拍照,我甚至可以把腳架架在馬路正中間慢慢調整角度,這在中午時段根本是不可能的事,因為屆時會有將近百人排隊等著拍同一張照片。我在附近的Brooklyn Roasting Company買了一杯四點五美元的黑咖啡,坐在河岸公園的長椅上看第一班渡輪離港,才發現橋墩的花崗岩在晨光裡會反出淡淡的粉橘色,這是照片完全拍不出來的細節。

提醒一件事:橋上的步道分成腳踏車道與行人道,地面油漆標示很清楚,但觀光客常常邊走邊看手機而誤入車道,我親眼看到一位騎士被迫緊急煞車還罵了一句髒話。另外橋上完全沒有廁所也沒有遮蔽物,夏天太陽升起後溫度會急速上升,建議像我一樣趁清晨完成這段路,回程再原路走回市政廳站搭車,單趟純步行時間加上拍照大約花了我一個半小時,完全免費,是我整趟紐約行程裡CP值最高的一段體驗。

深夜的Katz's Deli與街角Halal Cart:紐約味蕾實錄

下午三點多我搭地鐵到下東城的Katz's Delicatessen,這間開業超過一百三十年的猶太熟食店,進門後要先在入口拿一張小紙卡,店員會在上面手寫點餐紀錄,結帳時千萬別弄丟,我朋友就曾經弄丟卡片被多收了兩美元的「罰款」。我點了招牌的醃燻牛肉三明治(Pastrami on Rye),櫃檯師傅是個滿頭白髮的老先生,他切肉的時候會先塞一小片熱騰騰的牛肉到我嘴裡讓我試吃,肉質軟嫩帶著煙燻的焦香,油脂在舌尖化開卻不油膩,配上略帶酸味的裸麥麵包和一整條醃黃瓜,一份要價二十三點九五美元,分量大到我和同行的背包客朋友兩個人分食一份都吃不完。

晚上快十一點,肚子又餓的時候我走到第六大道與五十三街口,排在The Halal Guys最原始那個餐車攤位前面。隊伍雖然長但移動很快,我點了綜合盤(combo platter,雞肉加牛肉),十美元,師傅俐落地把烤得微焦的雞腿肉、切片牛肉鋪在番紅花黃薑黃飯上,再淋上大量的白醬和辣的紅醬,白醬帶著蒜味優格的濃郁香氣,紅醬則是後勁十足的辣,配著保麗龍餐盒裡冒出的熱氣,我就站在路邊跟其他背包客一起蹲在消防栓旁邊吃,街角傳來薩克斯風藝人即興演奏的爵士樂,那種混雜著烤肉油煙、車流廢氣與音樂聲的深夜氛圍,是任何一間裝潢精緻的餐廳都給不了的紐約味道。

紐約的美味往往不在菜單最漂亮的那家餐廳裡,而是在你排隊排到腳痠、卻捨不得離開的那個街角。

週五傍晚的社區雜貨店與猶太安息日:老闆教我的紐約人情

我住的青旅附近有一間叫Moshe's Corner的小雜貨店,老闆摩許是個戴著黑色圓帽的正統派猶太人,第一次去買瓶裝水時他就用帶著口音的英文問我從哪裡來,得知我是背包客後,他從櫃檯下拿出一片自製的哈拉麵包(Challah)要我試吃,說星期五太陽下山前他要提早打烊,因為安息日(Shabbat)從週五日落持續到週六日落,這段時間他不能工作、不能碰錢、也不能開車或使用電器。那天下午四點半,我特地繞去他的店裡,看著他一邊清點收銀機一邊跟街坊鄰居用意第緒語互道平安,五點整他準時拉下鐵門,街上原本熙來攘往的哈西迪猶太社區居民,幾乎同一時間換上黑色西裝與毛帽,牽著孩子步行前往會堂,沒有人開車、沒有人滑手機,整條街突然安靜下來,像是被按了靜音鍵。

摩許告訴我,如果我週六在威廉斯堡(Williamsburg)看到有人按了電梯卻不出電梯、或是在旅館裡看到「安息日電梯」每層樓自動停靠的按鈕標示,不用覺得奇怪,那是特地為守安息日規矩的住客設計的,因為傳統教義認為按電梯鈕等同於啟動電路、屬於工作的一種。這段對話讓我第一次明白,紐約表面上是全世界最國際化、步調最快的城市,但走進不同街區,時間感會完全不同——曼哈頓中城的證券交易員在早上七點已經西裝筆挺地衝進地鐵站,而威廉斯堡的猶太家庭卻在同一個週五傍晚,選擇讓整條街安靜下來,這種並存的反差,比任何觀光手冊上的介紹都更讓我理解這座城市的複雜性。

背包客帳本與五個踩雷提醒

整理七天的花費,住宿七晚青旅共約三百八十美元,交通七日無限地鐵卡三十四美元,餐飲平均每天約三十美元(靠熟食店、餐車、超市三明治交替解決),景點門票(高線公園免費、大都會博物館採「自由樂捐」實際上很多人只付一美元)大約花了六十美元,整趟七天扣掉機票,現金花費控制在六百美元左右是可以達成的目標。

第一個踩雷提醒:千萬別在JFK機場外隨便上一輛招手攔車的黑頭車,那些非官方載客車輛喊價常常是正常Uber的三倍,務必走到指定排班區搭黃色計程車(到曼哈頓為固定費率含過路費約七十美元上下)或直接用叫車App。第二,時代廣場那些穿卡通服裝的人偶會主動要求合照,拍完照後會強硬索取小費,價碼從五美元喊到二十美元都有,建議完全不要接近他們。第三,注意地鐵站內熱情推銷「免費」CD或手環的街頭藝人,一旦你伸手拿了就會被要求付費。第四,小費文化務必內建進預算,餐廳內用普遍要在稅前金額加上百分之十八到二十二的小費,結帳單上通常會列出三種計算好的比例供你選,別漏看。第五,深夜搭地鐵盡量選擇有站務員或人潮較多的車廂,尤其晚間十一點後,月台上人少的區域最好靠近其他候車乘客站立,這是我在青旅認識的紐約在地背包客特別提醒我的安全習慣。

離開紐約那天早上,我又搭著同一班E線列車回到牙買加站,轉AirTrain前最後看了一眼皇后區的天際線,忽然想起第一晚拖著背包狼狽走出航廈的自己。這座城市從沒對我特別溫柔,但它用最誠實的方式教會我怎麼看地圖、怎麼算小費、怎麼在深夜排隊裡找到一份便宜又滿足的晚餐。背包客的紐約,從來不是明信片裡那個閃閃發亮的天際線,而是七天下來,腳底磨出的水泡與地鐵卡刷過閘門那一聲「嗶」的踏實感。

ENGLISH

At 11:40 p.m. I walked out of Terminal 4 at JFK dragging a hiking backpack, cold air mixed with jet fuel hitting my face, my phone battery down to twelve percent, and my only preparation for this city was a crumpled piece of paper in my side pocket with a hostel address scrawled on it. That was the moment I understood New York doesn't wait for you to be ready. It just throws you in.

Landing in New York: The Airport, the Subway, and My First Night in a Hostel

The AirTrain to Jamaica Station costs eight dollars, and that fare can't be covered by a subway card, which trips up a lot of first-time backpackers who try to tap through the gates and get stuck fumbling. I stood in line at the ticket machine for nearly ten minutes before figuring out I needed to buy the AirTrain fare separately, then load an OMNY card or a physical MetroCard afterward to transfer onto the subway. From Jamaica I took the E train and transferred to Court Square in Long Island City, Queens — the whole trip took about fifty minutes, the car filled with a mix of Spanish, Korean, and Bengali conversation, and not a single person paid any attention to the Asian guy hauling a giant backpack. That was my first real impression of New York: nobody has time to notice you.

I'd booked a bed at The Local NYC hostel in Long Island City, a six-bed dorm running about fifty-three dollars a night with tax, each bunk fitted with a curtain and its own outlet. The front desk was still lit at half past midnight, and the Dominican-American guy on duty checked me in while chewing gum, casually tossing out, 'Don't eat at the restaurants in Times Square, that's a tourist trap.' I'd end up confirming that warning over and over during the week. Outside my window, the Manhattan skyline glowed, the Empire State Building's lights still on past one in the morning — the last thing I saw before falling asleep in New York.

New York doesn't slow down for anyone. It just hands you a subway card and says: keep up.

The next morning at a CVS buying an adapter, I picked up a seven-day unlimited MetroCard for thirty-four dollars, which pays for itself the moment you take more than three rides in a single day. That card became the single most important piece of gear on the whole trip, worn ragged before my shoes were. The turnstile sensors occasionally failed, especially on rainy days — I got stuck twice with a soggy card that wouldn't scan — and not one New Yorker behind me in line rushed me, but nobody asked if I was okay either. That mix of distance and civility is a city character I only learned to appreciate later.

Six A.M., Walking the Brooklyn Bridge: A Full Route Report

I deliberately left at six in the morning on a weekday, taking the 7 train from Long Island City and transferring to the F to Brooklyn Bridge–City Hall station. Five minutes on foot down Chambers Street gets you to the pedestrian entrance of the bridge. At that hour there were only a handful of local joggers and delivery drivers on the walkway, none of the shoulder-to-shoulder tourist crush that builds up later in the day. The wooden planks give a slight springy vibration underfoot, footsteps mixing with the hum of wind through the cables, a thin fog still sitting over the river, the silhouette of the Statue of Liberty faintly visible in the distance.

The full crossing takes about forty minutes, from the Manhattan side to that famous DUMBO photo spot on Washington Street in Brooklyn, framed between two brick buildings with the Manhattan Bridge dead center. Arriving at seven a.m., there was no line at all — I could set up my tripod in the middle of the street and adjust the angle at leisure, something completely impossible by midday, when nearly a hundred people queue for that exact same shot. I grabbed a four-fifty black coffee at Brooklyn Roasting Company and sat on a bench in the waterfront park watching the first ferry pull out, noticing the granite bridge piers catch a faint pink-orange glow in the morning light — a detail no photograph quite captures.

One warning: the bridge path is clearly divided into a bike lane and a pedestrian lane, well marked on the pavement, but tourists staring at their phones constantly wander into the bike lane — I watched a cyclist slam on his brakes and swear at someone who'd drifted over. There are no restrooms or shade anywhere on the bridge, and summer heat climbs fast once the sun is fully up, so I'd recommend doing this crossing early like I did, then walking back the same way to catch the train at City Hall. The whole round trip, including photos, took about ninety minutes, cost nothing, and was easily the highest value experience of my entire trip.

Late Night at Katz's Deli and the Halal Cart on the Corner: A Real Taste of New York

Around three in the afternoon I took the subway down to the Lower East Side for Katz's Delicatessen, a Jewish deli that's been open for over a hundred and thirty years. You grab a small paper ticket at the entrance, and the staff hand-write your order on it as you go — do not lose that ticket, because a friend of mine did and got charged an extra two dollars as a 'penalty.' I ordered the signature pastrami on rye, and the white-haired guy behind the counter slipped a hot slice of pastrami into my mouth to taste while he carved — tender, smoky, the fat melting on the tongue without ever feeling greasy, paired with slightly sour rye bread and a whole pickle on the side. A sandwich runs $23.95, and it's so massive that a friend and I split one and still couldn't finish it.

Close to eleven that night, hungry again, I walked to the corner of 6th Avenue and 53rd Street and got in line at the original Halal Guys cart. The line moves fast despite its length. I ordered the combo platter, chicken and beef, for ten dollars — the cook piled slightly charred chicken thighs and sliced beef over saffron-turmeric rice, then drenched it in white sauce and hot sauce, the white sauce thick with garlicky yogurt richness, the red sauce carrying a real kick. I stood on the sidewalk with steam rising from the styrofoam container, crouched by a fire hydrant alongside other backpackers, a saxophone player improvising jazz down the block. That mix of charred meat smoke, exhaust fumes, and live music at midnight is a flavor of New York no polished restaurant can replicate.

New York's best food is rarely at the restaurant with the prettiest menu — it's on the corner where your feet ache from waiting in line and you still don't want to leave.

Friday Evening at the Corner Store: What the Owner Taught Me About Shabbat

Near my hostel was a small grocery called Moshe's Corner, run by an Orthodox Jewish man in a black kippah. The first time I stopped in for a bottle of water, he asked, in an accented voice, where I was from, and once he learned I was backpacking, he pulled out a piece of homemade challah bread from behind the counter for me to try. He explained that every Friday before sundown he closes early, because Shabbat runs from Friday sunset to Saturday sunset, and during that time he can't work, can't handle money, can't drive or use electronics. That afternoon around 4:30, I made a point of stopping by his shop again and watched him count out the register while wishing his neighbors well in Yiddish. At exactly five o'clock he pulled down the metal shutter, and almost instantly the bustling Hasidic neighborhood around us transformed — people changed into black suits and fur hats, walking with their children toward the synagogue, nobody driving, nobody on their phones. The whole street went quiet, like someone had hit mute.

Moshe told me that if I noticed an elevator in Williamsburg stopping automatically on every floor without anyone pressing a button — a so-called Shabbat elevator — that's intentional, built for observant residents, because traditional interpretation holds that pressing a button completes an electrical circuit, which counts as work. That conversation was the first time it really clicked for me that New York, for all its reputation as the fastest, most international city on earth, holds wildly different senses of time within a few subway stops of each other. Midtown traders are sprinting into the subway in suits by seven a.m., while a few miles away in Williamsburg, an entire street chooses to go silent on a Friday evening — that kind of coexistence taught me more about the city's real complexity than any guidebook could.

The Backpacker's Ledger: Costs and Five Ways Not to Get Burned

Tallying up seven days: seven nights in a hostel came to roughly $380, the seven-day unlimited MetroCard was $34, food averaged about $30 a day by rotating between delis, food carts, and supermarket sandwiches, and admissions — the High Line is free, and the Met's 'suggested donation' means plenty of people genuinely pay just a dollar — added up to around $60. Excluding airfare, keeping cash spending around $600 for a full week is a realistic target.

First warning: never get into an unmarked car that flags you down outside JFK — those unofficial drivers routinely charge triple a normal fare. Head to the official taxi stand for a flat-rate yellow cab to Manhattan (around $70 including tolls) or just order a rideshare app directly. Second, the costumed characters in Times Square will pose for photos with you and then aggressively demand tips, anywhere from five to twenty dollars — best to just avoid them entirely. Third, watch out for street vendors near subway entrances handing you a 'free' CD or bracelet — the moment you take it, they'll demand payment. Fourth, build tipping into your budget from the start: restaurants typically expect eighteen to twenty-two percent on the pre-tax total, and the receipt usually lists three pre-calculated tip options, so don't skip over it. Fifth, late at night, pick subway cars with a conductor nearby or more people in them, especially after 11 p.m., and if a platform section looks empty, stand closer to other waiting passengers — a tip from a fellow backpacker I met at the hostel who actually lives in the city.

The morning I left, I rode that same E train back to Jamaica Station, and right before the AirTrain I took one last look at the Queens skyline, thinking back to that first exhausted night stumbling out of the terminal. This city was never particularly gentle with me, but it taught me, in the most honest way possible, how to read a map, how to calculate a tip, and how to find a cheap, satisfying dinner in a late-night line. Backpacking New York was never about the glittering postcard skyline — it was the blisters on my feet after seven days, and the solid, satisfying beep of a MetroCard clearing the turnstile.
