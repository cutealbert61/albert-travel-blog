中文

飛機降落在埃塞薄靴機場(Aeropuerto Internacional Ministro Pistarini)時是當地清晨五點四十分,天還沒亮。計程車司機跟我確認旅館地址時,我才發現自己對這座城市唯一鮮明的印象,是課本裡一張黑白照片——一個女人站在陽台上,對著廣場上成千上萬的人揮手。那個陽台所在的廣場,叫五月廣場(Plaza de Mayo)。我在旅館丟下行李,幾乎沒有睡,天剛亮就走出門,想去看看那個陽台真正的樣子。

清晨的五月廣場:金字塔下的低語

清晨六點四十分,五月廣場幾乎沒有人,只有幾隻鴿子繞著廣場中央那座白色方尖碑踱步。那座碑叫五月金字塔(Pirámide de Mayo),建於1811年,是紀念1810年五月革命的第一座紀念碑——阿根廷脫離西班牙統治、走向獨立的起點。碑身不算高,大概十公尺,表面因兩百多年的風雨已有剝落,但基座上還留著前一天有人獻的鮮花。我伸手摸了一下冰涼的白色石面,才意識到自己正站在整個國家敘事的起點。

廣場東側是玫瑰宮(Casa Rosada),粉紅色牆面在晨光裡透出一種奇怪的溫柔,不太像權力中心該有的樣子。有一說是粉紅色來自牛血與石灰混合的塗料,也有人說是執政者刻意調和紅白兩黨對立色——不管哪個版本是真的,那座陽台我已經在照片裡看過太多次:1951年艾薇塔·裴隆(Eva Perón)站在那裡對群眾演講,聲音因肺癌已經虛弱卻仍堅持說完;更早在1945年,裴隆本人也是在那裡向廣場宣告他不會辭職。我站在廣場中央抬頭看那個陽台,距離不到一百公尺,近得讓人有點不安——原來歷史發生的空間感,常常比想像中小很多。

五月廣場不是一個地方,它是阿根廷把傷口攤開來曝曬的一張桌子。

卡薩羅薩達的陽台與週四的婆婆們

我後來才知道,自己第一天清晨獨自閒晃的廣場,到了下午三點半會變成完全不同的場景。那天是週四,我特地又走回五月廣場,遠遠就看到一群年紀七、八十歲的婦女,頭上綁著白色頭巾,手裡拿著泛黃的照片,繞著五月金字塔一圈一圈地走。圍觀的人不多,約二三十個,有當地大學生,也有像我這樣的旅人。一位賣國旗徽章的小販跟我解釋,這是「五月廣場的母親們」(Madres de Plaza de Mayo),從1977年——阿根廷軍事獨裁最血腥的時期——起,每週四固定聚集,尋找在那段「污穢戰爭」(Guerra Sucia)中「被消失」的兒女。當年估計有一萬到三萬人被祕密逮捕、失蹤,至今許多家庭仍不知道孩子的遺骸在哪裡。

我跟著人群走了整整一圈,大約十五分鐘,沒有人吶喊口號,只聽到頭巾摩擦、腳步踏在石磚上的聲音,還有遠處街頭藝人手風琴的殘響飄過來。一位頭巾已經洗得發灰的婆婆,在繞圈間隙抬頭看了我一眼,笑了一下,那笑容裡沒有悲傷,反而像一種近乎日常的堅持——四十幾年了,她們把「等待」變成一種儀式,一種每週四準時發生、身體記憶般的抗議。後來我才讀到,這個團體早已分裂成兩派,一派堅持追究真相與正義,一派選擇原諒與和解,但無論哪一派,週四三點半、五月廣場,始終沒有缺席過。

從主教座堂步行到雷科萊塔:尋找艾薇塔

隔天上午九點,我決定用步行方式,重走一次布宜諾斯艾利斯的歷史軸線——從五月廣場旁的都主教座堂(Catedral Metropolitana,聖馬丁將軍的靈柩就安放在裡面)出發,沿五月大道(Avenida de Mayo)走到國會大廈(Congreso),再往北穿過佛羅里達步行街(Calle Florida)接上聖大非大道(Avenida Santa Fe),最後抵達雷科萊塔墓園(Cementerio de la Recoleta)。整條路線大約六公里,我走走停停,中途還被佛羅里達街上一位街頭探戈舞者攔下拍照(小費約一千披索),前後花了近三個半小時。若嫌太遠,其實可以在9 de Julio大道搭D線地鐵到Callao站再轉步行十五分鐘,單趟地鐵票用SUBE卡大約不到台幣十元,堪稱全世界最划算的地鐵之一。

雷科萊塔墓園入口不收門票,但我一走進去就有點迷路——裡面像一座石造的迷宮小城市,每座家族墓都像縮小的教堂或神殿,黑色鑄鐵大門、彩色玻璃窗,還有真的貓在墓與墓之間曬太陽。我在裡面繞了快四十分鐘,靠著地圖上標示的Duarte家族墓才找到艾薇塔的墓——外觀其實相當低調,黑色大理石,沒有巨大雕像,只有訪客留下的鮮花和小卡片一層層疊著。很難想像,這個曾讓整個國家陷入十天國喪、遺體被祕密運送、藏匿、流亡海外近二十年才回葬的女人,最後安息之處竟安靜得像個普通家族。站在墓碑前,我忽然理解為什麼阿根廷人談起她時,語氣總是又崇拜又矛盾——她是聖人,也是爭議本身。

聖特爾莫市集與烤肉的重量

星期天上午十一點,我搭計程車到聖特爾莫(San Telmo),下車瞬間就被氣味包圍——炭火、烤洋蔥,還有街角現榨葡萄柚汁的酸香。沿著防禦街(Calle Defensa)一路往下,週日限定的聖特爾莫市集(Feria de San Telmo)把整條街變成攤販與古董的長河,銅製馬黛壺、老唱片、手工皮件擠在一起,中間穿插著現場拉手風琴的探戈樂手。我在街角小攤買了一份烤香腸夾麵包(choripán),攤主把香腸片開鋪平,壓在鐵烤盤上滋滋作響,再配上一大匙自製青醬(chimichurri)跟醃紅椒莎莎(salsa criolla),一份大約3500披索(約合三美元多)。一口咬下去,油脂混著大蒜香跟醋的酸味在嘴裡炸開,我站在人群裡邊走邊吃,油順著手指流下來也顧不得擦。

晚上七點半,我照著在地朋友的推薦,到巴勒摩區(Palermo)一家叫Don Julio(位於Guatemala街3600號)的老字號烤肉館(parrilla)吃晚餐,沒有事先訂位,現場排隊等了約四十分鐘,店員先送上一杯免費氣泡酒。我點了一份牛肋眼(ojo de bife)配紅酒,肉端上桌時還在鐵盤上滋滋作響,外層炭烤到帶著焦脆的鹹香,切開後裡面粉紅多汁,幾乎不需要任何醬料,阿根廷牛肉本身的甜味就足夠說話。整套餐點加一瓶馬爾貝克紅酒,大約落在35到45美元之間(依匯率浮動),比起台北的和牛餐廳划算得不可思議。牆上貼滿幾十年來熟客留下的手寫紙條與照片,老闆說有些家庭三代都是這裡的常客——那一刻我才明白,這裡賣的不只是肉,是一種代代相傳的日常儀式。

探戈不是舞步,是兩個孤獨的人暫時同意不再孤獨。

馬黛茶、探戈與深夜的告別

離開前最後一個下午,我在雷薩馬公園(Parque Lezama,傳說中布宜諾斯艾利斯最早建城的地點)坐在長椅上寫筆記,旁邊一位六十多歲的阿伯正在喝馬黛茶(mate),看我盯著他手上那個銀吸管的葫蘆罐好奇,他主動遞過來要我試試看。他示範怎麼把熱水從保溫瓶注入裝滿黃綠色茶葉的罐子,再遞給我用同一根吸管(bombilla)喝——這在阿根廷是再自然不過的分享,拒絕反而顯得失禮。茶湯苦中帶草香,像濃烈的綠茶加一點土味,喝完他重新注水遞給下一個人,整個過程沒有人開口要求,彼此心知肚明輪到誰。他笑著跟我說,馬黛茶不是飲料,是「藉口」——藉口讓兩個陌生人可以安靜地坐在同一張長椅上十分鐘。

晚上十一點,我走進聖特爾莫一間不起眼的地下探戈館(milonga),樓梯又窄又暗,樂隊在角落用手風琴(bandoneón)拉出低沉的旋律,舞池裡的舞者大多是附近居民,年紀從二十到七十歲都有,沒有華麗舞台燈光,只有昏黃燈泡跟被鞋跟磨得發亮的木地板。探戈據說起源於十九世紀末的移民碼頭區,那些遠離家鄉、孤獨的碼頭工人與妓院,在擁擠雜亂的小酒館裡跳出這種帶著憂傷底色的舞蹈——原來我這幾天走過的每一段歷史,最後都繞回同一個主題:失落與堅持並存。走出milonga時已接近凌晨一點,街上還有人在路邊喝酒聊天,布宜諾斯艾利斯的夜,好像從來不真正打算結束。

回旅館的路上,我又經過一次五月廣場,深夜的金字塔在路燈下顯得特別安靜,白天那些婆婆、那些歷史的重量彷彿都沉回石頭裡去了。我站在那裡幾分鐘,想著這座城市怎麼可以同時裝得下獨立革命、國喪十天、軍事獨裁,還有一杯隨手遞來的馬黛茶。也許答案就是——布宜諾斯艾利斯從不打算把歷史封存進博物館,它讓歷史繼續走在街上,跟你我擦身而過。

ENGLISH

My flight landed at Ministro Pistarini International Airport at twenty to six in the morning, local time, the sky still dark. As the taxi driver confirmed my hotel address in Spanish, I realized the only image I actually held of this city was a black-and-white photograph from a history textbook: a woman on a balcony, waving to tens of thousands of people crowded into a square. That balcony belongs to a plaza called Plaza de Mayo. I dropped my bag at the hotel, barely slept, and walked out the moment the sky turned grey, wanting to see that balcony with my own eyes.

Dawn at Plaza de Mayo: Whispers Beneath the Pyramid

At twenty to seven in the morning, Plaza de Mayo was nearly empty, just a few pigeons pacing around the white obelisk at its center. That monument is the Pirámide de Mayo, built in 1811, the first memorial ever raised to commemorate the May Revolution of 1810 — the starting point of Argentina's break from Spanish rule. It isn't tall, maybe ten meters, its surface chipped and weathered after two centuries, but fresh flowers sat at its base, left, clearly, just the day before. I reached out and touched the cold white stone, and only then understood I was standing at the very origin point of an entire nation's story.

On the eastern edge of the square stands the Casa Rosada, the Pink House, its walls glowing with a strange tenderness in the morning light — hardly what you'd expect from the seat of executive power. One theory claims the pink comes from a colonial-era mixture of cow's blood and lime whitewash; another says a nineteenth-century president deliberately blended red and white to soften the rivalry between two warring political factions. Whichever story is true, I had already seen that balcony in countless photographs: in 1951, Eva Perón addressed the crowd from up there, her voice already weakened by the cancer that would kill her within a year, refusing to stop until she'd said everything she meant to say. Earlier still, in 1945, Juan Perón himself stood on the same balcony to declare he would not resign. Standing in the middle of the square and looking up, I realized the distance was barely a hundred meters — close enough to feel almost uncomfortable. History, it turns out, usually happened in much smaller spaces than we imagine.

Plaza de Mayo isn't a place. It's the table on which Argentina lays its wounds out to dry in the sun.

The Balcony of Casa Rosada and the Thursday Grandmothers

I later learned that the same square I'd wandered alone at dawn transformed completely by three-thirty in the afternoon. It happened to be a Thursday, so I walked back, and from a distance saw a cluster of women in their seventies and eighties, white headscarves tied over their hair, faded photographs clutched in their hands, circling the Pyramid of May again and again. Only twenty or thirty onlookers stood around — local university students, a few tourists like me. A vendor selling small Argentine flag pins explained that these were the Madres de Plaza de Mayo, the Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo, who had gathered every single Thursday since 1977, during the bloodiest years of the military dictatorship, searching for children who had 'disappeared' during what is now called the Dirty War. Estimates range from ten thousand to thirty thousand people secretly arrested and never seen again; many families still don't know where their children's remains lie.

I followed the circle for a full loop, maybe fifteen minutes. No one shouted slogans. All I heard was the rustle of headscarves, footsteps on stone, and the faint echo of a street accordion drifting over from somewhere else. One grandmother, her scarf washed to a pale grey, glanced at me mid-stride and gave a small smile — not sad, but something closer to quiet, stubborn persistence. Forty-some years on, they had turned waiting itself into ritual, a form of protest carried in the body, arriving punctually every Thursday at half past three. I read later that the group had split into two factions — one still demanding truth and justice, the other choosing forgiveness and reconciliation — but regardless of which side, neither has ever missed a Thursday at Plaza de Mayo.

Walking From the Cathedral to Recoleta: Looking for Evita

The next morning at nine, I decided to retrace Buenos Aires' historical spine on foot — starting at the Metropolitan Cathedral beside Plaza de Mayo, where General San Martín's tomb rests, then along Avenida de Mayo to Congress, north through the pedestrian shopping street Calle Florida, onto Avenida Santa Fe, and finally to the Recoleta Cemetery. The whole route runs about six kilometers. I stopped constantly along the way — even got pulled aside by a street tango dancer on Calle Florida for a photo, tipping him about a thousand pesos — and the walk ended up taking nearly three and a half hours. If that sounds excessive, you could instead take Line D of the subway to Callao station and walk the last fifteen minutes; a single ride on a SUBE card costs less than the equivalent of thirty New Taiwan cents, arguably one of the best subway bargains anywhere in the world.

Recoleta Cemetery charges no entrance fee, but I got lost almost immediately — the place is less a cemetery than a miniature stone city, a labyrinth of family mausoleums built to resemble tiny cathedrals or temples, black wrought-iron gates, stained glass windows, and actual cats sunning themselves between the tombs. I wandered nearly forty minutes before finding the Duarte family vault marked on my map, and with it, Eva Perón's grave. It's surprisingly understated: black marble, no grand statue, just layers of flowers and handwritten notes left by visitors over the years. It was hard to reconcile this quiet corner with the woman whose death triggered ten days of national mourning, whose embalmed body was secretly shipped, hidden, and exiled abroad for nearly two decades before finally being laid to rest here. Standing before that plain headstone, I finally understood why Argentines speak of her with a mixture of reverence and unease — she is both a saint and a controversy that refuses to resolve.

The San Telmo Market and the Weight of a Good Steak

On Sunday at eleven in the morning, I took a taxi to San Telmo, and the moment I stepped out I was hit by a wall of smells — charcoal smoke, grilled onions, and the sharp citrus tang of freshly squeezed grapefruit juice from a corner stand. Down along Calle Defensa, the Sunday-only Feria de San Telmo turns the entire street into a river of vendors and antiques: copper mate gourds, old vinyl records, handmade leather goods, all crammed together while street musicians play tango on the accordion nearby. I bought a choripán from a corner grill — the vendor slit a sausage open flat and pressed it onto a sizzling iron plate, then piled on homemade chimichurri and salsa criolla, a pickled red pepper relish. It cost about 3,500 pesos, roughly three US dollars. The first bite exploded with garlic, fat, and vinegar all at once; I ate it walking through the crowd, grease running down my fingers, not bothering to wipe it off.

That evening at seven-thirty, on a local friend's recommendation, I went to Don Julio, a legendary parrilla at Guatemala 3600 in Palermo. I hadn't booked ahead, so I queued for about forty minutes, during which the staff handed me a complimentary glass of sparkling wine. I ordered the ojo de bife, a ribeye, with a glass of red wine. It arrived still sizzling on a cast-iron plate, the outside charred to a crisp, salty crust, the inside pink and impossibly juicy — it barely needed sauce; the sweetness of Argentine beef spoke for itself. The whole meal, including a bottle of Malbec, ran somewhere between thirty-five and forty-five US dollars depending on the exchange rate — absurdly good value compared to a wagyu dinner back home. The walls were covered floor to ceiling with decades of handwritten notes and photos left by regulars; the owner told me some families had been coming for three generations. In that moment I understood: what they sell here isn't just meat, it's an inherited daily ritual.

Tango isn't a dance step. It's two lonely people briefly agreeing not to be lonely anymore.

Mate, Tango, and a Farewell Deep in the Night

On my last afternoon, I sat on a bench in Parque Lezama — said, by legend, to be the very spot where Buenos Aires was first founded — writing notes. Beside me, a man in his sixties was drinking mate. Noticing my curiosity about the silver straw sticking out of his gourd, he simply handed it over and gestured for me to try. He showed me how to pour hot water from a thermos over the yerba mate leaves packed into the gourd, then passed it to me to drink through the same bombilla straw — a form of sharing so ordinary here that refusing would have seemed rude. The tea was bitter with a grassy edge, something like strong green tea with a hint of earth. When I finished, he refilled it and passed it to the next person on the bench, without a word exchanged, everyone somehow knowing whose turn it was. He laughed and told me mate isn't really a drink — it's an excuse, an excuse for two strangers to sit quietly on the same bench for ten minutes.

At eleven that night, I climbed down a narrow, dim staircase into an unassuming underground milonga in San Telmo. A small band played low, mournful chords on the bandoneón in the corner; the dancers on the floor were mostly locals, ranging from their twenties to their seventies, no dramatic stage lighting, just a few dim bulbs and a wooden floor polished smooth by decades of heels. Tango, as the story goes, was born in the late nineteenth century among the immigrant dockworkers and brothels near the port — lonely people, far from home, dancing out their grief in cramped, noisy taverns. It struck me that every piece of history I'd walked through that week circled back to the same idea: loss and persistence, always coexisting. When I climbed back up out of the milonga, it was nearly one in the morning, and people were still drinking and talking on the street corners. Buenos Aires at night never really seems to intend on ending.

On the walk back to my hotel, I passed Plaza de Mayo one more time. Under the streetlights, the pyramid stood unusually still, as if the weight of the afternoon's grandmothers, the weight of all that history, had sunk back down into the stone. I stood there a few minutes, wondering how a single city could hold, all at once, a revolution for independence, ten days of national mourning, a military dictatorship, and a mate gourd casually passed between strangers on a park bench. Maybe that's the answer: Buenos Aires never intended to seal its history away in a museum. It lets history keep walking the streets, brushing shoulders with you, every single day.
