中文

飛機降落開普敦國際機場那天,桌山頂上正好蓋著一層薄薄的白雲,像有人在山頭鋪了一條桌布。當地人叫這片雲「桌布」(the tablecloth),我後來才知道,只要桌布一鋪上,纜車就會停開,而這座城市對此毫無怨言——它早就習慣了跟著自然的節奏過日子。這趟旅行我沒有排景點清單,只給自己一個規則:每天只做一件事,把剩下的時間拿來走路、吃飯、跟人說話。以下記錄的,是其中最完整的一天。

清晨五點半:卡爾克灣漁港的甦醒

鬧鐘在五點十分響起時,民宿窗外還是一片墨藍色。我住在卡爾克灣(Kalk Bay)一間叫Chartfield Guesthouse的老房子裡,從房間走到漁港只要七分鐘,沿著鐵軌旁的Main Road一路往海邊走,路上除了幾隻在垃圾桶邊翻找的巖狸(dassie,狐獴的遠親),幾乎沒有別的人。

五點四十分抵達漁港時,拖網漁船已經陸續靠岸,柴油引擎的味道混著海帶腐爛的鹹腥味撲面而來。漁民們用南非荷蘭語夾雜科薩語互相吆喝,把一箱箱還在抽動的黃尾魚甩上碼頭。一位穿著及膝雨鞋的老漁夫看我拿著手機拍照,咧嘴笑說:「早啊,妹妹,想買魚就趁現在,晚一點就被餐廳訂光了。」我用二十蘭特跟他買了兩條剛卸貨的鯖魚,他順手用舊報紙包起來遞給我——這魚後來成了我當晚在民宿廚房自己煎的晚餐。天光在六點十五分左右從桌灣那頭慢慢染成橘紅色,海鷗成群結隊地在漁船桅杆上盤旋尖叫,那種喧鬧不是觀光地的熱鬧,而是一座漁港每天都在重複的、屬於工作的聲音。

早餐時刻:Olympia Café & Deli 的家常滋味

七點半,我走進漁港正對面的Olympia Café & Deli。這家開了將近三十年的老咖啡館,木頭地板被踩得吱嘎作響,牆上掛著老闆一家人跟漁民的合照。我在靠窗的位置坐下,點了招牌的碳烤鯖魚早餐(約R95),還有一杯偏酸的手沖濃縮咖啡(R35)。魚肉端上來的時候還帶著炭烤的焦香邊緣,皮脆肉嫩,擠一點檸檬汁下去,鹹香跟油脂的味道在嘴裡化開,配的酸種麵包外殼硬得需要用力咬,內裡卻濕潤帶著微酸的發酵味,一定要沾盤子裡剩下的橄欖油跟魚汁才對味。

隔壁桌坐著幾個看起來像是熟客的老先生,點的是一模一樣的黑咖啡跟一份烤吐司,店員甚至不用問就直接端上桌——那種被記住的熟悉感,讓我這個外來的觀光客也感染了幾分屬於這條街的安穩。我後來又追加了一份自製薑餅(R18),口感偏乾偏硬,但薑味嗆得剛好,配咖啡吃完全不會膩。整頓早餐加起來大約R150左右,吃了將近一小時,店裡的人潮換了兩輪,我卻捨不得走,因為窗外正好可以看到漁船一艘艘出海,跟火車一班班從開普敦市區開進來,兩種節奏在同一扇窗裡交錯。

慢旅行不是走得慢,而是願意把時間留給一頓吃了一小時的早餐。

上午:布奧卡普彩色巷弄裡的信仰日常

搭上九點半的Southern Line火車回到市區(車程約四十五分鐘,票價三等艙約R11,建議選白天人多時段搭乘,不建議晚間獨自搭乘),我直接走進布奧卡普(Bo-Kaap)。這裡的房子漆成粉紅、薄荷綠、鵝黃、天藍,顏色鮮豔到有點不真實,但當地人告訴我,這些顏色其實跟觀光宣傳照裡那種「歡樂」無關。

我在Chiappini街口遇到一位坐在自家門廊乘涼的阿姨,她叫法蒂瑪(Fatima),在這條街住了四十多年。她說她小時候家家戶戶的房子都是白色的,是奴隸制度廢除後(1834年),這裡的開普馬來人(Cape Malay)社群才開始把房子漆上顏色,一來是慶祝終於能自己選擇房子的樣貌,二來是方便喝醉酒或路不熟的鄰居在夜裡找到自己家。她說話的時候,附近的Auwal清真寺——南非最古老的清真寺,建於1794年——正好傳出中午的喚拜聲(azaan),悠長的誦唸聲順著窄巷迴盪,家家戶戶的門窗都靜靜地開著,沒有人覺得吵。法蒂瑪順手遞給我一塊剛炸好的koeksister,是一種擰成麻花狀、炸過後浸在冰糖漿裡的甜點,外皮脆而內裡濕黏帶著椰絲香,吃起來甜到牙齒發酸卻讓人一口接一口。她說每年一月二日,整個布奧卡普都會擠滿人,因為那天是「第二個新年」(Tweede Nuwe Jaar),開普吟遊詩人嘉年華(Kaapse Klopse)會有數千名穿著亮片西裝、撐著花傘的遊行隊伍穿街而過——這個節日源自十九世紀,是當年被奴役的人們唯一被允許放假上街慶祝的一天,如今變成整座城市最盛大的自我慶祝。她說得雲淡風輕,但我聽完站在巷子裡愣了好一會兒,才明白這些鮮豔的顏色背後藏著這麼具體的歷史重量。

正午到午後:徒步獅頭山——尋常日子裡的儀式

下午一點,我從Kloof Nek路上的登山口出發,準備走獅頭山(Lion's Head)。這條路線全長來回約五公里,平緩的螺旋土徑先繞山三分之二圈,大概走四十分鐘後,會遇到一段需要手腳並用的鎖鏈跟鐵梯路段——這段路大約十五分鐘,岩壁陡峭但鑿有踩點,雨天或穿拖鞋絕對不建議嘗試,我那天特地換上登山鞋,還是被曬得滿身汗。停車問題也值得記一筆:Kloof Nek路邊有免費停車格,但週末跟滿月夜晚(當地人習慣滿月夜爬獅頭山看月出,會有上百人頭燈齊發的壯觀場面)車位一位難求,建議提早抵達。

爬到接近山頂的平台時,我遇見一位每週固定來練跑的當地男子塔博(Thabo),他穿著磨損嚴重的越野跑鞋,一邊喘氣一邊笑著跟我說,他每個工作日下班後都會來爬一趟,「這是我的健身房,而且不用月費」。我們一起坐在山頂的平坦岩石上,他指給我看遠方的羅本島(Robben Island)輪廓,還有腳下坎普斯灣(Camps Bay)那一整排白色沙灘。整趟行程我在下午三點半抵達山頂,山頂沒有廁所也沒有販賣部,水一定要自己帶足至少一公升,手機收訊斷斷續續。下山時因為腳踝有點軟,我花了將近五十分鐘才走完下坡路,途中經過一群悠哉曬太陽的巖狸,牠們對登山客毫不畏懼,近到可以聽見牠們吃草的細碎聲音。整趟活動除了停車費(約R20/小時)之外完全免費,卻是我整趟開普敦旅程裡流最多汗、也最有成就感的三小時。

桌山有纜車直達山頂,但獅頭山沒有捷徑,你得用自己的雙腳,一步步換來那片視野。

黃昏:坎普斯灣的日光收尾與星期五的儀式

傍晚五點多下山後,我搭Uber(車資約R65)到坎普斯灣(Camps Bay)的Victoria Road海堤。這條街整排都是棕櫚樹跟露天酒吧,五點半左右開始湧入剛下班的開普敦人——穿著西裝外套的上班族直接脫了皮鞋光腳踩沙,幾個大學生模樣的年輕人抱著一手啤酒坐在防波堤上。這是當地人口中的「日落酒」(sundowner)文化,週五傍晚幾乎是全民運動。我在The Bungalow點了一杯當地釀的白蘭地加可樂(R85),坐在露台看太陽一點一點沉進大西洋,海面被染成一種介於橘紅跟紫的顏色,溫度也跟著降下來,海風帶著涼意鑽進袖口。旁邊一桌本地人聊得起勁,聽得出他們是每週五雷打不動來這裡集合的老朋友,對他們而言,這不是觀光行程,而是一週工作結束後固定的儀式。

夜晚:古古雷圖的炭火與最後一杯Amarula

晚上八點,我特地叫車前往古古雷圖(Gugulethu)的Mzoli's Place,車程約二十五分鐘,車資大約R150。這是一間開在黑人聚居區裡、只有本地人才熟門熟路的燒烤餐廳:先在肉舖秤重買生肉(我點了羊排跟雞翅,兩人份大約R220),接著把肉交給後方的燒烤師傅,他們就在露天的炭爐前直接現烤,油脂滴在炭火上發出滋滋聲響,煙燻味瀰漫整個院子。等餐的四十分鐘裡,院子裡的音響放著kwaito跟amapiano,幾桌客人已經跟著節奏搖擺起來,有人直接在水泥地上跳起舞。烤好的肉端上桌時只簡單配著白麵包跟一點辣椒莎莎,肉質緊實帶著炭香,咬下去先是脆脆的焦邊,接著是滲出油脂的肉汁,完全不需要額外調味。

臨走前,老闆娘塞給我一小杯Amarula——用南非馬魯拉果釀的奶酒,喝起來像加了果香的奶酒版百利甜——說是「今晚的句點」。我坐在回市區的車上,窗外路燈一盞盞往後退,嘴裡還留著奶酒的甜香跟炭烤的煙燻味,忽然覺得這一天從漁港的鹹腥味開始、到布奧卡普的喚拜聲、獅頭山的汗水、坎普斯灣的海風,最後在這杯Amarula裡收尾,像極了開普敦這座城市本身——它從來不是靠幾個知名地標拼湊出來的,而是無數個像這樣具體、瑣碎、帶著溫度的日常時刻疊加而成。

回台灣前一晚,我又走回卡爾克灣漁港,五點半的天光跟第一天一模一樣,漁船照樣進港,老漁夫照樣在碼頭邊喊價。我沒有再多做任何「行程」,只是站在原地看了很久很久——這大概就是慢旅行教會我的事:比起走過多少地方,更值得記住的,是你在同一個地方,願意停留多久。

ENGLISH

The plane touched down at Cape Town International on a morning when Table Mountain wore a thin blanket of cloud across its flat summit. Locals call it 'the tablecloth,' and I later learned that whenever it settles in, the cable car simply stops running for the day — and nobody complains. This city has long made peace with moving at nature's pace rather than its own. I hadn't come with a list of sights to check off. I gave myself one rule instead: one thing a day, and the rest of the time spent walking, eating, and talking to whoever would talk to me. What follows is the fullest of those days.

5:30 AM: Kalk Bay Harbour Wakes Up

My alarm went off at ten past five, and the window of my guesthouse was still deep indigo. I was staying at an old house called Chartfield Guesthouse in Kalk Bay, seven minutes on foot from the harbour along Main Road, which runs parallel to the railway tracks. Aside from a few dassies rummaging near the rubbish bins — small rock hyraxes that look nothing like their supposed cousin, the elephant — the street was empty.

By 5:40 the trawlers were already pulling in, and the smell of diesel mixed with rotting kelp hit me before I even reached the dock. Fishermen shouted back and forth in a mix of Afrikaans and Xhosa, hauling crates of still-twitching yellowtail onto the concrete. An old fisherman in knee-high rubber boots caught me filming on my phone and grinned: 'Morning, sisi — buy your fish now, the restaurants will have it all by nine.' I bought two freshly landed mackerel off him for twenty rand, and he wrapped them in an old newspaper without being asked. Those same fish became my dinner that night, pan-fried in the guesthouse kitchen. By quarter past six, the light over False Bay had turned a slow orange, and gulls wheeled screaming above the ship masts — not the performative noise of a tourist attraction, but the ordinary racket of a harbour doing the same job it does every single morning.

Breakfast at Olympia Café & Deli

At 7:30 I walked into Olympia Café & Deli, directly across from the harbour. This place has been running for close to thirty years, its wooden floor creaking underfoot, the walls hung with photos of the owning family alongside local fishermen. I took a window seat and ordered the grilled mackerel breakfast (around R95) with a sharp, acidic pour-over espresso (R35). The fish arrived with a charred crust and tender flesh underneath; a squeeze of lemon released the fat and salt into something that coated the whole mouth. The sourdough on the side had a crust hard enough to require real effort to bite through, but the inside was damp and faintly sour with fermentation — best mopped through the leftover olive oil and fish juices on the plate.

At the next table, a few regulars — clearly old men who came here often — ordered black coffee and toast without even being asked; the staff already knew. That kind of quiet familiarity, the sense of being remembered, rubbed off on me even as an outsider. I added a homemade ginger biscuit (R18) — dry, a little hard, but with just the right sharp ginger bite to cut through the coffee without ever feeling cloying. The whole meal came to about R150 and took nearly an hour. Two full turnovers of customers passed through while I sat there, unwilling to leave, because the window framed both the fishing boats heading out to sea and the trains rolling in from the city — two entirely different rhythms crossing paths in the same pane of glass.

Slow travel isn't about moving slowly. It's about being willing to give an hour to a single breakfast.

Late Morning in Bo-Kaap: Prayer Calls and Painted Houses

I caught the 9:30 Southern Line train back into the city (about 45 minutes, roughly R11 for third class — best taken in daylight with a full carriage, not recommended alone at night) and walked straight into Bo-Kaap. The houses here are painted pink, mint green, egg-yolk yellow, sky blue — almost too vivid to look real. But a local resident told me these colours have nothing to do with the cheerful postcard image most visitors assume.

On Chiappini Street I met a woman named Fatima, sitting on her porch in the shade, who had lived on that street for over forty years. She told me that when she was a child, every house on the block was white. It was only after the abolition of slavery in 1834 that the Cape Malay community here began painting their homes in colour — partly to celebrate finally being able to choose how their own homes looked, and partly so that a neighbour walking home drunk or unfamiliar with the street could still find their front door in the dark. As she spoke, the midday call to prayer, the azaan, rose from the nearby Auwal Mosque — the oldest mosque in South Africa, built in 1794 — its long, rolling notes echoing down the narrow lane while every window and door around us stayed quietly open, nobody minding the sound at all. Fatima pressed a warm koeksister into my hand, a plaited dough twisted and fried then soaked in syrup, its crust crisp and its inside sticky and coconut-scented, so sweet it made my teeth ache while I kept reaching for another bite. She told me that every second of January, Bo-Kaap fills to bursting for what's called Tweede Nuwe Jaar, the 'second new year,' when thousands march through in sequined suits under decorated umbrellas as part of the Kaapse Klopse, the Cape Minstrel Carnival — a tradition dating back to the 19th century, born from the single day enslaved people were once permitted to take off and celebrate in the streets. Today it has become the city's largest act of collective self-celebration. She said all of this lightly, almost in passing, but I stood in that alley for a long moment afterward, only then understanding the specific historical weight carried inside those bright walls.

Midday to Afternoon: Climbing Lion's Head, an Ordinary Ritual

At one in the afternoon I set off from the trailhead on Kloof Nek Road to climb Lion's Head. The full loop runs about five kilometres round trip. A gentle spiral path circles roughly two-thirds of the peak, taking about forty minutes, before giving way to a fifteen-minute stretch of chains and metal ladders bolted into the rock — steep but fitted with footholds, absolutely not advisable in rain or flip-flops. I'd changed into proper hiking shoes for exactly this reason and still ended up drenched in sweat. Parking is worth noting too: there are free bays along Kloof Nek Road, but on weekends and full-moon nights — when locals traditionally climb to watch the moonrise, hundreds of headlamps bobbing up the mountainside at once — spots disappear fast, so early arrival matters.

Near the summit plateau I fell in beside Thabo, a local man who runs this trail every week, his trail shoes worn thin at the toes. Between breaths he laughed and told me he comes up after work on weekdays: 'This is my gym, and there's no membership fee.' We sat together on a flat outcrop at the top — I reached the summit around 3:30 — and he pointed out the silhouette of Robben Island in the distance, and below us the white curve of Camps Bay's beaches. There's no toilet or shop at the summit, so at least a litre of water is essential, and phone signal comes and goes. My ankle went soft on the descent, stretching what should have been a quick walk down into nearly fifty minutes, past a colony of dassies sunning themselves on the rocks, close enough that I could hear them chewing grass. Aside from the parking fee (about R20 an hour), the whole outing cost nothing — and it was easily the sweatiest, most satisfying three hours of my entire trip.

The cable car takes you straight to the top of Table Mountain. Lion's Head offers no such shortcut — you earn that view one step at a time.

Golden Hour in Camps Bay: The Friday Sundowner Ritual

After coming down around five, I took an Uber (about R65) to the Victoria Road promenade in Camps Bay. The street is lined with palm trees and open-air bars, and by half past five it was already filling with Capetonians just off work — men in suit jackets kicking off their shoes to walk barefoot in the sand, a knot of university-age kids sitting on the sea wall with a six-pack between them. This is what locals call 'sundowners,' and on a Friday evening it's practically a citywide ritual. I ordered a local brandy and Coke (R85) at The Bungalow and sat on the terrace watching the sun sink slowly into the Atlantic, the water turning a colour somewhere between orange and violet as the temperature dropped and a cool wind slid up my sleeves. At the table next to mine, a group of locals talked easily among themselves — clearly old friends who gathered here every single Friday without fail. For them, this wasn't sightseeing. It was simply how the working week ended.

Night in Gugulethu: Braai Smoke and a Last Glass of Amarula

At eight that evening I called a car out to Mzoli's Place in Gugulethu, about twenty-five minutes away for roughly R150. This is a braai spot tucked inside a Black township, the kind of place mostly known to locals: you first buy raw meat by weight at the butcher counter — I chose lamb chops and chicken wings, about R220 for two — then hand it over to the grill men working an open charcoal fire in the yard, fat dripping and hissing on the coals, smoke settling thick over everyone sitting around. During the forty-minute wait, speakers in the yard played kwaito and amapiano, and by the time our food arrived several tables were already swaying to the beat, someone dancing outright on the bare concrete. The meat came simply, with white bread and a bit of chili salsa — tight-grained and smoky, the outside charred and crisp, the inside releasing juice with every bite, needing nothing else added.

Before I left, the woman running the till pressed a small glass of Amarula into my hand — a cream liqueur made from the marula fruit, something like a fruitier Baileys — calling it 'tonight's full stop.' I sat in the car back into the city with streetlights sliding past the window, the sweet cream and charcoal smoke still on my tongue, and realised the whole day had traced a single arc: from the salt air of the harbour, through the call to prayer in Bo-Kaap, the sweat of Lion's Head, the sea wind of Camps Bay, ending here in a small glass of liqueur. That, more than any single landmark, is what Cape Town actually is — not a handful of famous sights stitched together, but an accumulation of small, specific, warm ordinary moments like these.

On my last evening before flying home, I walked back to Kalk Bay harbour one more time. The light at 5:30 looked exactly as it had on my first morning — boats coming in, the old fisherman still calling out prices from the dock. I didn't plan anything else. I just stood there for a long time watching. That, I think, is what slow travel actually teaches you: it isn't about how many places you cover, but how long you're willing to stay still in just one.
