中文

三月底訂機票的那個深夜,我原本只是想找一週歐洲便宜航班,螢幕右下角卻跳出一行小字提醒:「六月十三日為里斯本市假日」。我盯著那行字愣了幾秒,查了才知道,那天是聖安東尼奧日(Santo António),整個里斯本會為了他辦一整個月的節慶。我把出發日期硬是往後挪了兩週,只為了不要錯過這場一年一度的城市告白。

六月的機票與一則訂房網站的提醒

後來才知道,這個決定有多正確。里斯本的六月跟五月、七月完全是兩座城市。五月的阿爾法瑪(Alfama)還安安靜靜,巷弄裡只有觀光客拖著行李箱的滾輪聲;但六月一到,整座山丘像被點了火,家家戶戶把彩色紙串、塑膠沙丁魚旗、聖徒像掛滿窄巷,連晾衣繩上都吊著小燈泡。當地人告訴我,這叫「festas populares」,不是給觀光客看的表演,是社區自己過的節。我後來才明白這句話的重量。

阿爾法瑪的清晨:紙花與沙丁魚的氣味

六月十號,清晨六點半,我走出投宿的阿爾法瑪民宿,石板路還帶著昨夜的濕氣。整條 Rua de São Miguel 靜悄悄的,只有一位老先生踩著木梯,把一串串彩色紙旗掛上兩棟樓之間的鐵絲。空氣裡有股淡淡的炭火味,問了才知道,轉角那家社區協會已經開始醃漬今晚要烤的沙丁魚——鹽粒抹在魚身上,靜置一整天,晚上才下炭火烤。七點多,教堂鐘聲從主教座堂(Sé de Lisboa)那頭傳來,混著遠處某戶人家收音機播的法朵(fado),那種蒼涼又溫柔的嗓音,在還沒被遊客填滿的巷子裡格外清楚。我站在 Miradouro de Santa Luzia 瞭望台邊,藍白磁磚牆映著初升的陽光,底下特茹河(Tejo)泛著一層薄金,那一刻我才真正感覺到,自己是提早幾天闖進了這座城市的私密時刻。

在阿爾法瑪,節慶不是舞台上的表演,而是每一戶人家把自己的日常拆下來,重新掛成一整條會發光的巷子。

親身路線:從主教座堂走到聖喬治城堡,再繞進太陽門瞭望台

那天上午我決定用雙腳把阿爾法瑪的高低差走透。起點是主教座堂正門,九點整,教堂前的小廣場已經有賣紀念磁磚的攤販在架棚子。我沿著 Rua Augusto Rosa 往上爬,坡度陡得讓人喘氣,路面是濕滑的葡式碎石(calçada portuguesa),下雨過後特別容易打滑,我特地換了防滑的健走鞋才敢放心走。大約走十五分鐘,會經過一間不起眼的小教堂 Igreja de Santo António,傳說就蓋在聖徒出生的舊址上,門口總有本地老太太在賣手工小羅勒盆栽,一盆大約一到兩歐元。再往上十分鐘,抵達聖喬治城堡(Castelo de São Jorge)售票口,現場票十點半人潮已經排到快二十分鐘,單人票是十五歐元,我後來才知道官網訂票能省去排隊,早知道就前一晚先訂。城堡本身不算精緻,但站上城牆望出去,整片阿爾法瑪紅屋頂鋪到河邊,孔雀在草坪上開屏尖叫,那種粗獷感反而讓我印象更深。中午十二點,我特意繞遠路下山,走 Rua do Chão da Feira 接到 Portas do Sol 瞭望台,這裡視野比聖露西亞更開闊,能同時看到河景與遠方 25 de Abril 大橋,整趟路線我走了大約兩個半小時,加上排隊與拍照,體力消耗不小,建議像我一樣穿好走的鞋、帶足水,盛夏中午紫外線非常烈。

巷口的沙丁魚與紅酒:在 Zé dos Cornos 的一頓晚餐

傍晚六點半,城裡已經飄著焦香,我循著味道走進一家開業超過四十年的老字號小酒館 Zé dos Cornos,店面小到只有六張桌子,牆上貼滿泛黃的舊照片。老闆娘用葡文夾雜手勢招呼我坐下,菜單只有手寫一張紙,我點了招牌的 sardinhas assadas(炭烤沙丁魚)六尾套餐,配烤紅椒與水煮馬鈴薯,另外加點一份 salada de pimentos(甜椒沙拉)跟一杯冰鎮的 vinho verde 綠酒。魚一端上桌還滋滋作響,魚皮烤得微焦帶苦香,一咬下去油脂混著海鹽在舌尖化開,魚肉細嫩帶著淡淡的鐵鏽味,是那種只有現撈現烤才有的鮮甜。配著微氣泡、帶點青蘋果酸度的綠酒,整套吃下來大約十四歐元,鄰桌的葡萄牙一家三口熟練地徒手撕開麵包沾油,笑著跟我說「六月不吃沙丁魚,等於沒過節」。那頓飯我吃了快一個半小時,店裡愈晚愈擠,最後連門口都站滿了人,油煙混著隔壁鄰居陽台飄下來的煙火味,構成一種很里斯本的、混亂卻踏實的晚餐氣氛。

羅勒與情詩:聖安東尼奧的婚姻傳說

吃完晚餐往回走的路上,我在巷口遇見一位坐在小板凳上賣羅勒盆栽的阿嬤,她的攤子只有一塊木板跟幾十盆迷你羅勒(manjerico),每盆插著一支紙做的康乃馨跟一張手寫小紙條。我好奇問她那紙條寫什麼,她笑著用簡單的英文加比手畫腳解釋:那是「quadras」,一種押韻的四行情詩,傳統上是男生在聖安東尼奧節這幾天,買一盆羅勒送給心儀的女生,如果對方收下,就代表默許交往。她說聖安東尼奧被葡萄牙人稱為「婚姻之聖」,里斯本市政府甚至會在這天資助好幾對新人辦集體婚禮。她接著壓低聲音說,老一輩相信羅勒的香氣能驅走壞運氣,家家戶戶這幾天都會在門口放一盆,連不信教的年輕人也會照做,「這是習慣,跟信仰無關,是身分」。我買了一盆帶回住處陽台,那股清新帶點胡椒味的香氣,後來整整一週都陪著我。

一盆兩歐元的羅勒,裝著一整座城市對愛情最直白也最古老的告白方式。

遊行之夜:大道上燃燒的社區榮耀

六月十二日晚上八點,我提前兩小時到自由大道(Avenida da Liberdade)佔位置,馬路兩側已經站滿人,連路樹上都有小孩被扛在肩膀上。這是一年一度的「Marchas Populares」大遊行,里斯本各個歷史街區——阿爾法瑪、莫拉利亞(Mouraria)、比卡(Bica)——各自派出上百人的隊伍,穿著整條街區居民親手縫製、主題各異的華麗服裝,伴著現場銅管樂隊與打擊樂,邊走邊跳、邊唱各自改編的主題曲。晚上九點半,阿爾法瑪隊伍出場時,我旁邊一位阿伯激動地站起來大喊街區名字,他告訴我他女兒就在隊伍裡跳了三年,每年二月社區就開始排練,布料跟頭飾全靠鄰居募款跟手工製作。遊行持續到將近凌晨,煙火在城堡方向不時炸開,整條大道瀰漫著烤肉煙、香水與汗水混合的氣味,我站在人群裡,忽然覺得這才是真正的里斯本——不是明信片上安靜優雅的黃色電車,而是這種吵鬧、驕傲、全城動員的熱鬧。

回旅館的路上已經是凌晨一點多,阿爾法瑪的巷子還亮著燈,幾戶人家門口的烤爐仍飄著餘溫,遠處隱約傳來收尾的法朵歌聲。我在心裡盤算,如果只能選一個月造訪里斯本,一定是六月,不是為了躲開人潮,而是為了闖進人潮——闖進這座城市一年裡唯一一次,毫無保留地把自己攤開來過節的模樣。回台灣前一晚,我又去買了一盆羅勒,插著那張看不懂的葡文情詩,帶上飛機,一路捨不得丟。

ENGLISH

It was late one March night, half-heartedly hunting for a cheap flight to Europe, when a small line under the calendar caught my eye: 'June 13th – Lisbon municipal holiday.' I clicked through and learned it was the feast day of Santo António, Saint Anthony, and that the whole city spends most of June celebrating him. I pushed my travel dates back two weeks without a second thought. I didn't want to miss it.

A Booking Site's Footnote Changed My Whole Trip

That small decision turned out to matter more than I expected. Lisbon in June is a different city from Lisbon in May or July. In May, Alfama is quiet, its narrow lanes filled mostly with the rattle of tourist suitcase wheels. But the moment June arrives, the whole hillside seems to catch fire. Every household strings up paper garlands, plastic sardine flags, and little saints' figurines across the alleys, even hanging fairy lights from laundry lines. A local shopkeeper told me these were the 'festas populares' — not a show staged for visitors, but a celebration the neighborhoods throw for themselves. I didn't fully understand what she meant until a few days later.

Alfama at Dawn: Paper Flowers and the Smell of Sardines

At 6:30am on June 10th, I stepped out of my guesthouse in Alfama into streets still damp from the night before. Rua de São Miguel was silent except for an old man on a wooden ladder, stringing colored paper flags between two buildings. The air already carried a faint smoky sweetness — a neighborhood association around the corner had started salting sardines for that night's grill, letting them cure all day before the coals were lit. Around 7am, bells rang out from the Sé Cathedral, mixing with fado drifting from someone's kitchen radio, that mournful yet tender voice cutting cleanly through streets not yet crowded with tourists. I stood at the Miradouro de Santa Luzia, its blue-and-white azulejo walls catching the first sun, the Tejo River glowing faintly gold below. That was the moment I realized I had wandered, a little early, into something private that belonged to this city long before I arrived.

In Alfama, the festival isn't a performance on a stage — it's every household taking apart its ordinary life and re-hanging it as a glowing string of lights across the alley.

Walking It Myself: From the Cathedral to the Castle, Then Down to Portas do Sol

That morning I decided to walk Alfama's steep terrain on foot, start to finish. I began at the Sé Cathedral's main door at 9am sharp, where vendors were already setting up stalls of souvenir tiles. I climbed Rua Augusto Rosa, my calves burning against the slope, the wet Portuguese cobblestones (calçada portuguesa) slick enough that I was grateful I'd swapped my sandals for grippy walking shoes the night before. About fifteen minutes up, I passed the small Igreja de Santo António, said to sit on the very site of the saint's birthplace, where elderly women sell handmade basil pots for one or two euros each at the door. Ten more minutes brought me to the ticket booth of Castelo de São Jorge. By 10:30am the line had already stretched twenty minutes long, and the walk-up ticket cost fifteen euros — I later learned that booking online the night before would have saved me the wait. The castle itself isn't especially refined, but standing on its ramparts, looking out over Alfama's red rooftops rolling down to the river while peacocks screeched across the lawn, gave the whole visit a rough, memorable edge. At noon, I deliberately took the long way down via Rua do Chão da Feira to Portas do Sol, whose view is even wider than Santa Luzia's, taking in both the river and the distant 25 de Abril Bridge. The whole loop took about two and a half hours including queuing and photo stops — decent shoes, sun protection, and water are non-negotiable, since the midday sun in June is fierce.

Sardines and Green Wine: Dinner at Zé dos Cornos

By 6:30pm the whole city smelled faintly of charcoal. Following my nose, I ducked into Zé dos Cornos, a tiny tasca that's been open for more than forty years, with only six tables and walls plastered in yellowing photographs. The owner waved me to a seat with a mix of Portuguese and hand gestures; the menu was a single handwritten sheet. I ordered the house special — sardinhas assadas, six grilled sardines — with roasted red peppers, boiled potatoes, a side of pepper salad, and a chilled glass of vinho verde. The fish arrived still sizzling, skin charred just enough to turn faintly bitter, and the first bite released oil and sea salt straight onto the tongue, the flesh tender with that faint iron tang only truly fresh sardines have. Paired with the crisp, slightly fizzy green wine, the whole meal came to about fourteen euros. At the next table, a Portuguese family tore bread with their bare hands to mop up the oil, laughing as they told me, 'If you don't eat sardines in June, you haven't celebrated at all.' I lingered for over an hour; by the time I left, people were standing even outside the door, the smoke from the kitchen mixing with fireworks drifting down from a neighbor's balcony — chaotic, greasy, and utterly Lisbon.

Basil and Love Poems: The Marriage Legend of Santo António

Walking home after dinner, I passed an elderly woman sitting on a small stool selling manjerico, tiny potted basil plants, from a wooden board holding a few dozen pots, each stuck with a paper carnation and a small handwritten note. Curious, I asked what the note said. Laughing, she explained in broken English and hand gestures that it was a 'quadra,' a rhyming four-line love poem — traditionally, a young man buys a basil pot for a girl he fancies during the feast days, and if she accepts it, it means she's open to courtship. Santo António, she said, is known as the 'marriage saint,' and the Lisbon city council even sponsors group weddings on this day every year. Lowering her voice, she added that older generations believe basil's scent wards off bad luck, which is why every household sets a pot by the door during the festival — even young people who aren't religious still do it. 'It's not about belief,' she said, 'it's about who we are.' I bought a pot to take back to my rented room; its sharp, faintly peppery scent stayed with me the rest of the trip.

A two-euro pot of basil carries an entire city's oldest, plainest way of declaring love.

Parade Night: A City Burning with Neighborhood Pride

At 8pm on June 12th, I staked out a spot on Avenida da Liberdade two hours early, and the street was already packed, children perched on shoulders even between the trees. This was the annual Marchas Populares parade, where each of Lisbon's historic neighborhoods — Alfama, Mouraria, Bica — fields a troupe of a hundred or more, dressed in elaborate costumes sewn by the residents themselves, marching and dancing to brass bands and drumlines playing their neighborhood's own anthem. When the Alfama group came through around 9:30pm, an older man beside me leapt up shouting his neighborhood's name; his daughter, he told me, had danced in the troupe for three years, with rehearsals starting every February and every costume funded and hand-sewn by neighbors. The parade ran past midnight, fireworks occasionally cracking open over the castle, the whole avenue thick with the smell of grilled meat, perfume, and sweat. Standing in that crowd, I understood this was the real Lisbon — not the postcard image of quiet yellow trams, but this loud, proud, city-wide mobilization of joy.

It was past 1am by the time I walked back through Alfama, lanterns still lit, grills outside a few homes still warm, faint fado drifting from somewhere unseen. I decided then that if I could only visit Lisbon once, it would have to be June — not to avoid the crowds, but to walk straight into them, into the one time all year this city lays itself completely bare in celebration. The night before my flight home, I bought one more basil pot, its paper love poem still unreadable to me, and carried it all the way onto the plane, unwilling to let it go.
