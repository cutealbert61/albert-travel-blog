中文

飛機降落在溫哥華國際機場的那個下午,窗外還飄著細雨,但雲層縫隙裡透出的光線讓遠方的北岸山脈泛著一層藍灰色的輪廓。我拖著行李走出機場,聞到的第一口空氣是濕潤的針葉林味道,混著海水的鹹,那一刻我就知道,這座城市的靈魂不在市中心的玻璃帷幕大樓裡,而在那片山與海之間的縫隙。接下來的六天,我把相機、防水外套和一雙走爛的健走鞋都用上了,從史丹利公園的海堤到卡皮拉諾河的鮭魚群,再到深灣的夕陽獨木舟,我想寫的不是一份景點清單,而是一場真正被雨、被階梯、被海浪磨過的旅程。

清晨的海堤:史丹利公園甦醒時刻

第一天清晨五點五十分,天還沒全亮,我從Coal Harbour附近的旅館走出來,租了一台Mobi共享單車,直奔史丹利公園的海堤(Seawall)。這條環繞公園外圍的路徑全長約十公里,我打算只騎前半段,從Coal Harbour入口一路騎到獅門橋下方的Prospect Point。六點的海堤幾乎沒有觀光客,只有幾個穿著螢光背心的晨跑者和遛狗的老太太。海面上飄著薄霧,遠處貨輪的汽笛聲低沉地傳過來,海獅偶爾在防波堤附近冒出頭,發出粗嘎的叫聲。騎到Second Beach附近時,太陽終於從東邊雲層裡擠出一道金光,把整片English Bay染成琥珀色,我把單車靠在欄杆上,站了足足十分鐘,什麼都沒做,就是看著光線一點一點爬上西溫哥華的山頭。

溫哥華的美不是安靜的,它是海浪拍打防波堤、單車鏈條喀啦作響、遠方鷹叫混在一起的那種活著的美。

騎到Prospect Point時大約七點十五分,那裡有個小咖啡攤剛開門,我買了一杯美式咖啡暖手,順便啃著自己帶的燕麥棒,看著獅門橋上零星幾輛車呼嘯而過。整趟海堤晨騎花了我大約一個半小時,單車費用是每三十分鐘台幣約六十元,若騎超過半小時要注意重新解鎖計費,不然費用會累加得很快。我後來才知道,很多在地人會選在雨天清晨騎這條路,因為雨天遊客最少,海面反而最有戲劇性——這也解釋了為什麼溫哥華人幾乎沒人帶雨傘,他們穿的都是排水好走的Gore-Tex外套,對他們來說,雨不是阻礙,是這座城市的日常呼吸。

攀上雲霧:Grouse Grind的兩千八百階試煉

第二天上午八點半,我搭乘北岸的Blue Bus抵達Grouse Mountain纜車站山腳下的Grouse Grind登山口。這條被在地人稱為「大自然的階梯機」的健行步道,全長只有2.9公里,但垂直爬升高達853公尺,官方階梯數超過兩千八百階,是那種還沒走完就會讓小腿抽筋的路線。步道口立著醒目的黑熊出沒警示牌,提醒登山客不要單獨行動、不要攜帶會發出食物味道的零食,我把身上的堅果袋收進防水背包最底層,才安心出發。

前半段步道是碎石與木階混合,兩側是茂密的道格拉斯冷杉,空氣裡滿是松脂與濕土混合的氣味,我的呼吸聲很快就變得比腳步聲更大。走到大約三分之一處,遇到一位穿著慢跑鞋、看起來像是每天訓練的當地大叔,他一邊超過我一邊笑著說「加油,你已經比昨天的你更接近山頂了」,這句話後來一直在我腦中重複播放,支撐我走完剩下的一千八百階。整趟攻頂花了我將近兩小時十五分,途中在半山腰的休息平台停了三次,補水、拍照、喘氣。抵達山頂時是上午十一點左右,雲霧剛好散開一半,可以俯瞰整個Burrard Inlet與遠處的溫哥華市區天際線。山頂設有灰熊保護區,兩隻名叫Grinder與Coola的灰熊在這裡被安置照護,牠們慢悠悠地在草地上翻找食物,完全不理會隔著護欄興奮拍照的遊客。下山我選擇搭乘Skyride纜車,單程票加上山頂設施的套票約台幣八百元,對於膝蓋已經在抗議的我來說,這筆錢花得非常值得。

格蘭維爾島市場與漁人碼頭的鹹鮮午後

下午兩點多,我搭巴士轉渡輪抵達格蘭維爾島公共市場(Granville Island Public Market)。市場裡人聲鼎沸,魚販的叫賣聲、烘焙坊剛出爐麵包的焦香、還有現榨果汁機的嗡嗡聲混在一起。我先在Oyama Sausage攤位買了一份煙燻野牛肉腸夾在酸種麵包裡,老闆是位滿臉皺紋卻笑容爽朗的中年男人,他告訴我這款肉腸的煙燻木屑是用本地的蘋果木,咬下去先是脆皮的焦香,接著是帶點甜味的肉汁,配上旁邊攤位買的手工酸黃瓜,酸度剛好化解油膩,一份大約台幣兩百元。

吃完點心後,我沿著海邊步道走到不遠處的Fisherman's Wharf,那裡有間常被在地人推薦的小木屋海鮮店Go Fish。店家只收現金,排隊隊伍在下午三點依然有十幾人,我點了招牌炸比目魚薯條(Halibut Fish and Chips),價格約台幣六百五十元。魚塊炸得金黃酥脆,咬開後魚肉雪白細緻,幾乎沒有魚腥味,配上微酸的手打酸黃瓜塔塔醬與一旁堆得像小山的薯條,坐在碼頭邊的野餐桌上吃,海鷗在頭頂盤旋伺機而動,我一邊護著薯條一邊笑出聲,那種手忙腳亂又滿足的畫面,大概就是溫哥華海邊生活最真實的縮影。

鮭魚迴游的季節:卡皮拉諾河畔與原住民的低語

我特意把這趟旅程安排在九月底,正好趕上太平洋鮭魚迴游的尾聲。傍晚時分,我沿著卡皮拉諾河步道(Capilano River Regional Park)走到河魚孵育場(Capilano River Hatchery)附近的觀魚台,水流湍急冰冷,河水呈現一種近乎透明的灰藍色,偶爾能看到成群的秋鮭(Chum Salmon)逆流而上,魚身已經因為長途遷徙而泛紅褪色,牠們用盡最後的力氣一躍越過淺灘,失敗了再試,那種近乎悲壯的堅持讓我站在觀魚台上看了快四十分鐘,忘記按下相機快門。

就在那裡,我遇到一位穿著印有圖騰刺繡外套的Squamish原住民導覽員,他叫Wes,正在帶一小群遊客做免費的文化解說。他告訴我,鮭魚對Squamish族與Tsleil-Waututh族來說不只是食物,更是整個生態與祖先記憶的連結——魚群迴游餵養了老鷹、黑熊與河岸的森林,森林又反哺河流,形成一個完整的循環,他們稱這種關係為「萬物互相欠著彼此的禮物」。他指著河岸邊一棵倒下的老雪松說,族人傳統上不會砍伐這類巨木,而是等它自然倒下後才取用木材製作獨木舟,因為那代表這棵樹已經把生命交還給了森林。聽完這段話,我再看那些拚死游向上游的鮭魚,眼神都不一樣了。

鮭魚逆流而上的每一次躍起,都是這片森林與海洋之間,一場沉默了千年的對話。

深灣夕陽獨木舟:海面上的溫哥華尾聲

旅程的最後一個傍晚,我開車前往北溫哥華的深灣(Deep Cove),在Deep Cove Kayak租了一艘單人獨木舟,租金是兩小時約台幣一千六百元,店員特別提醒退潮時段水流方向會改變,建議我先往灣口方向划,回程順著水流會輕鬆許多。傍晚六點半下水時,海面平靜如鏡,只有我划槳劃破水面的聲音和遠處偶爾傳來的渡輪引擎聲。我朝著Grebe Islets方向划了大約四十分鐘,兩側是陡峭的花崗岩山壁,松樹幾乎垂直長在岩縫裡,夕陽把整片海灣染成粉橘色,水面上還漂著幾片提早落下的楓葉。

划到小島附近時,我熄了念頭般地停下划槳,讓獨木舟隨波輕輕晃動,遠處傳來一隻孤獨的潛鳥叫聲,空氣裡有海藻與松脂混合的味道,鹹鹹涼涼地貼在皮膚上。回程時天色已經全黑,只能靠岸邊零星的燈光辨認方向,划回岸邊時我的手臂又痠又抖,但那種疲憊裡帶著一種說不出的踏實感。

後來我在深灣一間叫Honey Doughnuts & Goodies的老字號小店買了一份還溫熱的手工甜甜圈當晚餐的甜點犒賞自己,外皮微脆內裡鬆軟,肉桂糖粉撒得毫不手軟,一份大約台幣八十元。坐在店外的長椅上,看著灣裡最後幾艘獨木舟的剪影慢慢消失在夜色裡,我才真正明白,溫哥華的自然探險從來不是為了征服哪座山、划完哪片海,而是讓自己在階梯、獎聲與魚群之間,重新學會呼吸的節奏。這趟旅程結束時,我的鞋底沾滿泥土,外套永遠留著一股松脂味,但我知道,我會想念這種被大自然狠狠擁抱過的疲憊感。

ENGLISH

The plane touched down at Vancouver International on a drizzling afternoon, and through the gaps in the clouds I could already see the North Shore mountains wearing a soft blue-grey haze. Stepping outside with my luggage, the first breath of air smelled of damp cedar and salt water, and in that instant I understood this city's soul doesn't live in its glass towers downtown, but in the narrow seam where mountain meets sea. Over the next six days I wore out my raincoat, my hiking shoes, and honestly my patience for stairs, cycling the Stanley Park seawall at dawn, gasping up the Grouse Grind, watching salmon fight their way upriver, and paddling into a burning sunset at Deep Cove. This isn't a checklist of attractions. It's a journal of a trip that got soaked, sore, and quietly humbled by nature.

Dawn on the Seawall: Stanley Park Wakes Up

At 5:50am on my first morning, the sky still hadn't fully lightened when I stepped out of my hotel near Coal Harbour, grabbed a Mobi bike-share, and headed straight for the Stanley Park Seawall. The full loop runs roughly ten kilometers, but I only planned to ride the first half, from the Coal Harbour entrance to Prospect Point beneath the Lions Gate Bridge. At six in the morning the seawall was nearly empty — just a few joggers in reflective vests and a woman walking her dog. Fog hung low over the water, a distant cargo ship's horn rumbled through the mist, and every so often a sea lion surfaced near the breakwater with a hoarse bark. By the time I reached Second Beach, the sun finally pushed a golden seam through the clouds, turning English Bay amber. I leaned my bike against the railing and just stood there for a full ten minutes, watching the light crawl up the West Vancouver hillside.

Vancouver's beauty isn't quiet. It's the slap of waves on the breakwater, the click of a bike chain, and a distant eagle's cry, all tangled together into something alive.

I reached Prospect Point around 7:15am, where a small coffee cart had just opened. I bought an Americano to warm my hands and ate an oat bar I'd packed, watching a handful of cars cross Lions Gate Bridge overhead. The whole ride took about ninety minutes, and the bike-share cost roughly two dollars Canadian per thirty minutes — worth noting that you need to re-dock and unlock again after thirty minutes or the fees stack up fast. I later learned that locals often choose rainy mornings for exactly this ride, since tourists disappear and the sea gets dramatic instead. It also explains why almost no Vancouverite carries an umbrella — everyone wears breathable Gore-Tex shells instead. Rain isn't an obstacle here; it's the city's daily breathing rhythm.

Into the Clouds: The 2,830 Steps of the Grouse Grind

By 8:30am the next day I'd taken the North Shore's Blue Bus to the base of the Grouse Mountain gondola, where the Grouse Grind trailhead begins. Locals nickname this trail 'Mother Nature's Stairmaster,' and for good reason: only 2.9 kilometers long, but with 853 meters of vertical gain and over 2,830 stone-and-timber steps, it's the kind of trail that makes your calves cramp before you're even halfway done. A bright warning sign at the trailhead cautioned hikers about black bear sightings, urging people not to hike alone or carry strong-smelling snacks. I buried my trail-mix bag at the very bottom of my waterproof pack before setting off, just to be safe.

The lower section alternates between gravel and wooden steps, flanked by dense Douglas fir, and the air smelled thickly of resin and wet earth. Within minutes my breathing was louder than my footsteps. About a third of the way up, a local man in worn running shoes — clearly a daily regular — jogged past me and grinned, 'Keep going, you're already closer to the top than yesterday's version of you.' That line looped in my head for the rest of the climb, carrying me through the remaining 1,800-odd steps. The full ascent took about two hours and fifteen minutes, with three rest stops for water, photos, and gasping for air. I reached the summit around 11am just as the fog cleared halfway, revealing Burrard Inlet and the downtown skyline in the distance. At the top sits a grizzly bear refuge, home to two rescued bears named Grinder and Coola, who wandered the grass completely indifferent to the excited tourists pressed against the fence. For the descent I took the Skyride gondola — a combo ticket with summit access ran about thirty Canadian dollars — money my protesting knees were more than happy to spend.

Salty Afternoons: Granville Island Market and the Fisherman's Wharf

By early afternoon I'd taken a bus and a short ferry hop to Granville Island Public Market. Inside, the noise was a wall of sound: fishmongers calling out prices, the toasted smell of fresh bread from a bakery stall, and the hum of a juice press somewhere nearby. I stopped first at Oyama Sausage, where I ordered a smoked bison sausage tucked into sourdough. The vendor, a weathered but warmly smiling man, told me the smoking wood was local applewood. The first bite was crisp and smoky, followed by slightly sweet, savory juices, balanced perfectly by a handful of house-pickled cucumbers from the next stall. The whole snack ran about eight Canadian dollars.

Afterward I followed the waterfront path to nearby Fisherman's Wharf, home to a small, cash-only shack that locals swear by: Go Fish. Even at 3pm the line was a dozen deep. I ordered the halibut fish and chips, about twenty-four Canadian dollars. The batter came out golden and impossibly crisp, giving way to snow-white, delicate fish with almost no fishy taste, paired with a tangy house tartar sauce and a mountain of fries. I ate at a picnic table right on the dock while seagulls circled overhead, plotting their theft attempts. Guarding my fries with one hand while laughing at my own paranoia, I realized this chaotic, joyful mess was probably the most honest snapshot of Vancouver's seaside life I'd find all trip.

The Season of Returning Salmon: Whispers Along the Capilano River

I'd deliberately timed this trip for late September, right at the tail end of the Pacific salmon run. In the early evening I followed the trail through Capilano River Regional Park to the viewing platform near the Capilano River Hatchery. The current ran fast and glacier-cold, an almost translucent grey-blue, and every so often a group of chum salmon surged upstream, their bodies already reddened and worn from the long migration. They leapt against the shallows with everything they had left, failed, and tried again. That desperate persistence kept me rooted at the railing for nearly forty minutes, my camera forgotten in my hand.

It was there I met Wes, a Squamish Nation guide in an embroidered jacket, leading a small free cultural walk. He told me that for the Squamish and Tsleil-Waututh peoples, salmon aren't just food but the thread connecting the entire ecosystem to ancestral memory — the returning fish feed eagles, bears, and the surrounding forest, and the forest in turn nourishes the river. They call this cycle 'a gift everything owes to everything else.' He pointed to an old fallen cedar on the riverbank and explained that traditionally, his people never cut down such giant trees while alive; canoes were carved only from trees that had already fallen naturally, since that meant the tree had finished returning its life to the forest. After hearing that, I looked at those salmon throwing themselves upstream with completely different eyes.

Every leap a salmon makes against the current is a conversation between forest and sea that has been going on, silently, for a thousand years.

Sunset Kayaking at Deep Cove: Vancouver's Closing Note

On my last evening, I drove out to Deep Cove in North Vancouver and rented a single kayak from Deep Cove Kayak, about forty Canadian dollars for two hours. The staff warned me that the tide would shift direction partway through, suggesting I paddle out toward the mouth of the inlet first so the current would carry me home more easily. I launched around 6:30pm into water so still it looked like glass, the only sounds my paddle cutting the surface and, occasionally, a distant ferry engine. I paddled toward the Grebe Islets for about forty minutes, granite cliffs rising steeply on either side, pine trees clinging almost vertically to the rock, while the sunset dyed the whole cove a deep peach-orange. A few early fallen maple leaves drifted on the surface nearby.

Near the islets I let the paddle rest across my lap and simply drifted, the kayak rocking gently. A lone loon called somewhere in the distance, and the air carried the mixed scent of seaweed and pine resin, cool and salty against my skin. By the time I turned back, the sky had gone fully dark, and I navigated by scattered shoreline lights alone. My arms ached and trembled by the time I reached the dock, but the exhaustion carried a strange, deep satisfaction.

Afterward, I bought a still-warm handmade doughnut from a local institution called Honey Doughnuts & Goodies as a reward — crisp outside, soft inside, generously dusted with cinnamon sugar, for about three Canadian dollars. Sitting on the bench outside, watching the last silhouettes of kayaks dissolve into the night, I finally understood: Vancouver's nature isn't about conquering a mountain or finishing a paddle route. It's about relearning how to breathe, somewhere between stairs, paddle strokes, and the sound of fish fighting upstream. By the time this trip ended, my boots were caked in mud and my jacket still smelled faintly of pine resin — and I already knew I'd miss being held so roughly, and so completely, by nature.
