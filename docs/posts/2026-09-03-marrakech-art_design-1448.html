中文

飛機在午後五點降落馬拉喀什,機艙門一開,乾燥滾燙的風混著塵土與孜然氣味撲面而來。計程車司機跟我用半法語半阿拉伯語討價還價,最後談定七十迪拉姆,把我載進舊城麥地那的紅色城牆裡。牆是黏土與石灰夯築而成,在夕陽下呈現介於磚紅與陶土之間的顏色,當地人叫它「馬拉喀什紅」。那一刻我就知道,這趟旅程真正的主角不是駱駝或沙漠,而是這座城市怎麼用顏色、線條與紋理說話。接下來五天,我把自己交給建築、庭園、工匠手藝與廣場上的即興演出,試著讀懂馬拉喀什的設計語言。

黎明的幾何啟示——本尤瑟夫古蘭經學院

清晨六點半,我從卡斯巴區的一間小型里亞德(riad)民宿走出來,老闆前一晚特別叮嚀:「八點前去本尤瑟夫學院(Ben Youssef Madrasa),不然你只會看到別人的後腦勺。」麥地那此刻還沒完全醒來,只有麵包師傅推著鐵皮推車經過,烤爐飄出的酵母香混著清晨潮濕的石灰味,貓在巷口的地毯堆上打盹。走了約二十分鐘,穿過幾條窄到只容一人通過的巷弄,才在一道毫不起眼的木門前找到入口,門票七十迪拉姆,約合台幣兩百三十元。

推門進去的瞬間,我幾乎倒抽一口氣——中庭一方水池映著天光,四面牆壁從地面到天花板全是幾何雕花:下段是彩色瓷磚拼貼(zellige),中段是灰泥雕刻(gebs),上段則是雪松木雕格柵。這些圖案沒有一絲重複的偶然,全部依循伊斯蘭藝術裡「無限延伸」的數學邏輯,象徵真主的創造沒有起點也沒有終點。我在二樓一間僅三坪大的學生宿舍裡站了很久,想像六百年前的學生就是在這樣密不透風卻精雕細琢的空間裡讀書,才明白這裡的「美」從來不是裝飾,而是信仰的具體化。

馬拉喀什的美不是拿來看的裝飾,而是用來走進去、住進去、跪下去禱告的信仰載體。

怎麼玩:步行加計程車,實走馬約爾花園與聖羅蘭美術館

離開古蘭經學院已近九點,巷子裡開始湧入旅行團,我決定放棄步行、改搭計程車前往城市西北邊的馬約爾花園(Jardin Majorelle)。在麥地那外圍攔車不難,但司機不跳表,我學乖了先講好價錢——四十迪拉姆,車程約十五分鐘,穿過新城區寬闊的Avenue Yacoub El Mansour,兩旁棕櫚樹取代了老城的窄巷,風景瞬間從中世紀跳到裝置藝術。

抵達時是九點半,售票口前已排了將近百人,現場排隊買票要等四十分鐘,我後悔沒有前一晚在官網先訂票。花園門票一百五十迪拉姆,隔壁的柏柏爾博物館與伊夫聖羅蘭美術館合併套票另加一百迪拉姆,總共約合台幣八百元出頭,說實話不便宜,但我後來覺得值得。園內那種介於鈷藍與電光藍之間的「馬約爾藍」漆牆,是畫家雅克・馬約爾在一九三〇年代自創的顏色,搭配亮黃色花盆與濃綠仙人掌林,強烈到讓人幾乎忘記自己身在沙漠邊緣的城市。我在裡頭待了快一小時,光是找角度拍那道藍牆配竹林剪影就花掉半卷底片般的手機容量。

隔壁的聖羅蘭美術館是二〇一七年由巴黎建築事務所Studio KO操刀,外牆用了兩萬七千塊手工燒製的陶磚,以經緯交織的紋理呼應紡織品的織法,呼應設計師畢生對摩洛哥織品與色彩的迷戀。館內展出他從一九六二到二〇〇二年間受馬拉喀什啟發的手稿與服裝,我站在一件以柏柏爾婚禮頭飾為靈感的禮服前,忽然理解為什麼這座城市能同時養出傳統工匠與國際級設計師——他們共用同一套色彩詞彙。整趟行程加上排隊、參觀、拍照,大約花了兩個半小時,建議上午九點前抵達,或改在下午四點後入園,人潮會少很多。

鋅鐵巷弄與拼貼工匠——認識Zellige

中午時分,我沿著麥地那南側走進費爾巴丁廣場(Place des Ferblantiers)附近的鋅鐵匠巷,叮叮噹噹的敲擊聲比任何招牌都更早告訴我這裡在做什麼。一位頭髮花白的師傅哈山(Hassan)看我站在他店門口張望太久,乾脆招手叫我進去,用簡單英語混法語跟我聊了起來。他的家族做拼貼瓷磚(zellige)已經三代,他十二歲就跟著父親當學徒,先學怎麼用鐵鎚與扁鑿(manqash)把方形彩釉磚敲成六角形、星形、菱形的小塊,一敲就是十年才能出師獨立拼圖案。

他拿起一塊剛敲好的八角星形碎片跟我解釋,伊斯蘭幾何圖案裡最核心的八角星象徵宇宙秩序,每一種顏色的釉料配方都是家族祕方,他父親那輩用的鈷藍釉如今已經很少人會調了。我問他會不會擔心這門手藝失傳,他笑著搖頭說現在有不少年輕人因為國外訂單變多又願意回來學,只是工序太慢——拼滿一平方公尺的複雜圖案要花上一週,難怪訂製一間浴室的zellige牆面動輒要價數千歐元。臨走前他泡了杯甜到嗆喉的薄荷茶請我喝,那股滾燙的甜味跟滿地陶土碎屑混在一起,成了我對這條巷子最鮮明的記憶。

每一塊瓷磚都要先被敲碎,才能拼出完整的圖案——這大概是我在馬拉喀什學到最摩洛哥的一件事。

午後的味蕾——Mechoui巷的手撕烤羊

下午兩點,肚子已經抗議多時,我照著在地朋友的建議走向巴布杜卡拉市場(Bab Doukkala)旁一條窄巷,當地人叫它「Mechoui Alley」。還沒轉進巷口就先聞到炭火烤羊油脂滴落的焦香,巷子兩側五、六個攤位各自守著一整隻蒸烤到骨肉分離的羊架,攤主用手直接把肉撕下來秤重賣。我點了半公斤,老闆問我要不要加孜然鹽跟現烤圓餅,一份總共約六十迪拉姆,折合台幣不到二百元。

肉端上來時還冒著熱氣,油脂已經燉到近乎透明黏稠,用手撕開後肉質軟得幾乎不用咬,混著孜然粉的鹹香跟一點點焦炭味在嘴裡化開,我一口氣配了兩個薄餅才停手。攤位旁擺著幾張矮塑膠凳,沒有桌巾也沒有菜單,隔壁桌一位摩洛哥老先生看我狼吞虎嚥,笑著遞給我一片他自己帶來的醃檸檬,說配著吃解膩。那種毫無距離感的分享,加上巷口飄出的孜然與炭煙味,比我後來在城裡任何一間裝潢精緻的餐廳都更接近我心目中「摩洛哥的味道」。

傑馬艾夫納廣場的黃昏與風俗

傍晚五點左右,我特意提早到傑馬艾夫納廣場(Jemaa el-Fnaa),想親眼看它從白天的果汁攤慢慢變身成夜晚的露天劇場。廣場邊我認識了在地導遊尤瑟夫(Youssef),他正等著帶一團德國旅客,見我一個人閒晃便隨口聊了起來。他告訴我每週五中午,摩洛哥家庭幾乎家家戶戶都會準備庫斯庫斯(couscous),這是老祖母傳下的規矩,連他在外地工作的哥哥都會特地請假回家吃這一餐,「不是為了吃飽,是為了讓全家人坐在同一張桌子旁」。

他也提醒我,如果想進公共澡堂(hammam)體驗,女性去女生時段、男性去男生時段是鐵律,而且絕對不能在裡面拍照,那是當地人少數還保有的私密社交空間。天色漸暗時,廣場上的變化像被按下播放鍵一樣加速:畫著繁複紋樣的亨娜彩繪師擺開小凳子,說書人(halqa)開始用阿拉伯語與柏柏爾語混雜著吟唱古老故事,古粵瓦音樂(gnawa)的鐵片響板聲從某個角落傳來,上百盞攤販的裸燈泡幾乎同時亮起,把整個廣場染成一片暖黃。空氣裡混著炭烤羊肉串的煙、現榨柳橙汁的酸甜與滿地灰塵,吵得像一場沒有指揮的交響樂,卻意外和諧。

我在編號十四號的一個小吃攤坐下,點了一碗蝸牛湯(babbouche),攤主用長針幫我一顆顆挑出蝸牛肉,湯頭用茴香、肉桂與十幾種香料熬煮,喝起來帶著奇異的藥膳感,一碗才十迪拉姆。坐在塑膠矮凳上,四周全是操著不同語言討價還價的聲音,我忽然意識到,這座廣場本身就是最徹底的「設計」——沒有建築師規劃過它的動線,卻靠著幾百年的攤販默契,形成一套完美運作的即興城市劇場。聯合國教科文組織把它列為非物質文化遺產,理由正是這種活生生、每晚重演一次的口述與表演傳統。

尾聲:天台上的最後一夜

最後一晚,我爬上一間老里亞德改建的餐廳天台吃晚餐,點了一份鴿肉派(pastilla),外皮是極薄的瓦卡皮,咬下去先是酥脆再是內餡的甜鹹交織——肉桂糖粉灑在鹹香的鴿肉碎與杏仁上,是我這輩子吃過最矛盾卻迷人的一道菜,主餐加一壺薄荷茶約一百八十迪拉姆。從天台望下去,整座麥地那的屋頂連成一片低矮的土色地平線,遠處古蘭經學院的宣禮塔在夜色裡發著暖光,廣場方向的喧鬧聲隱約傳來。我想起哈山師傅敲碎瓷磚的樣子,想起Studio KO那面兩萬七千塊陶磚砌成的美術館外牆,忽然覺得這座城市所有的美,不管是六百年前的雕花還是五年前的當代建築,骨子裡用的都是同一種邏輯:先拆解、再重組,把日常的材料敲碎揉進更大的秩序裡。飛機隔天中午起飛,我在行李箱夾層塞進一小片哈山多送我的星形瓷磚碎片,那大概是我從馬拉喀什帶走最誠實的紀念品。

ENGLISH

My plane touched down in Marrakech at five in the afternoon, and the moment the cabin door opened, a blast of dry, scorching air rolled in, thick with dust and cumin. My taxi driver haggled with me in a mix of French and Arabic until we settled on seventy dirhams, and he drove me straight into the red ramparts of the old medina. Those walls are rammed earth mixed with lime, and in the low sun they glow somewhere between brick and terracotta — locals simply call it 'Marrakech red.' I knew right then that the real subject of this trip wouldn't be camels or dunes, but how this city speaks through color, line, and texture. Over the next five days I let myself be pulled through madrasas, gardens, artisan workshops, and the nightly theater of the main square, trying to read the design language underneath it all.

Dawn's Geometry Lesson at Ben Youssef Madrasa

At six-thirty in the morning I stepped out of a small riad in the Kasbah district. The owner had warned me the night before: 'Get to Ben Youssef Madrasa before eight, or you'll only see the backs of other people's heads.' The medina hadn't fully woken yet — only a baker pushing a tin cart past me, the smell of yeast and warm dough mixing with the cool plaster smell of early morning, cats dozing on stacked carpets at the corner. After twenty minutes weaving through alleys barely wide enough for one person, I found an unremarkable wooden door. Entry cost seventy dirhams, roughly seven US dollars.

The second I stepped inside, I nearly gasped. A reflecting pool in the courtyard mirrored the sky, and every wall from floor to ceiling was covered in geometric carving — zellige tilework at the base, carved plaster (gebs) above it, cedar lattice screens on top. None of the patterns repeat by accident; they follow the Islamic principle of infinite extension, symbolizing a creation with no beginning and no end. I stood a long time in a tiny second-floor student cell, maybe three square meters, imagining a scholar six hundred years ago studying in a space this airless yet this meticulously carved. I understood then that beauty here was never decoration for its own sake — it was faith made physical.

Beauty in Marrakech was never meant to be admired from a distance — it's built to be walked into, lived in, knelt down and prayed inside.

How I Actually Did It: Walking, Taxis, and the Real Route to Jardin Majorelle and the YSL Museum

By nine o'clock, tour groups had started flooding the alleys around the madrasa, so I gave up on walking and flagged a taxi toward Jardin Majorelle in the city's northwest. Outside the medina, taxis are easy to find but don't run on meters — I'd learned by then to agree on a price first: forty dirhams for a fifteen-minute ride along the wide Avenue Yacoub El Mansour, palm trees replacing the medina's narrow alleys, the whole scene jumping centuries in a single street.

I arrived at nine-thirty to find nearly a hundred people already in line, a forty-minute wait for tickets. I regretted not booking online the night before. Garden entry alone runs a hundred and fifty dirhams; the combined ticket adding the Berber Museum and the Yves Saint Laurent Museum next door costs another hundred — about thirty US dollars total, not cheap, but worth it in the end. The cobalt-electric 'Majorelle blue,' a color the painter Jacques Majorelle invented himself in the 1930s, paired with bright yellow planters and dense stands of cactus, is intense enough to make you forget you're on the edge of a desert city. I spent nearly an hour just hunting for angles to photograph that blue wall against a bamboo silhouette.

Next door, the YSL Museum, completed in 2017 by the Paris firm Studio KO, wraps its facade in twenty-seven thousand hand-fired terracotta bricks laid in a woven pattern that echoes textile weaving — a nod to the designer's lifelong obsession with Moroccan fabric and color. Inside, sketches and garments from 1962 to 2002 trace how Marrakech shaped his work. Standing before a gown inspired by a Berber wedding headdress, I finally understood why this city can nurture both traditional artisans and world-class designers at once — they draw from the exact same visual vocabulary. The whole visit, queue included, took about two and a half hours. Go before nine in the morning, or after four in the afternoon, when the crowds thin considerably.

The Alley of Hammered Tin and Tile — Meeting a Zellige Craftsman

Around noon I walked south through the medina toward the tinsmiths' alley near Place des Ferblantiers, where the clang of hammers announced the trade long before any sign did. A gray-haired craftsman named Hassan, noticing me lingering at his shopfront, waved me inside and chatted in a patchwork of English and French. His family has made zellige tile for three generations. He started as an apprentice at twelve, learning to chisel square glazed tiles into hexagons, stars, and diamonds with a hammer and a flat chisel called a manqash — a full decade of practice before he could design a pattern on his own.

He picked up a freshly cut eight-pointed star fragment to explain that this shape represents cosmic order in Islamic geometry, and that every glaze color is a family recipe — the deep cobalt his father used, he told me, almost nobody still knows how to mix. When I asked if he worried the craft would disappear, he shook his head, smiling; younger people are coming back to learn now that international commissions have picked up, though the work is punishingly slow — a single square meter of intricate pattern can take a week, which explains why a custom zellige bathroom wall can run into the thousands of euros. Before I left he poured me a glass of mint tea so sweet it caught in my throat, and that scalding sweetness mixed with clay dust on the floor is the sharpest memory I carry from that alley.

Every tile has to be shattered first before it can become part of something whole — that might be the most Moroccan lesson I learned all week.

Lunch in Mechoui Alley: Slow-Roasted Lamb by Hand

By two in the afternoon my stomach had been complaining for a while, so I followed a local friend's tip toward an alley beside Bab Doukkala market that everyone simply calls Mechoui Alley. I smelled it before I saw it — charcoal smoke and dripping fat from whole lambs steamed and roasted until the meat falls off the bone. Five or six stalls each guard their own carcass, pulling meat apart by hand and selling it by weight. I ordered half a kilo; the vendor asked if I wanted cumin salt and a fresh round bread on the side. The total came to about sixty dirhams, under seven US dollars.

The meat arrived still steaming, the fat rendered down to something almost translucent and sticky. It pulled apart so tender it barely needed chewing, the cumin's earthy salt mingling with a faint char in every bite — I went through two flatbreads before I could stop myself. There were no tablecloths, no menus, just a few low plastic stools. An elderly Moroccan man at the next stool, watching me eat with obvious enthusiasm, laughed and handed me a slice of his own preserved lemon, telling me it cuts the richness. That easy, unguarded generosity, mixed with the cumin smoke drifting down the alley, felt closer to the taste of Morocco than anything I ate in the city's polished restaurants afterward.

Dusk and Custom in Jemaa el-Fnaa

I arrived deliberately early at Jemaa el-Fnaa around five in the afternoon, wanting to watch it transform from a daytime juice-stand plaza into a nightly open-air theater. There I met Youssef, a local guide waiting for a group of German tourists, who chatted with me while he waited. He told me that every Friday at noon, almost every Moroccan household prepares couscous — a rule handed down from grandmothers — and that even his brother, working in another city, takes the day off just to sit at the same table. 'It's not really about the food,' he said. 'It's about everyone sitting down together.'

He also warned me that hammam etiquette is strict: women go during women's hours, men during men's hours, and photography inside is absolutely forbidden — it remains one of the few truly private social spaces locals still have. As the sky darkened, the square seemed to fast-forward through its own transformation. Henna painters unfolded their stools, storytellers began chanting in a mix of Arabic and Berber, the metallic clatter of gnawa castanets drifted over from somewhere unseen, and hundreds of bare bulbs snapped on almost in unison, washing the whole square in warm amber light. The air filled with grilled meat smoke, the sharp sweetness of fresh orange juice, and dust — a chaos with no conductor that somehow still felt perfectly composed.

I settled at stall number fourteen and ordered a bowl of snail soup, babbouche, watching the vendor pick out each snail with a long needle. The broth, simmered with fennel, cinnamon, and a dozen other spices, tasted almost medicinal, and cost just ten dirhams. Sitting on a low plastic stool, surrounded by haggling voices in a half-dozen languages, I realized this square is itself the most complete design object in the city — no architect ever planned its traffic flow, yet centuries of vendor consensus have produced a perfectly functioning, improvised urban theater. UNESCO listed it as Intangible Cultural Heritage for exactly this reason: a living oral and performance tradition that replays itself, slightly differently, every single night.

Epilogue: One Last Night on a Rooftop

On my final evening I climbed to the rooftop terrace of an old riad turned restaurant and ordered pastilla — pigeon pie wrapped in impossibly thin warqa pastry. The first bite was crisp, then sweet and savory all at once, cinnamon sugar dusted over shredded pigeon and almonds, the most delicious contradiction I think I've ever eaten. The main course with a pot of mint tea came to about eighteen US dollars. From the terrace, the medina's rooftops stretched out into a low ochre horizon, the minaret near the old madrasa glowing faintly in the dark, the square's noise drifting up faintly from somewhere below. I thought of Hassan chiseling tile, of Studio KO's twenty-seven thousand terracotta bricks, and realized that everything beautiful in this city — whether carved six centuries ago or built five years back — runs on the same underlying logic: break something ordinary apart, then reassemble it into a larger order. My flight left the next afternoon, and tucked into my suitcase lining was a small star-shaped tile fragment Hassan had pressed into my hand — probably the most honest souvenir I brought home from Marrakech.
