中文

降落巴黎戴高樂機場那晚,是十一月中的一個週四,班機延誤了四十分鐘,我拖著行李箱走出海關時,大廳的時鐘已經指向晚上十一點半。空氣裡有種說不清楚的味道——地毯的塵味、咖啡機殘留的焦香,還有一種像舊書頁的潮氣。我腦子裡冒出的第一個念頭不是「我到巴黎了」,而是「我要怎麼在半夜安全地進城,而且不要被坑」。

深夜降落:戴高樂機場到市區的第一堂課

機場裡到處都是招攬計程車和「機場快線」的黃牛,一個穿反光背心的男人湊過來說五十歐元直達市區,我笑著搖頭走開。真正該找的是藍色的RER B月台指標,班次不算密集,深夜時段大約每十五到二十分鐘一班,單程票在自動售票機買是十點三歐元(2024年價格,實際會隨年份調整),坐到北站Gare du Nord大約三十五分鐘。車廂裡的椅墊是磨舊的深藍絨布,燈光偏黃,對面坐著一個戴耳機的年輕人全程沒抬頭,這才是我心目中真正的巴黎日常,不是明信片,而是深夜通勤的疲憊臉孔。我落腳的是北站附近運河區的一間青年旅館St Christopher's Inn,床位每晚約三十二歐元,櫃檯的女生遞給我房卡時說了句「Bonne nuit」,語氣平淡卻不冷漠。房間是六人混宿,鐵床架吱嘎作響,但窗外正對聖馬丁運河,隔天一早我拉開窗簾,河面上飄著薄霧,才真正確認自己人在巴黎。

提醒一件事:戴高樂機場往市區絕對不要在深夜接受任何人主動搭訕的「省時方案」,巴黎警方近年在機場加強巡邏,但私接計程車糾紛還是時有所聞。另一個實用建議是提前在手機下載Île-de-France Mobilités的App,可以直接用手機感應過閘門,比排隊買紙本車票省下至少十分鐘,這在拖著行李、時差未消的深夜特別珍貴。

巴黎不會在機場大廳歡迎你,它只在你願意搭上那班深夜火車之後,才慢慢對你打開一條縫。

巴黎的第一個清晨:蒙馬特麵包店與階梯教堂

隔天清晨六點四十分,我還沒完全倒好時差就醒了,索性走出旅館往北走,搭四號線轉二號線到Blanche站,再爬上蒙馬特的坡道。這個時間點的蒙馬特幾乎沒有觀光客,只有拉下鐵捲門的紅磨坊招牌,還有一個老人牽著一隻胖臘腸狗慢慢走過石板路。我的目標是Yves Toudic街上的Du Pain et des Idées麵包店,開店時間是早上七點十五分,我到的時候已經有五、六個當地人排在門口,大多是穿著風衣、拿著保溫杯的上班族,沒有人滑手機,大家安靜地看著櫥窗。

我點了一份招牌的escargot pistache-chocolat,一種捲成蝸牛狀的酥皮,外層是開心果醬混著巧克力碎,價格是四點二歐元。咬下去的瞬間,外皮先是喀啦一聲碎開,接著裡層濕潤的奶油香氣混著開心果的堅果甜味在嘴裡化開,溫度還帶著剛出爐的熱氣,我站在店門口的窄巷裡,一邊呵著白煙一邊吃,旁邊一位老太太用法語跟我說了句什麼,我沒聽懂,只能傻笑點頭,她也笑了,轉身走進晨霧裡。這種語言不通卻彼此微笑的瞬間,比任何導覽都更接近巴黎的日常溫度。

吃完麵包,我沿著坡道爬向聖心堂,教堂前的階梯在清晨還沒被觀光團佔滿,只有幾個街頭藝人在整理畫架。從階梯頂端俯瞰,整個巴黎在薄霧裡泛著灰藍色,鐵塔的輪廓隱約浮現,那一刻我才真正感覺到,自己不是在看明信片,而是站在明信片裡面。

鐵塔攻頂實戰:樓梯派的省錢攻略

第三天下午兩點,我從戰神廣場那頭走近艾菲爾鐵塔,決定不排電梯的長龍,改走南柱腳的樓梯入口。這是背包客最實際的省錢方法:樓梯票只到二樓,成人票在官網預訂大約十點八到十一點八歐元,而電梯票到頂樓要價超過三十歐元。樓梯口的排隊人潮明顯短很多,我等了大約二十分鐘就進場,安檢像機場一樣要過X光機,包包裡的水壺被要求打開喝一口證明不是危險液體。

爬樓梯這件事,官方數字是六百七十四階到二樓,實際爬起來比想像中喘,鐵鑄的樓梯結構在腳下發出規律的鏗鏘聲,扶手是冰涼的鐵條,爬到一半我開始能透過鏤空的鋼架看到巴黎鐵灰色的屋頂連綿成海。大約十五分鐘後抵達二樓平台,風比地面大了許多,吹得人瞇眼,但視野開闊到讓人忘記剛才的喘。如果想上到頂樓,還需要在二樓另外排隊搭電梯,我當天因為雲層低索性作罷,省下時間去了戰神廣場邊的小攤買了一杯熱可可,兩歐元,暖手也暖胃。

務實的提醒是:務必提前在官網訂票並選定時段,現場排隊的隊伍在旺季可能長達兩三小時;樓梯入口通常人少很多,體力夠的背包客真的划算;鐵塔周邊有很多兜售自拍棒和「請簽名做公益」的人,那個簽名本其實是要你捐錢的小圈套,微笑搖頭走開就好,千萬別停下來簽名。

省下的不是二十歐元,而是一種親手爬上去、用喘息換來的視角——那和電梯直達的風景,終究不太一樣。

瑪黑區的排隊哲學:一份法拉費教我的生存法則

第四天中午我特意繞到瑪黑區的Rue des Rosiers,傳說中的L'As du Fallafel招牌是亮眼的黃色,門口永遠排著隊。我到的時間是中午十二點半,隊伍大概二十人,排了將近二十五分鐘。點餐窗口的大叔動作快得像機械手臂,我點了招牌的Falafel Spécial,九點五歐元,裡面塞滿炸鷹嘴豆餅、酸黃瓜、紫甘藍、茄子泥和一種帶著芝麻醬香氣的白醬,外層皮塔餅被醬汁浸得微濕卻不軟爛。咬下第一口,鷹嘴豆餅外酥內鬆,茄子泥的煙燻味和優格醬的酸味在嘴裡打架又和解,我站在巷口的路邊啃著,醬汁滴到手背上也顧不得擦。

瑪黑區本身就是一堂風俗課:這裡是猶太社區與同志文化並存的街區,週五傍晚我經過時,幾間猶太潔食烘焙坊已經拉下鐵門準備安息日,而隔壁的酒吧卻正要開始亮燈迎接夜晚,兩種節奏在同一條街上共存卻毫不衝突。一位在附近開書店的老闆跟我閒聊時說,瑪黑區的居民彼此都懂得「錯開時間過日子」,這句話我後來想起蒙馬特那位老太太的微笑,忽然覺得巴黎人擅長的不是熱情,而是恰到好處的共存分寸。

星期天的寂靜與市集的喧嘩:巴黎人怎麼過周末

第五天是星期天,我起了個大早想去逛市場,卻先撞見了整條大街店鋪全部拉下鐵門的巴黎日常——法國的星期天,除了麵包店、花店和少數餐廳,絕大多數商店真的是全城休息,連我想買瓶水的小超市都關著。後來我搭地鐵到巴士底附近的Marché Bastille,才發現熱鬧全都藏在這種週日限定的露天市集裡。

市場從早上七點開到下午兩點半,賣起司的老闆娘看我猶豫不決,直接切了一小塊Comté起司遞給我試吃,笑著用簡單的英文說「你先嚐,不好吃不用買」。這種直接又帶點驕傲的待客方式,和巴黎市中心餐廳裡略顯冷淡的服務生形成鮮明對比。我後來才懂,巴黎人對待熟悉的市集攤販和陌生的觀光餐廳,態度真的是兩套劇本。攤位間我還撞見一個小小的儀式:每逢週日市集尾聲,賣花的攤販會把賣剩的花束對半降價出清,附近的老太太們早就摸透規律,兩點左右才慢悠悠出現,用五歐元帶走一大束玫瑰,那種精打細算又從容不迫的生活智慧,是任何導覽書都寫不出來的細節。

巴黎人的星期天不是沒有生活,而是把生活收進了市集的攤位和一句「先嚐再說」裡。

離開前的帳本:七天巴黎背包客花費與踩雷筆記

回旅館整理收據時,我把七天的花費攤開來算:住宿六晚青旅約一百九十歐元,交通用了Navigo Easy儲值卡搭配單程票約四十五歐元,景點門票(鐵塔樓梯、羅浮宮、蒙帕納斯瞭望台)加起來大約七十歐元,餐飲每天控制在十五到二十五歐元之間,七天下來大約一百三十歐元,再加上零星的咖啡與紀念品,整趟旅程含機票外的實際花費落在五百五十歐元左右,對背包客來說算是可以接受的區間。

幾個真心想提醒後來者的踩雷筆記:第一,地鐵四號線和一號線是扒手熱區,尤其在夏特雷Châtelet轉車時人潮擁擠,包包一定要背前面;第二,聖心堂與艾菲爾鐵塔附近常有人硬把「幸運手環」綁上你的手腕再跟你要錢,遇到伸手靠近的人直接後退並大聲說「Non merci」,不要停下腳步;第三,香榭麗舍大道上的觀光餐廳普遍貴又難吃,真正划算的餐廳往往藏在瑪黑或第十區這種住宅街區裡;第四,法國餐廳的服務費其實已內含在帳單裡,不需要額外加成小費,留個一兩歐元零錢當作心意就好,不必有壓力。

回程那天清晨,我又走去運河邊看了一次晨霧,水面倒映著早起晨跑的巴黎人身影,忽然覺得這座城市最迷人的不是鐵塔或羅浮宮,而是這種你必須親自走過、親自排過隊、親自被騙過一次才學會的節奏感。背包客的巴黎,從來不是明信片裡的那個,而是你自己一步一步走出來的那條路線。

ENGLISH

I landed at Charles de Gaulle Airport on a Thursday night in mid-November. The flight was forty minutes late, and by the time I dragged my suitcase through customs, the clock in the arrivals hall already read half past eleven. The air carried a strange mix of smells—worn carpet, burnt coffee residue from a vending machine, and something faintly damp like old book pages. My first thought wasn't 'I made it to Paris.' It was 'how do I get into the city safely at this hour without getting ripped off.'

Landing at Midnight: My First Lesson from CDG to the City

The terminal was full of men in reflective vests offering fifty-euro 'express taxis.' I smiled, shook my head, and kept walking toward the blue RER B signs. That's the real move. Trains run roughly every fifteen to twenty minutes even late at night, and a one-way ticket from the machine cost about 10.30 euros, taking around thirty-five minutes to Gare du Nord. The seats were worn navy velvet, the lighting a dim yellow, and across from me a young guy in headphones never once looked up the whole ride. That, to me, was the real Paris—not a postcard, but a tired commuter's face at midnight. I was staying at St Christopher's Inn near the canal by Gare du Nord, about 32 euros a night for a bunk in a six-bed dorm. The receptionist handed me my key card with a flat but not unkind 'Bonne nuit.' The bed frame creaked, but the window looked straight out onto the Canal Saint-Martin, and when I pulled back the curtain the next morning to find mist hovering over the water, that's when it finally hit me: I was actually in Paris.

One real warning: never accept a stranger's 'shortcut' offer at the airport late at night, even though police patrols have increased in recent years, unlicensed taxi disputes still happen. A more useful move is downloading the Île-de-France Mobilités app before you land, so you can tap through the gates with your phone instead of queuing at a ticket machine—a small mercy when you're jet-lagged and hauling luggage.

Paris doesn't welcome you in the arrivals hall. It only cracks the door open once you've boarded that midnight train.

My First Morning: A Bakery in Montmartre and a Staircase Church

The next morning I woke at 6:40, jet lag still unresolved, and walked out of the hostel heading north, taking line 4 then switching to line 2 to Blanche station, then climbing up into Montmartre. At that hour the neighborhood had almost no tourists—just the shuttered red sign of the Moulin Rouge and an old man walking a fat dachshund slowly across the cobblestones. My target was Du Pain et des Idées on Rue Yves Toudic, which opens at 7:15am. When I arrived there were already five or six locals in line, mostly in trench coats holding thermoses, nobody on their phones, everyone quietly staring into the window display.

I ordered the signature escargot pistache-chocolat, a snail-shaped pastry swirled with pistachio cream and chocolate flakes, for 4.20 euros. The crust cracked audibly on the first bite, then the moist buttery layer inside melted with the nutty sweetness of pistachio, still warm from the oven. I stood in the narrow alley outside, breathing little clouds of steam while eating, when an elderly woman beside me said something in French I didn't catch. I just smiled and nodded like an idiot; she smiled back and disappeared into the fog. That kind of wordless, mutual smile is closer to the real texture of Paris than any guided tour could offer.

After the pastry, I climbed toward Sacré-Cœur. The steps in front of the basilica weren't yet swarmed by tour groups—just a few street artists setting up their easels. Looking down from the top, the entire city was bathed in a hazy blue-grey, the silhouette of the Eiffel Tower just barely visible through the mist. That was the moment I truly felt I wasn't looking at a postcard—I was standing inside one.

Climbing the Tower: The Budget Traveler's Stairwell Strategy

On my third afternoon, at 2pm, I approached the Eiffel Tower from Champ de Mars and decided to skip the elevator queue entirely, heading instead for the stairwell entrance at the South Pillar. This is the most practical money-saving move for any backpacker: stair tickets only go up to the second floor, costing roughly 10.80 to 11.80 euros online, while elevator tickets to the summit run over 30 euros. The stair line was noticeably shorter—I waited about twenty minutes before getting through airport-style security, where a guard asked me to sip from my water bottle to prove it wasn't dangerous liquid.

The official count is 674 steps to the second floor, and climbing it felt more breathless than I expected. The cast-iron staircase rang out with a steady clang under my feet, the railings cold to the touch. Halfway up, I could see through the lattice steelwork as Paris's grey rooftops unfurled like a sea beneath me. About fifteen minutes later I reached the second-floor platform, where the wind hit much harder than at ground level, forcing me to squint, but the view was wide enough to make me forget the climb entirely. Going to the summit required another elevator queue from the second floor, which I skipped that day since the clouds were low—instead I bought a hot chocolate from a stall near Champ de Mars for two euros, warming both hands and stomach.

Practical tips: book your slot online in advance—queues in high season can stretch two to three hours. The stair entrance is almost always shorter, and it's genuinely worth it if you have the legs for it. Around the tower, people will try to hand you 'petition' clipboards asking you to sign for a charity—it's a scam designed to guilt you into donating cash. Smile, shake your head, and keep walking; never stop to sign.

What you save isn't twenty euros—it's a view earned by your own breath, and that's a different kind of view than the one the elevator gives you.

The Queueing Philosophy of Le Marais: What a Falafel Taught Me About Survival

On day four at noon I made a point of walking to Rue des Rosiers in Le Marais, where the bright yellow sign of L'As du Fallafel always has a line out the door. I arrived at 12:30 and the queue was about twenty people deep, taking nearly twenty-five minutes. The man at the counter moved like a machine—I ordered the signature Falafel Spécial for 9.50 euros, stuffed with crispy fried chickpea balls, pickles, purple cabbage, eggplant purée, and a tahini-scented white sauce, the pita soaked just enough by the sauce without turning soggy. The falafel balls were crisp outside and fluffy inside, the smoky eggplant purée and tangy yogurt sauce fighting and reconciling in my mouth all at once. I stood eating on the corner of the alley, sauce dripping onto the back of my hand, too hungry to care about wiping it off.

Le Marais itself is a lesson in local custom. This is a neighborhood where the Jewish community and the LGBTQ+ scene coexist on the same streets. Walking through on a Friday evening, I saw kosher bakeries already pulling down their shutters ahead of Shabbat, while the bar next door was just turning on its lights for the night—two completely different rhythms sharing the same street without any friction. A bookshop owner nearby told me, in our small chat, that locals here have simply learned to 'live at staggered hours.' That line stuck with me, and later, thinking back to the old woman's smile in Montmartre, I realized what Parisians are actually good at isn't warmth—it's the precise art of coexisting without stepping on each other's time.

Sunday Silence and Market Noise: How Parisians Spend Their Weekends

Day five was a Sunday, and I got up early hoping to explore a market, only to be met first by an entire street of shuttered storefronts—the true reality of French Sundays. Aside from bakeries, flower shops, and a handful of restaurants, almost everything closes citywide; even the small supermarket where I hoped to buy a bottle of water was locked up. I eventually took the metro to Marché Bastille, and that's where I discovered all the noise and life had simply relocated into this Sunday-only open-air market.

The market runs from 7am to about 2:30pm. A cheese vendor, seeing me hesitate, cut off a small piece of Comté and handed it to me, saying in simple English, 'Try it first, no need to buy if you don't like it.' That directness, tinged with a kind of quiet pride, stood in sharp contrast to the somewhat cool service I'd gotten at restaurants in central Paris. I realized then that Parisians run two completely different scripts—one for the market vendors they know, another for the anonymous tourist restaurants. Between the stalls I stumbled onto a tiny ritual: near closing time, flower vendors slash prices on unsold bouquets by half, and the neighborhood grandmothers clearly know the pattern by heart—they show up around two o'clock, unhurried, and walk off with a huge bunch of roses for five euros. That kind of frugal, unhurried life wisdom is something no guidebook ever writes down.

A Parisian Sunday isn't a lack of life—it's life folded neatly into a market stall and the phrase 'try it first.'

The Ledger Before Leaving: A Week of Backpacker Costs and Real Warnings

Back at the hostel, sorting through my receipts, I tallied up seven days of spending: six nights in a hostel dorm cost about 190 euros; transport using a Navigo Easy card plus single tickets ran roughly 45 euros; attraction tickets (the tower stairs, the Louvre, the Montparnasse observation deck) added up to about 70 euros; food averaged 15 to 25 euros a day, totaling around 130 euros for the week. Add in scattered coffees and souvenirs, and my actual on-the-ground spending, excluding flights, landed around 550 euros—a reasonable range for a backpacker.

A few honest warnings for anyone following in my footsteps. First, metro lines 4 and 1 are pickpocket hotspots, especially at the crowded Châtelet interchange—wear your bag on your front. Second, near Sacré-Cœur and the Eiffel Tower, people will try to physically tie a 'lucky bracelet' onto your wrist and then demand money for it—if someone reaches toward you, step back immediately and say a firm 'Non merci,' don't stop walking. Third, the touristy restaurants along the Champs-Élysées are generally overpriced and mediocre; the genuinely good, affordable spots are tucked away in residential streets like Le Marais or the 10th arrondissement. Fourth, service charges in French restaurants are already included in the bill—you don't need to add extra tip; leaving a euro or two in loose change is a nice gesture, not an obligation.

On my last morning, I walked back to the canal to watch the mist one more time, the water reflecting the shapes of early-morning joggers. It struck me then that what makes this city magnetic isn't the tower or the Louvre—it's this particular rhythm you can only learn by walking it yourself, queuing for it yourself, and getting fooled by it once yourself.
